Why Effort Drifts Toward the Controllable - Four Tests That Separate Preparation From Progress

The gap between a completion signal that lands at once and an outcome signal that lands late, and what that gap builds in a person and in an organization

2026-09-05 Trends & Culture

People are usually busy even during the stretches when nothing moves. The gap between being busy and making progress is not explained by the total volume of effort. If anything, the gap tends to widen in exactly the weeks when the volume goes up.

That gap opens in the same place almost every time. When work whose result arrives late sits beside work whose completion is confirmed immediately, people move toward the second one. The move is usually described as a failure of will, but it is not that simple. The shift happens even when the destination is the harder of the two.

What follows is an attempt to build the structure of that shift. Before pointing at what has gone wrong, it is worth explaining why the choice feels reasonable from inside. Only then does a dividing line become a tool rather than a lecture.

The subject here is how work and goals get allocated. Clinical vocabulary such as burnout or depression has no place in it. If avoidance repeats to the point where daily life stops holding together, that belongs to a different domain and is a matter for a qualified professional. The piece that prompted this is a personal essay, so its claims appear as the author saying something rather than as established fact.

What the essay behind this actually says

On 19 August 2026, Justin Andersun published a piece called Exit the Cave on his newsletter Turtle's Pace. He wrestled for four winters at Kenowa Hills High School in Michigan, and he writes that despite hundreds of hours of training in sweatsuits and severe weight cutting, he was pinned more often than he pinned and lost more often than he won.

What he points at in hindsight is not the training volume. He believed that perfecting the individual inputs, technique and conditioning, would guarantee the outcome, and the mat told him otherwise. His own diagnosis is that he was wrestling to avoid losing rather than to win. He wanted his team not to lose, and underneath that wish, he says, sat a desire to avoid shame.

The cave is his metaphor for the place where only controllable inputs get repeated. He has a line about technology too. Smart devices reward private fitness, recommendation algorithms propagate a person's preexisting philosophy back through personalized programming, and AI tells us what we want to hear and bolsters our assumptions instead of challenging them. The sentence that carries his conclusion runs like this. Better to be pinned in public with courage than to train endlessly in private like a coward.

It is worth being explicit about the scope of that source. It is a personal essay, and it cites no research. The only outside references inside it are a wrestling aphorism and a sportswear advertisement featuring a swimmer. So what the piece offers is one person's observation rather than data, and the question of why that observation resonates so widely has to be built somewhere else. Three things are worth naming as absent from the original.

  • The concept of substitute behaviour - not in the original. The author points at the same phenomenon with the cave metaphor, but he never handles it as a psychological construct
  • Feedback delay - not in the original. What he talks about is the test of reality, not a comparison of when signals arrive
  • Organizations and metrics - not in the original. The essay stays with individual disposition and never extends to how a team or a company measures itself

Substitution is not laziness - it is a difference in arrival time

Explaining substitute behaviour as laziness holds up badly in practice. Substitute work is usually hard. Spending an entire weekend classifying research, rebuilding an environment until dawn, or revising an outline for the twelfth time takes real stamina. Quite often it costs more hours than the thing it displaced. The two activities do not separate along the axis of how much force they demand.

They separate along when the signal arrives. Send a manuscript and the answer comes three weeks later. Ship a product and the reaction takes months to take shape. Quote a price and you have no idea when the other side will reply. Rebuilding a folder structure, by contrast, is confirmed as finished the instant it ends. The signal comes immediately and it comes with certainty. On top of that, the signal is guaranteed to be positive. Tidying ends in a tidied state; it does not get rejected.

A timetable of arriving signals

Line up five items from a single working day purely by when their signal lands, and the order comes out like this.

  • Immediately - filing, tool configuration, unifying document formatting. The moment of finishing is the moment of the signal
  • Within hours - gathering and summarizing material. The accumulating volume itself reads as a progress indicator
  • Within days - sending one paragraph of a draft to one person. The signal is only complete once the other side answers
  • Weeks to months - launches, applications, proposals. The signal does arrive, but late, and attributing it to any one cause is difficult

That ordering matters from the standpoint of learning. Robin M. Hogarth, Tomas Lejarraga and Emre Soyer, writing in Current Directions in Psychological Science in 2015, framed inference as taking place across two settings: the one in which information is acquired, and the one in which it is applied. Where the informational elements of the two settings match closely, they call the environment kind; where they diverge, wicked. A kind environment, the authors write, is a necessary condition for accurate inference.

The popular summary renders a kind environment as a place where feedback is fast and accurate. That rendering is narrower than the paper's own definition. The criterion the authors set is not speed but the match between the learning setting and the application setting. Speed is one of the conditions that makes such a match easier to achieve, and fast feedback can perfectly well be mismatched feedback. Still, the direction survives. Tool maintenance sits nearer the kind side and real goals sit nearer the wicked side. People go where they can learn.

The pull to finish early - what the bucket experiment showed

One experiment shows just how strong a completion signal is as an incentive. David A. Rosenbaum, Lanyun Gong and Cory Adam Potts published it in Psychological Science in 2014, volume 25 issue 7, pages 1487 to 1496. The definition the authors give reads as follows. Pre-crastination is the tendency to complete, or at least begin, tasks as soon as possible, even at the expense of extra physical effort.

The design is simple. Two buckets sit on either side of an alley, one closer to the starting point and one closer to the end point. A participant picks up one of them and carries it to the end of the alley, and the researchers explicitly told participants to choose the easier task. The prediction was the bucket nearer the end point, since choosing that one shortens the carry.

The result went the other way. Participants took the bucket nearer the start and carried it the longer distance. The conditions are worth recording alongside the finding.

  • Sample - 257 university-student participants across nine experiments. It is a walking and reaching task inside a laboratory, which is a different layer from office work or long-horizon goals
  • The authors interpretation - they ascribed the preference to a desire to reduce working memory loads. Holding the fact that something remains to be done felt more expensive than a few extra paces of carrying
  • The limit the authors named - they wrote that they did not explicitly record walking speed, and called that a limitation of their procedure that they freely acknowledge

A challenge to the interpretation came from within the same field. Michael Richter published a commentary in Frontiers in Psychology in 2015 arguing that the observation that individuals prefer effortful tasks is not new and that there are theoretical approaches that can account for the findings. He wrote that the studies did not provide information regarding the underlying motivation or mechanisms, and listed alternatives that had not been ruled out: participants might have chosen the more effortful option to demonstrate high ability, out of learned industriousness, because the option was more attractive, or because of a lower perceived overall demand.

So what this research supports narrows to a single item. Choices that accept an extra cost in order to bring completion forward really are observed. That the reason is working memory is the authors' interpretation, and that interpretation drew a published rebuttal in the same field. Explaining desk-bound substitute behaviour with this one experiment goes beyond the sample and the task it used.

Where the urgent beats the important

There is a second axis that travels alongside completion speed, and that is urgency. Meng Zhu, Yang Yang and Christopher K. Hsee published five experiments together in the Journal of Consumer Research in 2018, volume 45 issue 3, pages 673 to 690. The claim stated in the abstract is that people pursue urgency over importance even once the normatively legitimate reasons for doing so are controlled for.

Concretely, tasks with objectively lower payoffs are chosen more often than tasks with objectively better payoffs when the lower-payoff tasks carry nothing more than spurious urgency. The authors write that such a choice violates the basic normative principle of dominance, which is to say a plainly better option is available and the worse one is taken anyway.

  • The word spurious is doing the work - the task is not actually urgent, it merely carries markers of urgency. A deadline stamp, a notice that something is about to disappear, a line saying today only
  • Its relation to completion speed - the two axes are conceptually distinct but travel together in practice. A small item with a near deadline also finishes soon, and so its signal comes back quickly
  • Scope - these are results from laboratory tasks in a consumer context. Transposing them directly onto annual goals versus weekly chores is an extension, and it is not one the paper underwrites

Overlay the two results and the picture sharpens. Controllable work finishes quickly, usually looks urgent, and delivers a reliably good signal when it ends. When an option satisfying all three conditions is sitting right there, not taking it would be the strange behaviour.

Substituting a judgment and substituting an allocation are different layers

The word substitution already carries another meaning in psychology, and mixing the layers wrecks the explanation, so it is worth separating them once. Daniel Kahneman and Shane Frederick contributed a chapter titled Representativeness Revisited: Attribute Substitution in Intuitive Judgment to Heuristics and Biases, edited by Gilovich, Griffin and Kahneman and published in 2002. Attribute substitution as described there is a mechanism of judgment, in which an inaccessible target attribute is answered with a more accessible heuristic attribute. A question about probability gets answered with similarity.

The substitution at issue here is not judgment but allocation. Answering the question of how likely this company is to succeed with the question of how plausible this company looks, and answering the question of what to do today with the question of what can be finished today, have similar shapes. But the first is a swap of attributes inside a single judgment, while the second is a resource called time flowing toward a different object. No single theory covers both.

  • What goes wrong when they are merged - any behaviour at all becomes labellable with the name of a cognitive bias, at which point the name explains nothing
  • What genuinely carries over - in both cases the question quietly changes into one that is easier to answer. That shared feature works as an analogy, but it does not work as evidence

Effort or progress - four tests

With the structure in place, a dividing tool is next. The four questions below are asked of whatever work is currently in hand. They serve classification rather than moral evaluation, and the point at which the answers split is the boundary.

Test 1 - Will finishing produce something to show another person

Progress ultimately means reaching a state where an external signal can arrive. If something showable exists, that state has been reached; if not, it has not. Sorting folders produces nothing to show. The showable thing need not be a finished artefact. One paragraph, one screen, one email can all receive a signal. The criterion is not scale but whether it touches anything outside.

Test 2 - What is actually blocked if it goes undone

If the answer is that it will be inconvenient later, this is preparation. If the answer is that the next step does not start, this is progress. The question is useful because it exposes ordering. Most substitute behaviour blocks nothing at all if left until later. It gets done now precisely because it can be finished now.

Test 3 - Can the result of this work turn out to be wrong

This is the strongest of the four. Work that stays a job well done regardless of the outcome produces no information. The sentence I polished the landing page has nowhere to record what result would show it was wrong. The sentence changing to this wording will increase inquiries does have such a place. The same hours are spent either way, but only the second one changes next week's judgment.

Test 4 - Who confirms completion, you or somebody outside

Work that ends because I declare it ended and work that ends because somebody else responds are different in kind. If only the first sort accumulates, a day feels full and a month looks empty. This test attaches well to practice because it is easy to count. Beside each item finished last week, write who confirmed it.

Passing all four is not required. Some weeks contain nothing that could possibly qualify as progress. The problem is when such weeks run on for months. Compressed to four lines it looks like this.

  • Does finishing produce something to show
  • Is anything actually blocked by skipping it
  • Can the result turn out to be wrong
  • Who confirms that it is done

A classification table for everyday activities

Laying out the activities that genuinely occupy one day along the axes of the four tests makes the boundary visible. The verdict is not about the worth of an activity but about what it is functioning as right now.

Activity Time to completion signal Who gives the signal Falsifiable Verdict
Choosing a new notes app and rebuilding the folder structure Hours Yourself No Effort
Collecting reading material into a list A day Yourself No Effort
Refining the palette and spacing of presentation slides Hours Yourself No Effort
Rebuilding a development environment that already works A day Yourself No Effort
Standing up a test environment for the first time A day Yourself Yes - the next step fails to run Preparation (sound)

One regularity stands out in the table. Every row classified as effort has its signal supplied by the person doing the work. Those are also the rows where the falsifiability column is empty. The two columns are effectively the same value recorded from different angles. Work I confirm myself leaves nowhere for me to be wrong.

Falsifiability as a criterion, and its limits

Falsifiability was originally proposed as a criterion for deciding what qualifies as a scientific theory. What is used here is not that strict criterion but a much looser borrowing, and the two are not the same thing. Saying so plainly is the better course. Borrowing a concept and importing its original weight along with it turns an argument into decoration.

The borrowing earns its keep anyway, because it forces one sentence to be written in advance. If this result appears, this direction is wrong. Work that deserves the name progress can usually have that sentence written before it starts. When it cannot be written, the work holds the same position no matter what comes back.

  • Cases where it cannot be written - I organized the documents, I tidied the code, I gathered more sources. There is no place to record which observation would make the work wrong
  • Cases where it can - this screen order will reduce drop-off, this price will get one acceptance in three. Both can be wrong, and the way they would be wrong is specified
  • A borderline case - refactoring. Attach the sentence that the next feature will take less time to add and it becomes falsifiable; leave it off and it stays tidying

The limit is equally clear. Demanding falsifiability of every task turns the demand itself into one more controllable ritual. When writing the hypothesis becomes the goal, hypothesis documents multiply and shipping stays where it was. The criterion works in practice when it is kept as a minimum count that has to exist in a week rather than as a sieve applied to everything.

When the same thing happens inside an organization

Just as an individual has completion signals that land immediately, an organization has observable outputs. Meeting counts, document counts, ticket counts, commit counts, deployment counts, training completion rates. These values get promoted to metrics for exactly the reasons an individual drifts, not out of bad intent.

  • Observation is cheap - the numbers already exist inside the systems. There is no separate cost to measure them
  • The signal is fast - how many happened this week can be known today. A contribution to revenue only takes shape once the quarter closes
  • Interpretation is uncontested - there is little to argue about in how tickets are counted. Customer satisfaction gets argued about at the level of definition
  • Attribution is easy - who did how many is unambiguous. Nobody knows whose work lifted retention

Those four conditions are the same list that explains why an individual drifts toward sorting folders. So the whole organization optimizes toward values that settle quickly. Just as a person's day fills up with completion signals, an organization's quarter fills up with observable outputs. What is gained and what quietly disappears when the metric set is cut down to one, and how proxy metrics are separated from outcome metrics, are each handled separately. How the habit of reading activity volume as skill gets built is something the fashion for turning activity stats into cards covered earlier.

What happens at this point has an old name attached to it. But the sentence carrying that name changed along the way.

Three sentences, as they were originally written

The most widely quoted form is when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, and it usually travels under the label of Goodhart's law. Goodhart did not write it.

Original author Source Original sentence What it is about
Charles Goodhart (1975) Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience, Papers in Monetary Economics Vol. 1, Reserve Bank of Australia Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. Monetary policy. It describes an observed statistical regularity collapsing once it is made an instrument of control. Neither the word target nor the phrase good measure appears
Marilyn Strathern (1997) Improving ratings: audit in the British University system, European Review vol. 5 no. 3, pages 305 to 321 When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. University assessment and audit regimes. This is the actual origin of the sentence now quoted as Goodhart's law
Donald T. Campbell (1979) Assessing the impact of planned social change, Evaluation and Program Planning vol. 2 no. 1, pages 67 to 90 The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor. Measuring the effects of social policy. Distortion is written as a matter of degree, structured as more use bringing more pressure

The difference has a real effect in practice. Goodhart's original contains no evaluative phrase such as good measure. What he recorded is the observation that the statistical relationship between an indicator and its subject collapsed once that indicator became a policy instrument. Strathern's sentence, by contrast, reads as a verdict on the quality of a measurement. Overlaying the two produces the conclusion that every metric becomes useless the moment it becomes a target, which is a claim neither of them made.

  • Campbell's form sits closest to practice - distortion is a dial, not a switch. Since the pressure varies with how heavily an indicator is used in decision-making, the option of adjusting its weight remains available instead of abolishing it
  • The working rule that follows - separate the numbers you watch from the numbers you tie things to. Watch ticket counts without hanging anything on them. The moment weight lands on a value, that value starts becoming something else

When preparation is not avoidance

Reading this far and concluding that all preparation is avoidance goes wrong in the opposite direction. Turn preparation into a sin and what follows is starting without it, and that bill is frequently larger than the one substitute behaviour runs up. A dividing line is needed.

Three conditions for sound preparation

  • Reversal costs more than the preparation - printing, contracts, relocation, data migration. Where nothing can be recovered once it goes out, preparation is straightforwardly a rational allocation. The problem is preparing at the same intensity for work that is trivially reversible, not preparation as such
  • The preparation is itself falsifiable - standing up a test environment for the first time can produce the result that it stands and the next step still fails to run. Then the preparation produces information too. That is why that one row in the earlier table carries a different verdict
  • No external signal is available - the three weeks waiting on a review, the two weeks a contract spends in circulation. Doing controllable work in that window is allocation rather than avoidance. It is rare that doing nothing while waiting is the better choice

The marker of avoidance, conversely, is not the presence of preparation but repetition. A first tool migration is preparation and a third one is something else. Drafting an outline for the first time is design; revising it for the twelfth is not. When the same kind of work recurs and not one external signal has arrived in between, treat the character of the work as already changed. The version of this story where the toolchain grows while the work stays the same is handled in The Productivity Mirage, so it is not repeated here.

A decision table for preparation versus avoidance

Run one piece of work in hand past these six lines and it usually tips one way. If the marks land on both sides, that work is safer done under a fixed time limit.

Question Answer leaning to preparation Answer leaning to avoidance
How many times have you done this kind of work This is the first Something similar for the third time
What starts once it ends The next step starts straight away Another round of preparation starts
Can an external signal arrive right now No, this is a waiting window Yes, and it is not being sought
What does reversal cost Effectively irreversible Reversible at any point
How would this preparation show itself to be wrong It shows immediately at the next step There is nowhere for it to show
Is there a fixed end point The preparation itself has a deadline No end point was ever set

A weekly review that takes five lines

A criterion that nobody counts does not run. The shorter the counting procedure, the longer it survives. Five lines are enough.

[Weekly review]
1. List everything finished in the past 7 days
2. Beside each item, note who confirmed completion   (me / someone else)
3. If 'someone else' appears zero times, count that week as zero progress
4. If 'me' hits 100% three weeks running, force one signal-bearing item into next week
5. Pick the smallest possible item                   (one paragraph / one screen / one email)

The purpose of the procedure is not to reduce effort. It is to count where the signals came from. The volume is already sufficient in most cases; what is missing is values arriving from outside. There is nothing principled about the number three. One week is indistinguishable from a waiting window and a month is too late to correct, so it sits between them.

  • Moving it to a team - applying the same procedure to tickets yields the share of closed tickets that were closed by someone outside. That value shows how far inward a team has bent
  • But do not tie anything to it - make that share a target and the thing Campbell described happens. Only tickets that are easy for outsiders to close get created, the number climbs, and the meaning drains out. Keep it as a number you watch
  • Keep the record short - one character beside each item. Once the record itself starts getting elaborate, the record is already substitute behaviour

Conclusion - design around the fact that signals are slow

Effort drifting toward the controllable is a problem of signal structure rather than of character. When work whose result comes late and resists attribution sits beside work that delivers a certain signal the moment it ends, people go to the second. From where the person is standing, the choice is reasonable. It is the side where learning comes back immediately.

Splitting what can be fixed from what cannot goes like this. The slowness of signals cannot be fixed. The answer on a manuscript arrives three weeks later and that will not change. What can be fixed is designing a day without knowing it. Arrange the day so that at least one slow signal is put into flight first, and substitute behaviour stops being a sin and becomes the work that fills the waiting time.

The order is the same inside an organization. Abolishing observable outputs is not the answer. Since distortion is a matter of degree, as Campbell wrote, adjusting the weight carried by a value is the manoeuvre that is actually available. Separate the numbers you watch from the numbers you tie things to, and keep a minimum number of items each week that somebody outside confirms. The gap between busy and moving narrows. It does not vanish. A state where that gap has vanished entirely never existed in the first place.

What could not be verified

  • Goodhart's 1975 volume itself - Papers in Monetary Economics volume 1 was not consulted directly. The quoted sentence follows the form recorded identically across several reference works, and the surrounding context of the original paper is outside what was established here
  • The bodies of Strathern 1997 and Campbell 1979 - only the bibliographic details and the quoted sentences were secured. Where each sentence sits within its paper's argument was not collated
  • Page numbers for the Kahneman and Frederick chapter - bibliographies record it as pages 49 to 81 and as pages 103 to 119. The discrepancy looks like an edition difference, but since neither could be settled, no page range appears in the body
  • Per-experiment conditions in Zhu and colleagues 2018 - only the count of five experiments and the claim stated in the abstract were secured. Sample sizes, participant composition and payoff magnitudes per experiment lie outside what was established
  • How far pre-crastination replicates - a 2019 review is reported to gather subsequent work including animal studies following the original nine experiments, but that review was not collated. The extent of replication is not asserted here
  • The original wording behind falsifiability - the phrasing in which the concept was first proposed in philosophy of science was not collated. What appears in the body is a loose borrowing rather than that criterion itself
  • Whether Exit the Cave was edited after publication - newsletter pieces can be revised after they go out. The quoted sentences and the factual details reflect the piece as published on 19 August 2026
