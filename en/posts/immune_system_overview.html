What Is the Immune System?

The immune system is a complex network that protects the body from outside invaders and internal threats. It detects and eliminates not only pathogens such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites, but also abnormal cells such as cancer cells. Without it, we would be easily overwhelmed by serious infections from the countless pathogens we meet in daily life.

"Immunity" is an everyday expression rather than a strict medical term. This article focuses on how the immune system is built and how it works, and it does not claim that any particular food or supplement raises immunity.

Components of the Immune System

The immune system is usually described as physical barriers, innate (natural) immunity, and adaptive (acquired) immunity. The two arms do not work separately; they exchange signals.

1. Physical Barriers

The first line of defense includes the skin, mucous membranes, tears, saliva, and stomach acid. These barriers block pathogens from entering the body physically and chemically.

2. Innate (Natural) Immunity

Innate immunity is the immunity we are born with, and it responds immediately when pathogens enter the body. Its main components include:

  • White blood cells: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes, and macrophages attack or engulf pathogens. Dendritic cells show pieces of a pathogen to T cells and so link innate to adaptive immunity.
  • Natural killer cells (NK cells): recognize and destroy virus-infected cells and cancer cells.
  • Inflammatory response: draws immune cells to the site of infection or injury, producing redness, swelling, heat, and pain. It is uncomfortable but part of the defense process.
  • Complement system: a group of proteins that directly attack pathogens or mark them so other immune cells recognize them more easily.

3. Adaptive (Acquired) Immunity

This system builds a memory of specific pathogens so the body can respond faster and more strongly on re-infection. Its main components include:

  • T lymphocytes (T cells): divided into cytotoxic T cells, which attack infected cells directly, and helper T cells, which regulate other immune cells.
  • B lymphocytes (B cells): when activated, they differentiate into cells that make antibodies matched to a specific pathogen.
  • Antibodies (immunoglobulins): proteins that bind a specific pathogen or toxin, neutralizing it or making it easier for other immune cells to remove.
  • Memory cells: keep information about previously encountered pathogens so the body can respond quickly on re-infection.

What Is Immunity?

Immunity is an everyday way of describing how effectively the immune system fights pathogens and disease. It cannot be measured with one number, and saying it has been "raised" or "lowered" is hard to justify, because the immune system is a composite of different cells and organs working together.

Immune function is genuinely lowered by medical conditions such as inherited immunodeficiency, immunosuppressants, steroids or chemotherapy, severe malnutrition, and poorly controlled diabetes. In those cases medical care comes first, not lifestyle adjustment.

The Relationship Between the Immune System and Immunity

The immune system is the protective network itself, and immunity is a way of talking about how well that network works. Several factors affect how it functions.

1. Immune System Imbalance

A stronger immune response is not simply better; balance matters. Overactivity can lead to allergies and autoimmune disease, while underactivity leaves the body vulnerable to infection. That is why "maximizing immunity" cannot be a medical goal.

2. Immunosenescence

As we age, immune function gradually declines, leaving older adults more susceptible to infection. This is called immunosenescence, and it is one reason vaccination is emphasized for older adults.

3. Immune Memory

A defining feature of adaptive immunity is storing information about a pathogen met before, so the response is more effective on re-exposure. The CDC explains that a vaccine introduces a killed or weakened form of the organism so the body makes antibodies, which is active immunity. This immune memory is the principle behind vaccines. Passive immunity, in which a person is given antibodies, lasts only weeks or months.

Lifestyle Habits That Support Immune Function, and the Evidence

An earlier version of this section was titled "How to Strengthen Immunity" and said foods and supplements strengthen immune cells. That went beyond what primary sources support, and it was corrected in the 2026-09-29 update. The table below shows what is confirmed and where the limits are.

FactorWhat is confirmedLimits and cautions
SleepThe CDC recommends that adults (ages 18-60) sleep at least 7 hours a night, and links sleeping less than 7 hours with higher risk of obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure, among others.This is a general health benchmark, not a figure for how much sleep reduces infection.
ExerciseWHO recommends adults do 150-300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days.A general health recommendation, not a measure of infection reduction. Do not push hard when you have body aches or a fever.
NutritionThe NIH Office of Dietary Supplements says vitamins A, C, D, E, selenium, and zinc are important for proper immune function, and that clinical deficiencies of these nutrients can weaken immunity and raise susceptibility to infection.Correcting a deficiency is different from eating more to raise immunity. Evidence that going beyond recommended amounts boosts immunity is generally limited. The effect of vitamin C supplements may vary with a person's vitamin C status, and probiotics are described only as something that might affect immune function.
VaccinationThe CDC explains that vaccines produce active immunity, which is long-lasting and sometimes life-long.Who should be vaccinated, and when, differs by country and age. Follow your national or local health authority.

1. A Balanced Diet

The basics are eating vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish, legumes, and nuts so that no nutrient runs short. Getting nutrients from food is safer than from supplements. If you suspect a deficiency, or you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a chronic condition, or take medication, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking supplements. Daily requirements and upper limits differ by age and sex, so check your national dietary reference intakes.

2. Regular Exercise and Enough Sleep

Keep up low-burden activity such as walking and keep bedtime and wake time consistent. Aim for the WHO and CDC recommendations in the table, adjusted to how your body feels.

3. Stress Management

Long-lasting stress has been reported to be associated with a higher risk of catching a cold later (Cohen et al., 2012). It cannot be said to "sharply lower" immunity, however. Keep up rest and social contact, and talk to a professional if stress is disrupting daily life.

4. Vaccination

Vaccination is regarded as the best-established means of preventing infectious disease. Check which vaccines apply to you and your records with your local health authority or clinic.

5. Alcohol and Smoking

Excessive alcohol and smoking are harmful to health overall, so cutting back or quitting is advised. This update did not confirm from a primary source how large their effect on immune function is.

Recent Trends in Immunology Research

Immunology keeps advancing. The topics below are under active research and are not conclusions that a general reader can apply to daily life.

1. Immunotherapy

These treatments help a patient's own immune system recognize and attack cancer cells. They are used in practice for some cancers, but the effect differs by cancer type and patient, so whether to use them is a decision for the treating clinicians.

2. Microbiome Research

The interaction between gut microbes and the immune system is being studied. How particular microbes change immunity in people is still under research, and this update found no basis to claim that a specific food or product improves immunity by adjusting gut microbes.

3. mRNA Technology

Research is under way to extend the mRNA technology used in COVID-19 vaccines to vaccines for other infections and to treatments. How far it applies depends on the results of each clinical trial.

When to Talk to a Professional

Some signs are not problems to be solved by adjusting habits. Talk to a clinician if any of the following applies.

  • If you have trouble breathing or chest pain, or symptoms are getting worse quickly, seek care without delay.
  • When a fever or cough will not go away, or unexplained weight loss comes with it
  • When infections such as ear infections, pneumonia, or sinusitis keep coming back or recovery is unusually slow
  • When you take immunosuppressants, steroids, or chemotherapy, or have an underlying condition such as diabetes

Conclusion

The immune system is a defense system in which physical barriers, innate immunity, and adaptive immunity work together, and its key word is balance rather than strength. What is confirmed is the basics: a diet without deficiencies, sleep, exercise, stress management, and vaccination, rather than any single food or supplement said to raise immunity. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist first about supplements and about repeated infections.

## 1. Innate vs adaptive immunity

| Arm | How it responds | Main components | Memory |

|---|---|---|

## 3. Questions to ask about "boost your immunity" claims

- Does it claim one food or supplement raises immunity?
- Is it about correcting a deficiency, or about extra intake raising immunity further?
- Does it say "maximize", ignoring balance? (Too strong a response can cause allergies or autoimmune disease.)

## 4. Signs to see a clinician first

- Trouble breathing, chest pain, or symptoms getting worse quickly
- Fever or cough that will not clear, or unexplained weight loss with it
- Repeated ear infections, pneumonia, or sinusitis, or unusually slow recovery
- Taking immunosuppressants, steroids, or chemotherapy, or having diabetes or another underlying condition
