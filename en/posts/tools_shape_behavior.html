What Is Easy Becomes the Default - What It Actually Means to Say Tools Are Not Neutral

You can change it, and mostly you do not. How defaults, counters and friction decide most of the outcome

Aug 31, 2026 Trends & Culture

Most people never open the settings screen on a new tool. Those who do open it look at a handful of items and close it again. And for years afterwards, a large share of what they do with that tool runs along whatever was switched on from the start. This is not a story about laziness. It is how people normally use tools.

That is why what is easy stops being a matter of taste and becomes a matter of outcomes. This piece is not here to repeat the large sentence that tools change us. It is here to strip that sentence down to the part you can actually check. Three things can be checked: the default, the number the tool counts, and where the friction sits. And then the return trip, where people bend all three back.

This is a general observation about tool design and user behaviour, not an assessment of any particular product, service or company. The studies cited differ in period and sample, so none of them transfers wholesale to an individual case.

The trigger - an argument against just a tool

In late August 2026 an English-language blog post circulated in a Korean developer community. The title argued that there is no such thing as just a tool, and the claim was summarised like this: a tool does not only extend what a person can do, it changes what feels natural to do. The supporting material was a philosophical analysis of equipment, in which a familiar tool merges with its user, and the examples ranged from eating utensils and cars to the argument that a choice of frontend framework shapes team structure and the hierarchy of expertise inside it.

The reaction in the same thread was the interesting part. Readers who agreed pointed out that technology can be designed to strengthen the position of particular groups. Readers who were sceptical noted that the utensil and framework comparisons look less like causation and more like explanation after the fact. Someone else objected that food culture cannot simply be attributed to cutlery. That objection is fair. The statement that tools change something is broad enough that, left broad, it can be neither confirmed nor refuted.

So rather than restate the original, this piece keeps only the part of it that can be measured. That part is a single sentence. A tool makes some actions cheap and other actions expensive. Everything after that is arithmetic rather than assertion.

The narrow sense of not neutral

Not neutral reads two ways. One reading is that a tool wants something. It is an attractive reading with no way to check it, and a claim that cannot be checked does not win arguments. The other reading is that a tool rewrites the price list for behaviour. That one you can count. Which action takes two taps, and which sits three menu levels down, is visible the moment you open the thing.

The historian of technology Melvin Kranzberg put his first law this way in Technology and Culture in 1986: technology is neither good nor bad, nor is it neutral. The first clause blocks the habit of assigning moral character to machines. The second blocks the comforting idea that nothing follows from them. What he meant by the second clause was that the same technology, placed in a different context, produces different results. Not that it has a will.

An older observation from philosophy points at the same place. In the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Heidegger's analysis of equipment, a tool that has become familiar is not experienced as an object while it is being used. You are objectively using the door as a door, but you are not experiencing the door as a door. The tool shows up as an object mostly when it breaks. That matters for a practical reason rather than a mystical one: what has receded into the background does not get reviewed.

  • Checkable claim — in this tool, A takes two moves and B takes five. Open it and count
  • Hard-to-check claim — this tool changed how people think. There is no agreed comparison case
  • What this piece uses — only the first. The second appears only as an accumulation of the first

The default decides most of it

Being able to change something and changing it are different facts. The sharpest illustration of that gap is organ donation. In a short 2003 paper in Science volume 302, Eric J. Johnson and Daniel G. Goldstein showed that differences in consent rates across European countries track the default setting on the enrolment form more closely than national character or religion. Countries where you had to sign up sat around ten percent. Countries where you were enrolled unless you said otherwise sat close to a hundred.

Stop there and defaults look like a universal lever. But a 2025 follow-up in PNAS Nexus traced what happened next. In countries that switched to an opt-out regime, deceased donation rose by 1.21 donors per million people per year, an increase that was not statistically significant, while living donation fell by 4.59, a drop of 29 percent. In other words, the default bites hardest on the enrolment screen, and the effect does not carry downstream at the same size. Holding both papers together is what lets you talk about defaults without overselling them.

The conditions under which a default holds are fairly predictable. The left column below is the condition; the right column is where it breaks down.

ConditionWhy the default survivesWhere the condition breaks
The choice comes up rarelyYou decide once and forget. Nothing prompts you to reopen the settingsDaily exposure lets irritation accumulate until you finally change it
The consequence arrives lateNothing visibly improves today if you change it todayAnything that hurts immediately gets changed on day one
The path to change it is off-screenFinding which button to press is itself a costA switch on the same screen produces a different rate entirely
You cannot judge the right answer yourselfThe default reads as a recommendationWhere people hold strong preferences, defaults are ignored
  • Setting a default is not the absence of a decision. It is choosing the option that most people will end up with
  • So the line that users could have changed it does not explain the design decision. Changeability is not evidence of change
  • Conversely, not every default is a problem. Pre-filling the value that suits most users is good design
  • The dividing line is single. Does the value tilt toward the user or toward the provider?

What the tool counts becomes what matters

Tools count things. What is counted stays on the screen, and what stays on the screen ends up in the meeting. What is not counted does not reach the meeting. This chain does not require anyone to intend it, because the only thing that works as shared evidence in a room is a number everyone can see.

The social scientist Donald T. Campbell wrote in Evaluation and Program Planning in 1979 that the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it was meant to monitor. The line is usually quoted about test scores or performance reviews, but it applies unchanged to a product dashboard. A metric is an observation device and an incentive device at once.

Leaving it there tells half the story, because counting also bought something. Things became visible for the first time once they were counted: where latency actually accumulates, which documents are actually read, which step in a process loses the most people. Before counting, all of that was handled by intuition. The problem is not the counting. It is the moment the counted items monopolise the language of the argument.

  • Look at which columns a tool counts and you can read what the organisation that built it treats as success. That is a more honest signal than the marketing page
  • Uncounted work does not disappear. It just loses the language it could be argued in, and therefore loses negotiations
  • The response is not to abolish metrics but to write down where the metric and the reality diverge. The person who knows the divergence uses the number best

Where the friction sits decides the result

Hard things happen less often. The sentence is so obvious that it drops out of design discussions, yet a large part of building a tool is choosing where to put the friction. Put one confirmation step in front of an irreversible action and that action becomes rarer. Some of what becomes rarer is mistakes. Some of it is legitimate work.

Reversibility matters most. If an action cannot be undone, people get careful, and careful people attempt less. If it can be undone, people are casual, and casual people attempt more. Neither of those is simply good. Places that need experiments need undo; places where one mistake is unrecoverable need friction. Trouble starts when a single rule is applied across an entire product without separating the two.

Where friction is addedWhat decreasesWhat decreases along with it
Confirmation before deleteIrreversible mistakesTidying up. Dead material keeps accumulating
Undo-send waiting periodHasty messagesImmediacy. The rhythm of a conversation slows
Approval before deployUnreviewed changesSmall fixes. Changes get batched into large ones
Identity checks at signupBulk automated accountsEntry. Some legitimate users drop out too
No undo at allCasual attemptsAttempts themselves. Experiment and learning shrink together
  • Friction is a question of distribution, not total amount. Make everything smooth and the dangerous actions get smooth as well
  • Treat friction purely as a safety device and you also close the path along which learning happens. No attempts, no feel for the thing
  • A workable rule of thumb: measure how long undo takes, and place friction only in front of the actions where that number is large

But people change tools too

Stopping here would leave the claim that tools determine people. That is half of it, and half is wrong. The most famous case in this field shows why. In a 1980 essay in Daedalus, Langdon Winner argued that artefacts can carry political properties, and the example most often quoted from it was a set of low overpasses on Long Island, said to have been built so that buses could not pass and certain groups could not reach the beaches.

In 1999 Bernward Joerges argued in Social Studies of Science that the story does not hold up against the record, and a chain of replies and counter-replies turned the episode into a long-running dispute of its own. The lesson is not that Winner was wrong. It is that the claim that intent is inscribed in an object wobbled under checking even in its best-known case. Sentences in that direction should always be written one step back from certainty.

In the field the reverse direction is far more common. The feature a designer placed at the centre goes barely used, while something added at the edge becomes the main use. Workflows that an organisation blocks rarely disappear; they route around into another tool. When the official procedure is inconvenient, people rebuild it in a spreadsheet and a chat thread. And the improvised path being absorbed into the product later as an official feature is an ordinary sequence, not a rare one.

  • A default is not a law of nature. It is something decided in a meeting, meetings reconvene, and user pushback does in fact reverse defaults
  • An unused feature is a record of a designer's prediction failing. It is the most common counterexample to the story that tools determine people
  • The same tool is used differently in different organisations. That is what the back half of Kranzberg's sentence looks like in practice
  • So the accurate sentence is this. A tool changes probabilities, it does not make decisions. Change the probabilities and, at scale, the outcome changes

Where responsibility lands on the builder

If choosing a default means choosing the option most people will end up with, then that choice is a design decision, and design decisions carry responsibility. Overstate the scope of that responsibility, though, and it becomes a demand nobody can meet. Builders cannot know every context, and the fact that the same feature is used differently in different countries and organisations is exactly the point of Kranzberg's line.

What can realistically be asked for is closer to disclosure of the structure than a guarantee of results. Three items hold up in practice.

  • Make the default visible. Placing a switch deep in a settings tree and then saying users could have changed it is not an explanation
  • Make it reversible. A feature with undo and a feature without it expose the user to genuinely different amounts of risk
  • Show what changed. If a person cannot tell what happened after flipping a setting, that setting effectively does not exist

Add metrics to that list. Choosing what to count is the same kind of decision as choosing what to default. Whatever sits in the first slot of a dashboard sets the meeting agenda of every organisation using that tool for years. The first slot is not a neutral position.

Counting the losses and the gains together

Conversations about tools tilt easily toward the claim that things used to be better. That direction is usually wrong, because what became possible for the first time once something got easy is generally much larger. Still, something shrank in the same place. The two values are not the kind that cancel out; they are different in kind, which is why they cannot be summed into a verdict. Setting them side by side in a table is more accurate.

AreaWhat grew because it got easyWhat shrank in the same placeCan an individual reverse it?
WayfindingGetting lost on a first visit has nearly vanished. Plans form on the spotThe practice of holding a region's layout in your headYes, though a tight schedule removes the option
TranslationThe range of documents you can read widened beyond comparisonThe habit of going back to check nuance in the originalYes, at a large cost in time
PhotographyVolume of record. Far more moments survive than beforeThe act of selecting. Most photos are never looked at againPartly, through personal rules
WritingThe time to reach a first draft dropped sharplyTime spent stuck, and whatever used to emerge from itYes, though deadlines often do not permit it
PaymentsCompletion rates. Small-value sales became viable at allThe pause at the moment of buying, between deciding and payingPartly, through settings and separate accounts

The column worth studying is the last one. Some of what was lost can be reversed by an individual and some cannot. What can be reversed belongs to the domain of taste; what cannot belongs to the domain of structure. Advice that tells people to solve a structural item through personal resolve generally does not work.

Questions worth asking when you pick a tool

Resolving to use tools mindfully is not an executable instruction, because it leaves blank what to be mindful of. Five questions you can answer in the first few days of using something are more useful. They will not tell you whether the tool is good or bad. They will tell you which way it is tilted relative to you.

  • What can you reach in two moves from the first screen? That is the behaviour this tool is trying to increase
  • What used to take one step and now takes several? That is the behaviour that will decline
  • How many seconds does undo take? Time it. Guessed estimates run short almost every time
  • What does it count, and how often does that number come up in meetings? If those two track each other, the metric has already become the goal
  • Do you know what the defaults are, and have you ever changed one? If not, the tool is still running on the designer's settings

One thing is worth stating plainly. Not every default needs adjusting. Leaving most settings alone is rational. In areas you do not know well the default is on average the better choice, and spending time on features you rarely touch is a net loss. The ones worth touching are the few you hit often and whose results stick around. How many that is differs per person, and that judgement is not one anyone can make on your behalf.

Summary

Saying a tool is not neutral does not mean it has intentions. It is a question of which actions it makes cheap and which it makes expensive. That distribution shows up in three places: the value that is switched on from the start, the number the tool counts, and where the friction sits. All three were decided in meetings, and all three can be decided again.

And the opposite direction runs the whole time. People leave features unused, find other uses, route around blocked paths, and sometimes force a default to be reversed. So the accurate sentence is neither that tools change people nor that a tool is just a tool. Tools change probabilities, and people renegotiate those probabilities.

What is left is not a verdict but a set of questions. In the tool you use now, what is the easiest action, what became harder, and how many seconds does undo take. Answer those three and the direction of the tilt becomes visible on its own. From there it is yours to decide.
