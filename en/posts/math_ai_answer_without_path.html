The Answer Is Correct but Unusable - What Disappears When Only the Conclusion Arrives

A proof settles a proposition and leaves behind tools for the next problem. This is about where those two functions come apart

2026-09-08 Trends & Culture

On 3 September 2026, the mathematician Terence Tao posted a six-part thread on his Mastodon account. It travelled onward through aggregators, and the headline it acquired along the way settled into something close to the danger of prematurely solving mathematical problems with AI alone.

Read that headline by itself and you slide into a familiar argument. Is AI good at mathematics or is it not. The thread is not standing there. It sketches in concrete terms a route by which AI tools could actually resolve the problem in question: machine learning powered simulation, rigorous interval arithmetic and formalization, and a language model proposing candidates. What the thread objects to is not those tools but the arrangement they are placed in. One arrangement specifically. The case where the whole iteration happens out of sight and only the final answer comes out.

So the axis that actually divides this subject is not capability. It is whether an answer being correct and an answer being usable are the same thing. And the point where those two come apart exists outside mathematics too.

Below, sentences from the thread are placed inside 「」 with a note of who wrote them and where. No judgement is offered on the mathematical content. The nature of the problem and the kind of difficulty it presents are as far as this goes, and no proof technique is explained. Items whose wording could not be pinned down are kept out of the body and collected, with reasons, in a later section.

The conditions the thread actually attached

The six posts form a single argument. Cut them apart and the direction changes. So the first thing to do is line up what each part says.

PartWhat it says
1/6Takes the global regularity problem for the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations as its example. The equations come from a very natural physical motivation, the study of incompressible fluids, but the regularity problem itself is 「not important for its direct physical application」. Computational fluid dynamics is already a mature subject, deployed extensively in fields such as the atmospheric sciences, with its empirical capabilities and limitations already well understood. A theoretical guarantee of regularity, or a pathological instance of blowup, would be intellectually interesting but would not radically transform how weather or climate is modelled
2/6Lists what attempts on the question have historically produced anyway: Leray-Hopf weak solutions, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Ladyshenskaya inequalities, the Prodi-Serrin partial regularity theorems, the Beale-Kato-Majda blowup criterion, the Escuriaza-Seregin-Sverak conditional regularity result, and so on. As his own contribution, the author names the introduction of fluid computation and Turing universality into the problem, which he writes led to unexpected connections with symplectic topology
3/6Writes that with recent advances in related fluid equations it is now the emerging consensus that the answer to the global regularity problem is negative, meaning there should exist very specific initial conditions that develop singularities in finite time. Then lays out a four-step strategy for locating such conditions
4/6Says the steps are 「incredibly complicated, and interlock with each other」. Even so, a heroic combination of tools might resolve the problem. The final construction would likely be enormously complicated and 「impossible to verify by purely human means」, and verification in a formal language such as Lean might end up 「among the largest such proof artefacts ever created」
5/6Then says that such an incomprehensible proof 「would not be the primary value of the exercise」. The value lies in starting with one ansatz, discovering the precise obstruction that prevents it from working, adjusting to eliminate that obstruction, and iterating. 6/6Returns to why problems in pure mathematics get posed at all. Not because the solutions are desperately wanted in themselves, but because past experience shows that human-directed efforts to solve them tend to spur the development of the field. Solving such a problem prematurely by purely AI-powered methods, particularly without full transparency into the process, can contaminate that dynamic to the point of becoming a net negative

One more thing has to be attached to all of this. Two days later, on 5 September, the same account replied to its own thread. Responding to rumours about a possible solution to Navier-Stokes, it says the author is 「not aware of any significant developments in this regard」 and that the preceding discussion is hypothetical. It does add a qualifier: the scenario is 「not completely implausible at the current level of development of AI technology」.

Strip that reply away and the six posts read as a report of events. Turning a conditional scenario into a rumour that something has been solved takes nothing more than one link falling off the end.

The two functions of a proof - settling and leaving behind

A proof does more than one job. At least two, and the two can be separated.

First, a proof settles a proposition. A sentence whose truth value was unknown becomes fixed as true. This is the function most people picture, and it is where the prizes and the headlines attach.

Second, a proof leaves behind tools for the next problem. In the course of building it, concepts get invented, inequalities get proved, criteria get established for what holds under which conditions. Those things then get used again in places that have nothing to do with the original question.

The list in part 2/6 is precisely an inventory of that second function. Not one item on it solved the global regularity problem. Every one of them is a by-product of attempts that failed, and those by-products flowed outward into fluid mechanics, analysis and partial differential equations. The contribution the author names as his own has the same shape: bringing fluid computation and Turing universality into the problem, with the result being a connection to symplectic topology that nobody was looking for.

The second function is not an appendix to the first. Part 6/6 states plainly why problems in pure mathematics get posed in the first place. Not because anyone desperately wants the solution in and of itself, but because past experience shows that human-directed efforts to solve such problems tend to spur the development of the field. The problem is an instrument, not a destination.

Once that distinction is in place, the next question arrives on its own. Is there any reason the two functions have to travel together as one body? There is not. A deliverable can perfectly well carry the first and not the second.

Why there is prize money on the problem

Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute. They were announced at a meeting held in Paris on 24 May 2000 at the College de France, with one million dollars allocated to the solution of each problem. Clay's own page describes the equations as governing 「the flow of fluids such as water and air」 and notes that there is no proof for 「the most basic questions one can ask: do solutions exist, and are they unique」.

The stated purpose of the prizes touches this article's axis directly. They were conceived to record some of the most difficult problems mathematicians were grappling with at the turn of the second millennium, to elevate in the consciousness of the general public the fact that the frontier in mathematics is still open and abounds in important unsolved problems, to emphasize the importance of working towards a solution of the deepest and most difficult problems, and to recognize achievement in mathematics of historical magnitude.

The phrase worth pausing on is working towards a solution. What the prize is attached to is not only a final line of output.

The rules make this sharper. Clay's rules require that a proposed solution be published in a qualifying outlet, that at least two years have passed since publication, and that the proposed solution have received general acceptance in the global mathematics community. Only then does consideration begin.

These rules are not a verdict on machine verification. When they were framed, the tools we have now did not exist. But what they presuppose is unambiguous. The procedure is designed so that a correct answer on its own does not make it run. Time for people to read it, to use it, to try to break it is written into the rules as a hard requirement. The institution already knew that the correctness of an answer and the acceptance of an answer are two different events.

Four steps - the strategy as written

Part 3/6 lays out a four-step strategy for locating initial conditions that develop singularities. Below is that strategy as written. The right-hand column, describing the shape of the information left in hand when a step gets stuck, is this article's summary rather than the thread's words.

StepAs written in the threadShape of what is left when it stalls
(a)Design a nearly-self-similar ansatz for a finite time blowup solutionA candidate is ruled out. What ruled it out remains
(b)Locate an approximate solution to this ansatz, one that numerically obeys the ansatz up to an extremely small, computable residualThe residual will not shrink as far as needed. Where it refuses to shrink remains
(c)Demonstrate that, in suitably renormalized coordinates, the ansatz is stable around this numerical solution and can be perturbed into an exact solution if the residual is small enoughStability does not come out. The direction of the instability remains
(d)Verify that the residual of the solution falls within the threshold of stability of the solutionThe threshold is not met. The distance between threshold and residual remains

Part 4/6 says these steps are 「incredibly complicated, and interlock with each other」. It also separates two kinds of failure. Many naive ansatze can be ruled out immediately, for reasons such as violation of conservation of energy. Other ansatze might initially seem viable and can only be ruled out after extremely intensive numerical computation.

That second kind is the protagonist of this article. Expensive failure produces information proportional to its expense. Being ruled out only after the computation has run all the way through means that how far the candidate held up and where it broke is inscribed in the results.

The condition that the answer must not be known in advance

The strongest sentence in the whole argument sits in part 5/6. Having said that iterating on the ansatz is what produces insight, it immediately attaches a condition. The iteration only works well at producing such insight if the iterator does not have access to the final ansatz in advance. Knowing the answer naturally inhibits the exploration of alternate routes that are superficially dead ends. And those routes, in the thread's phrasing, 「end up being highly instructive in the nature of their failure」.

To see why that condition is strong, compare it with the usual demand. The standard reaction to a conclusion arriving on its own is give me the process too. That treats the problem as one that a later disclosure fixes.

The thread's condition sits upstream of that. It claims there is something that later disclosure does not restore. Receiving the final route afterwards does not cause the other routes to have been explored. And once the answer is known, that exploration does not happen again. It is the same reason a person who has seen the solution finds it hard to take another path seriously, and the same reason a problem worked after peeking at the answer leaves so little behind.

Which puts this argument on the opposite side from cannot do it. The problem arises precisely because an AI system can carry that iteration through on its own. If it could not, the worry would never get off the ground.

What a dead end actually produces

Failure is knowledge is a phrase in wide circulation, usually as consolation. That is not the sense needed here. What a blocked attempt yields has to be broken into items.

It narrows the range of the next attempt

When a candidate is ruled out, it is rarely just that one candidate disappearing. Often the whole family it belongs to shrinks with it. A reason like violation of conservation of energy applies not to a single ansatz but to every ansatz sharing that property. So one exclusion cuts the search space by families. That cut is not contained in the answer. The answer holds only the one thing that survived.

The reason for failure exposes a property of the object

The grounds for exclusion are information about the candidate and, at the same time, information about the problem itself. If an attempt is blocked by a particular conserved quantity, how forcefully that quantity acts in that direction becomes visible along with it. A single successful route carries almost none of this. What a successful route tells you is that this way works, and it says nothing about why the other ways do not.

It builds a map of costs

Which kinds of judgement resolve in a few hours of computation and which kinds swallow months is something you find out by doing it. That map is what the next person uses to decide where to spend resources. A conclusion-only deliverable drops the cost information entirely. Look at the final route alone and everything appears to have run straight in that direction from the beginning.

Put the three together and it comes out like this. The answer is a point in a space; the record of failures is the terrain of that space. Receive a point and you know where it sits, but not what surrounds it. The next problem is usually not at that point but somewhere in the surroundings.

Verification and understanding are different cells

The obvious objection arrives here. If a machine has checked the proof line by line, does it matter whether any human understands it?

Checking raises whether it is true. Understanding gives why it is true. They are non-overlapping cells, and the important thing is that the thread itself rests on that separation. Part 4/6 says the final construction would be impossible to verify by purely human means and that the formal artefact could be among the largest ever made; part 5/6 then says that precisely this incomprehensible proof would not be the primary value of the exercise. That is not a rejection of formal verification. It is a statement that the cell formal verification fills and the cell it leaves empty are different cells.

What formalization does and does not guarantee is separated out in what thirteen million lines of formalized proof actually establish. That article's axis is what becomes certain when verification gets stronger; this one's axis is what goes missing when only the answer arrives. The directions are opposite, so neither substitutes for the other.

If the question you have is where the line between machine-checkable and non-machine-checkable answers falls, where a verification harness draws that line is the right place.

What the thread does not oppose

The surest way not to read the argument backwards is to copy out the picture it supports. The route sketched in part 4/6 is this: machine learning powered simulation, rigorous interval arithmetic and, or, formalization, and language model generated proposals for a suitable ansatz. A heroic combination of the three, all of it guided by expert human mathematicians iteratively learning from previous attempts. Such a combination, the thread says, could potentially resolve the problem.

Look at that list of tools and it becomes clear the tools are not the target. Machine learning is on it. Language models are on it. The only things that change are who is doing the iterating and whether the iteration is visible from outside.

The opposing scenario in part 5/6 uses the same tools. An autonomous AI harness, backed by an enormous amount of computational resources, performs the entire iteration internally, produces the final ansatz and thence the solution, while the company running the harness keeps the process almost completely out of public view. The conclusion is stated flatly: 「Technically, one of the most prominent open problems in mathematics would now be solved; but there would be almost no value added to mathematics as a consequence.」

The thread allows that a third party might, with herculean and heavily AI-assisted effort, reverse-engineer some portion of the process and recover some actual insight, while adding that this would be far less efficient than obtaining the solution through a mix of human mathematicians and machine assistance. Recovery is possible; the price is different.

The reply of 5 September puts a name on this: opportunity cost. There is, it says, a substantial opportunity cost in converting a historically productive and motivating problem into 「a mere viral social media post advertising some benchmark progress」, rather than actually advancing the field and developing the next generation of both problems to ask and people to work on them.

Same conclusion, four kinds of deliverable

Fold everything so far into one table. Assume all four rows carry the same conclusion. The only thing that varies is the form in which it arrives.

Form of deliveryIs the proposition settledCan a third party use it next timeWhat is left behind
1. A conclusion, one lineNot settled. One more assertion existsNoNews
2. Conclusion plus a machine-checkable proof artefactSettled to the extent you trust the checker and the translation of the statementBarely. The unit of reuse is not a human conceptThe fact that it is true
3. Conclusion plus a description of the final routeSettledWithin the reach of that routeOne successful path
4. The full iteration plus discarded routes and why they were discardedSettledBroadlyThe range for the next attempt and a map of costs

This is not a table where higher numbers are better. Row four is expensive to produce and is overkill for most everyday work. All the table says is that the form you received determines what you can do with the answer. Receive row two and try to do row four work with it, and the time simply vanishes.

Where the same structure shows up elsewhere

From here on, nothing about other fields is being settled on the strength of a mathematical argument. There is no basis for claiming that what holds for Navier-Stokes holds identically for code, for diagnosis, or for legal documents. What can be done is to point at places where the structure looks similar. Similar is as far as this goes.

Code - a patch that works

Say you receive a change that runs. Tests pass and the symptom is gone. What is missing is why this approach rather than another. The next person to touch that area repeats the same search from the beginning. Where the bottleneck moved once generation became cheap is already covered in how understanding became the new bottleneck, so it is not repeated here.

Reading and diagnosis - conclusion severed from grounds

When an assessment arrives as a conclusion with no grounds attached, the recipient stacks the next decision on top of something they cannot check. As long as the conclusion is right, nothing happens. The trouble starts when conditions change. Without grounds there is no way to know which change would shake the conclusion, and therefore no way to set a moment for re-evaluation.

Legal and regulatory review - an opinion that cannot be reused

A one-line verdict of permitted or not permitted applies only to the matter it was written for. Without knowing which provision and which element it turned on, and which reading would flip it, the next matter, similar but not identical, has to be asked from scratch.

What the three share is this. Is the deliverable an answer to this instance, or is it something usable on the next one?

The difference deserves stating just as plainly. In mathematics the degree to which a failed route becomes knowledge in its own right is unusually high. In most working contexts, most dead ends really are nothing. So a demand to hand over the entire process is a cost in itself. You have to choose what to ask for, and the criterion for choosing is in the next section.

What to ask for when you get only a conclusion

The measure for whether an ask is worth it is easiest kept as a single question. If the answer is wrong, who pays how much? When the cost is small, one line of conclusion is enough. When the cost is large, or other decisions will stack on top of this one, or the same kind of judgement will recur, you have to buy the process.

What to ask forWhat it preventsIf you do not get it
Discarded candidates and why each was droppedThe next person repeating the same search from zeroDo not carry the conclusion over to other matters
The conditions under which the conclusion breaksContinuing to use it after conditions have quietly changedNail the scope down to this instance only
Every input needed to reproduce itLosing any way to check whether the same answer comes backTreat it as a reference input, not an established fact
Whether an independent route existsStacking decisions on a single point of failureAttach one cheap cross-check of your own
Degree of confidence and where it comes fromMistaking a confident tone for strong groundsUse it only in a form you can reverse

Writing the ask down once saves rebuilding it every time. The form below can be pasted as is.

Received: 1 conclusion
Additionally required
  1. Candidates that were tried and dropped, and why each was dropped
  2. Three conditions under which this conclusion stops holding
  3. Whether another route reaches the same conclusion, and if so, that route
  4. Every input needed to reproduce it - versions, settings, sources consulted
  5. Degree of confidence, and where that confidence comes from

Format
  - As a list, not as prose
  - Discarded routes before the successful one

The last line carries the weight. Ask for the successful route first and the successful route is usually all that comes. Ask for what was thrown away first and you get terrain.

When asking does not work

Sometimes you ask and do not receive. The other side cannot produce it, or will not, or never kept it. What is available then is not changing the deliverable but changing the standing of the deliverable.

  • Demote the conclusion one notch - treat it as a probable input rather than an established fact, and write that down in the document. Standing that is not written down turns into equal weight for everything within days
  • Keep the dependency shallow - do not stack several layers of decision on top of it. The deeper the stack, the more the cost of reversal multiplies
  • Attach one cheap cross-check - find the cheapest independent way to arrive at the same conclusion. Often a single point of comparison is enough, not a full reproduction
  • Use it only in reversible form - if what to roll back and how is decided in advance, the conclusion can be used as it stands
  • Price the recovery in advance - as the thread notes, reverse-engineering part of it is possible, only far less efficient. Estimate that inefficiency now instead of discovering it later

None of the five improves the quality of the deliverable. They are devices for limiting the damage when the deliverable is poor. Mix the two purposes and the response lands in the wrong place.

How to read the next round of this argument

This subject will come back in a different shape. Three separations, made up front, keep the argument from inverting.

  • Who wrote it and where - a personal account posting a serial thread, an institutional announcement, or a media summary. This case is the first, and the six parts form a single argument. Detach one part and it reads backwards
  • Hypothesis or report - this case is a conditional scenario, and the author said so explicitly two days later. When that reply does not travel with the thread, the scenario is dressed up as an event
  • Capability or arrangement - if a piece is about capability, benchmark numbers are a rebuttal. If it is about arrangement, benchmark numbers rebut nothing. This case is the latter, and its structure is one where better performance makes the argument stronger

The headline formula that AI solving mathematics is dangerous already fails on the third item. What the thread calls dangerous is not the solving but the arrangement in which results emerge while the process stays invisible. And that arrangement is not confined to mathematics.

What could not be confirmed

  • The body of the official problem description - Clay's official statement of Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness is supplied as a PDF authored by Fefferman, and its text did not open as readable characters. So the body here uses only the sentences on Clay's own description page and rules page, and does not touch the formal statements labelled (A) through (D)
  • The full text of the qualifying outlet clause - the rules page states that a solution must be published in a qualifying outlet, that at least two years must have passed since publication, and that the solution must have received general acceptance in the global mathematics community. The clause defining a qualifying outlet sits in a separate PDF whose wording was not obtained
  • Attribution of the theorems listed in 2/6 - the theorem names and personal names are carried over exactly as the thread writes them. What each theorem states, and precisely to whom each name should be attributed, is outside the scope kept here, which excludes judgements on mathematical content
  • The basis for the emerging consensus toward a negative answer - the thread cites recent advances in related fluid equations but attaches no list of papers. Which results produced that consensus cannot be established from the thread alone
  • The comparison behind largest proof artefact - the thread says the formal artefact might be among the largest ever created, but does not say what it is being compared against or how size would be measured
  • The substance of the rumours mentioned on 5 September - the reply says only that it responds to recent rumours about a possible solution, without saying what those rumours were. So the content of the rumours is not discussed in the body either
  • Third-party replies in the thread - a number of replies from other users sit beneath the six posts. The body deals only with the author's own six posts and his reply of 5 September. Third-party replies are not quoted, since they can be misread as the author's position
  • Quantitative grounds for code, diagnosis and legal review - the section on similar structures goes only as far as structural resemblance. No measurements of the losses that conclusion-only deliverables actually cause in those fields are attached
