How to Choose an Electric Heater

Heat reaches you by different paths depending on the type, the bill is set by wattage and tariff tier, and accidents start at clearance and wiring rather than performance

2026-08-16 Life & Practical

This article recommends no particular product or brand. Electric heaters connect directly to fire, burn, and carbon monoxide incidents, so safety takes up close to half of what follows. All tariff figures and certification rules here are Korean — the rate table is KEPCO residential low voltage as of August 2026, and the marking scheme is Korea KC. Readers elsewhere should substitute their own utility tariff and national certification mark; the physics and the safety rules carry over unchanged. Rated wattage and minimum clearance are set by the manufacturer label and manual, and nothing here replaces those.

The room is cold. Turning up the boiler heats the whole home when you only need the chair you are sitting in, so you go looking for something smaller. What comes back is a wall of options: fan heaters, convectors, carbon heaters, oil radiators, wall mounted panels, footwell units. The copy on all of them reads about the same. Fast, economical, safe.

Three things actually decide this purchase. How much space you need to warm, how many hours it will run, and what sits near it and which outlet it uses. The first two set the type and the bill. The third sets whether anything goes wrong. Product pages usually cover only the first.

Summer air movers are a separate question, covered in choosing between a fan and an air circulator. This article is only about devices that generate heat. Boiler decisions live in when to replace a boiler and boiler maintenance, and the wider tariff picture is in cutting your electricity bill.

1. Wattage Is the Heat Output — Why a High Efficiency Heater Does Not Exist

This is where heater shopping departs from every other appliance. A refrigerator or a washing machine can be engineered to do the same job on less electricity. But a heater that makes heat from electrical resistance turns essentially all of its input into heat. Even the share that leaves as visible glow ends up absorbed by walls, furniture, and skin, and becomes heat there. There is no escape path that fails to end in heat.

So a fan heater marked 1,000W and an oil radiator marked 1,000W put the same amount of heat into the room over an hour. To the meter they are identical: 1kWh. For a heater to save thirty percent of your electricity, it would have to produce more heat from the same watts, and in resistance heating that ceiling is already closed.

There is exactly one exception: the heat pump. It does not make heat, it carries heat from the outside air into the room, so the heat delivered indoors can exceed the electrical energy spent. This is what the heating mode of a reversible air conditioner does, and it is the only axis on which a heating bill genuinely falls by half. Two resistance heaters compared against each other cannot produce that gap. The caveat is that performance drops as outdoor temperatures fall and defrost cycles interrupt operation, so the size of the advantage depends on conditions.

Why They Still Feel Different — the Path Heat Takes

Total heat can be identical while the person in the chair feels something completely different, because the route from element to body differs. That route is the real selection axis.

  • Radiant — infrared leaves the element and lands on your body without warming the air in between. A cold room still feels warm the moment you sit in front of it. Put anything between you and the element and the warmth stops instantly
  • Natural convection — warmed air rises, cool air slides in underneath, and the room evens out slowly. Silent and uniform, but it takes time before the room is actually warm
  • Forced convection — a fan pushes the warmed air out. Fastest by a wide margin, and it brings fan noise, stirred up dust, and dry air along with it

So two 1,000W units differ enormously in how long until you feel warm and how much warmth survives after you leave the room. Those two, not the heat output, are what you are actually comparing.

2. Heater Types and the Situations That Suit Them

Fan Heater, Convector, Radiant, Oil Radiator

A fan heater blows across a heating element. Warm air arrives the second you switch it on, which makes it the fastest by feel. In exchange, only what the airflow touches gets warm, the fan is audible, and floor dust gets lifted. The outlet area runs hot, so nothing may block the front.

A convector warms air by natural convection with no fan. The silence suits bedrooms and workspaces and the temperature spreads evenly. The catch is that it warms air, so in a poorly insulated room that warm air simply leaves. If the room is drafty, sealing it up pays back faster than adding heaters — see DIY window insulation.

Radiant heaters use quartz, carbon, or halogen elements that throw infrared straight at you. Warmth is immediate in front, and because the air is not being heated the room stays less dry. On the other side, the element surface runs the hottest of any type, so fire and burn risk is highest here, and stepping outside the beam angle means no warmth at all.

An oil radiator heats internal oil, and that heat leaves the surface as radiation and natural convection. Silent, with a surface that is less scorching than an exposed element. It is also slow to come up to temperature and heavy to move. And that slow warm up is exactly why people leave it running, which is where the bill grows — not from inefficiency, but from hours on the clock.

3. Bill Math — Wattage x Hours x Unit Rate

The arithmetic is trivial. What people get wrong is not the multiplication but which unit rate belongs in it.

Energy used (kWh) = rated wattage (W) / 1,000 x hours per day x days

A 1,500W unit, three hours a day, thirty days: 1.5 x 3 x 30 = 135kWh. Then comes the unit rate, and that rate depends on how much the household has already used that month. Under the KEPCO residential low voltage table current in August 2026, energy charges run 120.0 KRW per kWh for the first 200kWh, 214.6 KRW for the next 200kWh, and 307.3 KRW beyond 400kWh. So the same 135kWh costs different amounts in different homes.

Read across and you see the power of wattage. Read down and you see the power of the tier. The same 1,500W heater costs one household about 16,000 KRW a month and another over 41,000 KRW. Knowing your existing consumption predicts the bill far better than swapping models does.

Treat the table as a comparison floor, not a bill estimate — check the actual number with the Korea electricity bill calculator or the KEPCO customer portal
  • Units with a thermostat stop heating once the set temperature is reached, so real consumption can land below the rated calculation. How far below is decided by the room insulation and the setpoint
  • The reliable method is measuring with a meter or smart plug. A few days of readings give you that home's actual duty cycle

4. The Progressive Tariff — Why Winter Bills Jump in Steps

There is a structural reason winter invoices spike in Korea. The KEPCO residential low voltage tariff widens its tier boundaries only in summer, from 1 July to 31 August, and does not widen them in winter.

  • Other seasons (January to June, September to December) — three tiers at up to 200kWh, 201 to 400kWh, and above 400kWh. Base charges run 910 KRW, 1,600 KRW, and 7,300 KRW in that order
  • Summer (July and August) — the boundaries widen to 300kWh and 450kWh, an allowance made for cooling load
  • Winter gets no such allowance. December and January use the same 200kWh and 400kWh boundaries as any other month, even though heating load rivals summer cooling
  • On top of that sits the super user rate. In summer and in winter, defined as 1 December to the last day of February, consumption above 1,000kWh in a month is charged at 736.2 KRW per kWh

So for a household already running near 400kWh every month, the math gets simple. Nearly all the consumption a new heater adds lands in the third tier. That is the case where a 1,000W unit at three hours a day costs the high 27,000s per month in the table above. Two units, or six hours a day, doubles it. A one or two person household that normally sits below 200kWh carries far less of a burden from the identical appliance. That is why the question of what a heater costs per month has no single answer.

5. How to Check a Claim That a Heater Saves Electricity

Once section 1 is clear, verification is short. There are only a few things to look at.

  • Find the rated wattage first. It is printed on the unit and stated in the manual, in watts. Product pages rarely put it in the graphics and often bury it in the spec table at the bottom. A product where you cannot find this number is telling you something by itself
  • Separate maximum from average wattage. A unit whose thermostat cycles often can show a low average, but that is duty cycle, not efficiency. Any competing product with a thermostat does the same thing under the same conditions
  • Ask what the comparison was against. If the claim is half the cost of a conventional heater, the whole claim rests on how many watts that conventional heater drew. Comparing 2,000W to 1,000W halves it by definition, and that is size, not efficiency
  • Element names do not change the total. Far infrared, carbon, graphene, and ceramic describe the path and wavelength of the heat, not the fraction of electricity that becomes heat. Comfort may genuinely improve; that is a different claim from a lower bill
  • A bill really halves when the method changes. Going from one resistance heater to another halves the cost only when the wattage halves. The one genuinely different axis is the heat pump, which in Korea usually means a reversible air conditioner

Separately, electric fan heaters and electric stoves fall under the Korea Energy Agency efficiency management scheme and must be registered. But being covered by that scheme does not mean resistance heaters differ much in thermal efficiency. The scheme sets labelling duties and a minimum bar; it does not move the physical ceiling described above.

6. Safety 1 — Fire, Clearance, and Wiring

This is the heart of the article. Heater incidents do not come from weak performance; they come from the thirty centimetres around the unit and a single outlet. Korean fire service data shows fires clustering from December through March, and a large share of winter heating equipment fires start not with a defective unit but with someone leaving it running unattended or failing to switch it off.

  • Follow the minimum clearance in the manual exactly. It differs by product and is often stated separately for front, back, sides, and top. A product that does not state it at all is one to skip
  • Never block the front of the element. Curtains, blankets, sofa backs, discarded clothing, cardboard, and drying racks keep reappearing at the top of incident lists
  • Put nothing on top of the unit. Drying wet towels or socks on a heater is the worst version of this: it blocks the vent and presses combustible material against the element at the same time
  • Plug it into a wall outlet on its own. Heaters sit at the high end of household draw and easily exceed the rating of a power strip or extension lead. Chaining one strip into another is worse still
  • If you must use a strip, read its printed rating. The rated current and maximum load are marked on the body or packaging, and the total of everything plugged in must stay under that value
  • Stop immediately if the plug or outlet feels hot. That signals a poor contact or an overload. Running the cord pinched, sharply bent, or under a carpet produces the same outcome
  • Switch it off when you leave the room, and wipe dust off the outlet. Damp dust accumulating between the plug pins can lead to tracking, where current leaks across the surface. The risk grows on outlets left occupied all winter

A household fire extinguisher and a standalone smoke alarm are worth having regardless of the heater. Home fire insurance is another thing to review before winter.

7. Safety 2 — Low Temperature Burns and Overnight Use

Burns do not only come from touching something hot. A low temperature burn is tissue damage from prolonged skin contact with something only slightly above body heat, roughly 40 to 50 degrees Celsius. The figures Korean medical outlets cite put serious burn risk at around six hours of exposure at 44 degrees and three hours at 45. The core problem is that it never feels hot, so nothing prompts you to move.

  • Sleep is the worst case. Sensation dulls, posture does not change, and the exposure runs for hours. Every condition for a low temperature burn is satisfied at once
  • Do not run a heater unattended all night. Beyond burns, you cannot notice bedding sliding onto the element or the unit tipping over. If you use one anyway, set a timer for automatic shutoff
  • Avoid arrangements with bare skin in contact, and be stricter where sensation is reduced. Tucking a footwell heater under a duvet or sleeping with legs pressed against a radiator recurs in incident reports. Children and older adults cannot readily move away
  • Pets approach the heat source, knock units over, and put noses and paws against grilles. Skip exposed radiant designs and choose a model that cuts out when tipped

A 2018 Korea Consumer Agency safety advisory on electric heating mats found that of 2,411 reported harm cases, fire, overheating, and explosion made up 62.9 percent, the largest group, with burn injuries concentrated on the buttocks and legs. The two takeaways are that more than half of incidents fall in the three winter months, and that these products should never be used together with latex bedding. The advisory is some years old, but the mechanism behind the incidents has not changed.

8. Safety 3 — Protective Devices and Certification Marks

These are the items you can verify on a product page, and whose absence is a sound reason to walk away.

  • Tip over cutoff — power is cut when the unit tilts or falls. Tilt it gently when it arrives and confirm it actually works. Units that carry the claim but do not respond exist
  • Overheat cutoff — power is cut when intake or exhaust is blocked or internal temperature passes a threshold, usually via a thermal fuse or thermostat
  • Guard grille and timer — a structure that prevents direct contact with the element is effectively mandatory with children or pets around. A timer is the last device that saves you when you forget
  • Power cord — gauge, length, and whether it reaches a wall outlet without an extension. A short cord that forces an extension lead is itself the setup for an incident
  • KC mark and certification number — under Korea Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act, electrical products are managed in three tiers by risk level: safety certification, safety confirmation, and supplier declaration of conformity. Electric heaters fall under this regime

The product label should carry the model name, rated voltage, rated wattage, manufacturer or importer, and certification marking together. If none of that appears in the photos and the seller cannot produce it, the decision is made. Overseas direct purchases and marketplace bargain listings sometimes lack this marking entirely, and a heater is the wrong category in which to accept that gap, because the consequences of a failure are heavy. The Korean Product Safety Information Centre run by the National Institute of Technology and Standards lets you look up certification records and recall history in one place. Outside Korea the equivalent marks are the ones your own market requires, and the principle is identical: no marking, no purchase.

9. Fuel Burning Heaters Are a Different Category — Carbon Monoxide

Kerosene and gas heaters are not the same class of object as electric ones. They burn fuel to make heat, which consumes oxygen and produces exhaust. The carbon monoxide in that exhaust has neither colour nor smell, so a person cannot detect it, and by the time symptoms begin they may already be unable to move themselves out. They must not be used in enclosed spaces such as tents and vehicles. This article does not explain how to use one inside an enclosed space, because there is no such method.

  • Indoors, follow the ventilation conditions the manufacturer sets. The manual specifies how many minutes of open window per hour, and that is an operating condition rather than a suggestion
  • Inspect boiler and heater flues before winter. Korea National Fire Agency lists gaps in flue joints among the main causes of indoor carbon monoxide poisoning. Look for separated or corroded sections
  • Never burn coal or wood inside a vehicle or tent. Even with butane heating products, follow the instruction manual and ventilate frequently. This is the fire agency prevention guidance verbatim
  • Install a carbon monoxide alarm. The gas is undetectable by human senses, so a device is the only warning available. Placing one where people sleep is the placement that matters
  • Know the symptoms. Headache, dizziness, and nausea come first, progressing to vomiting, difficulty breathing, and general weakness. Several people showing the same symptoms at the same time is a strong signal
  • If you suspect it, open windows, leave the space, and then call emergency services. Staying inside to work out the cause is the most dangerous choice available

Labels reading smokeless or low smoke do not mean carbon monoxide is absent. Less visible smoke is a different statement from no exhaust gas, and the invisibility arguably makes it worse by encouraging people to skip ventilation.

10. The Order to Check Things In

There is a sequence. Pick the type first and you will keep having to backtrack.

  • Fix the place and the hours. Where it sits and how many hours a day it runs. The decision table in section 2 only works once this is settled
  • Look up last winter monthly consumption on your invoice. Whether you sit below 200kWh or near 400kWh changes the cost of the identical appliance by two or three times
  • Decide the wattage you need. A footwell unit does not need many watts; a whole room will not warm on a small one. This is where the monthly cost is effectively settled
  • Choose the type within that wattage band. Narrow by whichever of noise, dryness, warm up time, and surface temperature actually bothers you in that spot
  • Filter candidates on safety and labelling. Tip over cutoff, overheat cutoff, guard grille, timer, a stated minimum clearance in the manual, plus rated wattage, manufacturer, and certification marking. If the photos do not show it, ask the seller; if it does not appear, drop the candidate

One more thing remains at installation: whether the outlet at that spot is free and unshared. If the location is good but the layout forces a power strip, change the location rather than the appliance.

11. Frequently Asked Questions

How much cheaper is a fan heater than an oil radiator to run?

If the rated wattage matches, the energy drawn per hour matches. A 1,000W unit pulls 1kWh per hour regardless of type. Bills differ because of run time and thermostat duty cycle. Oil radiators warm up slowly, so people leave them on; radiant heaters get switched on in short bursts. To cut the bill, look at watts and actual hours rather than the category name.

How do I check a claim that a heater saves electricity?

The rated wattage on the label settles most of it. A resistance heater turns essentially all its input into heat, so there is little headroom to produce more from the same watts. Advertised savings usually trace to one of three things: the comparison unit was rated higher, a thermostat cycled off often enough to lower the average, or the unit heats a small area so it runs less. A bill genuinely halves when you switch to a heat pump.

Can I plug an electric heater into a power strip?

It is not advisable. Heaters sit at the high end of household draw and easily exceed the rating of a strip or extension lead. Wiring pushed past its rating heats at the contacts and along the cord, and that heat rarely shows until the insulation is already damaged. Use a wall outlet on its own, and if the plug or outlet feels hot during use, stop and have the wiring checked.

Is it safe to leave an electric heater on while sleeping?

It is not advisable. While asleep you cannot notice bedding covering the element or the unit tipping, and low temperature burns occur at temperatures that never feel hot, which makes sleep the worst exposure condition. If you use one anyway, set a timer for automatic shutoff and keep generous distance from the element. Be stricter in rooms with children or older adults.

Can I use a kerosene or gas heater indoors?

A fuel burning heater is a different category from an electric one. It consumes oxygen and produces exhaust, and the carbon monoxide in it has no colour and no smell. Indoor use requires following the ventilation conditions the manufacturer specifies, and enclosed spaces such as tents and vehicles are off limits. Korea National Fire Agency guidance is to never burn coal or wood inside a vehicle or tent, to follow the instructions and ventilate frequently with butane heating products, and to install a carbon monoxide alarm.
