Correcting a Wrong Entry in the Korean Property Register - What Is Wrong and Whose Mistake Decide the Route

Description errors and rights errors, correction by the registrar and by the party, and the consent of interested parties that you cannot get around alone

2026-08-01 Life/Legal

Introduction: saying it can be fixed is not a procedure

People usually discover that something on their property register is off at an awkward moment. While arranging a loan, while preparing to sell, while sorting out a parent's estate. The area does not match what you remember, one character of a name is different, the ownership share looks wrong, or a mortgage repaid years ago is still sitting there.

The most common reaction is to say that it can simply be fixed. That is only half true. There is more than one route to correcting an entry in a Korean property register, and which route applies is not something you choose. The facts choose it. Three questions split those facts apart.

  • What is wrong. Is it a matter of description, such as area or lot number, or a matter of a right, such as owner or share? These two share the label of a register error and nothing else
  • Whose mistake was it. Was it the registrar's own fault, or an error in the information supplied with the application? The answer decides who has to move
  • Is there a third party. Does the register record any right holder other than you? If it does, this is not a decision you get to make alone

This article maps those three forks. It does not tell you which procedure to pursue. It sets out what the options are and what each of them demands, because filling in a form before you can say what you are asking for is the most common way to waste a month.

What this article is. This is a general explanation written for information and is not legal advice. It describes Korean law only and does not cover land registration in any other country. Outcomes in register matters shift sharply with the particular property and the state of the register. For your own case, consult a Korean attorney or a certified judicial scrivener. No firm is recommended here, and no particular procedure or litigation is urged.

Statute and date of verification. The provisions cited below come from the Real Estate Registration Act of the Republic of Korea (Act No. 20435, partially amended 20 September 2024, in force from 31 January 2025), verified on the Korean Law Information Center on 1 August 2026. Statutes can be amended at any time, so the current text should be checked again before you act.

1. Two kinds of error - description and rights

A Korean certified register extract falls into three blocks. The description section records what the property is. Section A records matters concerning ownership. Section B records rights other than ownership. Which block the error sits in is the first fork.

1.1 Errors in the description

The description section carries area, lot number, land category, structure, floor count and the name of the building. What marks these items out is that a separate source record exists outside the register. For land it is the land ledger, for buildings the building ledger.

So the first move on discovering a description error is not to correct the register but to compare it against the ledger. Three situations come out of that comparison.

  • The ledger is right and only the register differs. This becomes a question of aligning the register with the source record
  • The ledger is wrong. Here, asking to fix only the register leaves nothing to support the filing. Correcting the ledger at the authority in charge has to come first
  • Both are right and reality differs. An unpermitted extension is the classic case, and it pulls in procedures that sit outside the register question altogether

1.2 Errors in a right

Sections A and B carry the owner's name and resident registration number, the address, the ownership share, and the maximum secured amount and holder of a mortgage. Here there is no ledger handing you the right answer. What stands behind these entries are contracts, succession papers and judgments, which differ case by case.

And there is one decisive further difference. Changing how a right is recorded actually changes the extent of somebody's right. Adjust an ownership share and another co-owner's portion shrinks or grows. Touch a mortgage entry and the rank and reach of a security interest move. Which is why, in this territory, the consent of interested parties covered in section 4 comes attached almost every time.

To put it plainly: a description error is close to aligning the register with a source record, while an error in a right changes who holds what. The second is far heavier, and so is its procedure.

2. Whose mistake - where ex officio and applicant filed corrections part

The second fork is whose fault put the wrong text there. On this point the statute marks out one branch clearly.

Article 32 paragraph 2 of the Real Estate Registration Act provides that where the registrar finds that an error or omission in a registration was caused by the registrar's own fault, the registrar shall correct that registration ex officio without delay, provided that where a third party with a registered interest exists, the consent of that third party is required. (Act No. 20435, in force from 31 January 2025. Verified on the Korean Law Information Center on 1 August 2026. Later amendment is possible.)

Three things are worth reading out of that provision.

  • If the fault is the registrar's, the registrar corrects it. The structure is not one where nothing happens until a party files. The statute places a duty on the registrar to put it right without delay
  • Discovery is the trigger. The provision speaks of the registrar finding the error. In practice, then, what matters is bringing the error to the registry office and having it examined
  • A third party means consent is needed even for an ex officio correction. This may be the single most important sentence here. Even where the registrar's fault is plain, nothing proceeds without the consent of another right holder on the register who could suffer loss

The other side, where the information supplied by the applicant was itself wrong, has a different character. The registrar recorded what was filed, so this cannot be called the registrar's fault, and the provision above is not the one operating. Here the party moves toward filing a registration that puts it right. However, the statutory basis, requirements and attachments for that filing were not verified in the original text for this article. They are listed as unverified in section 9, so check the current text of the Act and its Rules on the Korean Law Information Center directly.

One common misreading deserves flagging. Assuming that the registry made the mistake and will therefore sort it out is not a safe posture. The provision speaks of errors the registrar finds, and an error nobody has found simply stays there. Meanwhile, if a new right holder enters the register, the side that did nothing wrong now has one more person whose consent is needed.

3. Correction and change - wrong from the start versus altered later

The third fork looks like terminology and is really about facts. Since when has it been wrong?

  • Correction. Putting right something that was already wrong when the registration was completed. There was an error, or something was left out
  • Change. Reflecting a fact that was correct at the time and altered afterwards. The registration was never wrong

Take an example. If the shares were six to four from the outset but the register says seven to three, that is correction territory. If the building was extended after registration and the area moved, that is change territory. Both look identical from outside, as the register disagreeing with reality, yet the registration you apply for differs and so do the documents required.

On the change side, some items carry a filing deadline. The one whose text was verified this time concerns buildings.

Article 41 paragraph 1 requires that, where a building is divided, partitioned or merged, or where a matter registered under article 40 changes, the registered owner of that building apply for the registration within one month from the fact. (Verified on the Korean Law Information Center on 1 August 2026.)

There is a corresponding provision for land in the same Act, but its text and the applicable period were not verified for this article. Check the filing period for a change of land description on the Korean Law Information Center directly.

Confuse the terms and the filing comes back over the counter. Which is why writing one sentence saying whether this was wrong from the start or changed later, before choosing any form, is in practice the fastest route.

4. Consent of a third party with a registered interest

This section is the heart of the article, because plenty of cases clear the first three forks and stop here.

Why a third party matters. A property register is not a private ledger describing your asset. It records the rights of several people around that property and publicises their order and extent. Fix one entry and the position of somebody recorded beside it can shift. So the statute makes that person's consent a requirement at several points.

4.1 Three points confirmed in the statute

  • Ex officio correction by the registrar, article 32 paragraph 2. Even when correcting an error or omission caused by the registrar's own fault, consent is required where a third party with a registered interest exists
  • Cancellation of a registration, article 57 paragraph 1. Where cancellation of a registration is applied for and a third party with a registered interest in that cancellation exists, that party's consent is required
  • Restoration of a cancelled registration, article 59. For restoring a cancelled entry as well, where a third party with a registered interest exists, that party's consent, or a certified copy of a judgment that can be asserted against them, is called for

That three provisions repeat the same requirement is itself the message. At almost every point where the register is touched, the agreement of the person recorded beside you is the gate.

4.2 Who counts as such a third party

A third party with a registered interest means a right holder on the register who risks suffering loss if the registration in question is allowed. Both halves of that matter.

  • They must be a right holder on the register. Their name appears in the register itself. Someone with a practical stake who does not appear on the register is a different thing
  • There must be a risk of loss. Appearing on the register is not enough on its own. It means someone whose position could worsen if the registration goes through
  • So whether a given person actually falls inside the concept has to be argued against the concrete state of the register. That judgment is not something a general explanation can substitute for, and it calls for professional review

4.3 What this means in practice

  • Once you hold the extract, write down every person other than you recorded in sections A and B. Mortgagees, jeonse right holders, provisional attachment creditors and other co-owners are all candidates
  • If that list is empty, the situation is simpler. If names are on it, your relationship with those people sets the pace of the whole procedure
  • Failing to obtain consent does not automatically mean a dead end. As article 59 mentions a certified copy of a judgment standing in place of consent, the statute contemplates other routes. What those routes are and what they require differs by case and lies beyond this article
  • This article does not urge any procedure. All that can be said is that arriving at a point where consent is in play is the point at which professional advice is needed

5. Cancellation and restoration - entries erased unlawfully

A register error usually brings to mind something written wrongly, but an entry that ought to be there and has vanished is also an error. And that kind is far harder to spot, because absence does not catch the eye.

For those situations there is restoration of a cancelled registration. It is described as a registration carried out, where an entry has been unlawfully cancelled in whole or in part, for the purpose of restoring that entry so that it holds the same effect as if the cancellation had never occurred.

The phrase about the same effect as if the cancellation had never occurred is the key. The aim is not merely to register it afresh from today but to reach back to the earlier point in time. Which inevitably touches other right holders who entered the register in the meantime, and so the consent requirement from the previous section attaches here as well.

  • The requirement is an unlawful cancellation. It is not a mechanism for reviving an entry cancelled through a proper procedure because someone changed their mind
  • The consent of a third party with a registered interest, or a certified copy of a judgment assertable against them, is what article 59 points to
  • The remaining requirements and procedure were not verified here. Which cancellations count as unlawful, and who may apply, are questions to be taken up against the statute and the facts together
  • How to find it matters more in practice. A cancelled entry may not show on an ordinary extract. As section 6 shows, you have to obtain it including cancelled matters for the past history to appear

The mirror image also exists: an entry that ought to have been erased and is still there. A mortgage discharged long ago and never removed is the standard case. That puts the cancellation route in issue, and once again what decides the shape of it is whether there is a third party with a registered interest in that cancellation under article 57 paragraph 1.

6. What to check and where - documents and access routes

What you need before moving is an accurate copy of what the register actually says now. Not memory, not an old document, but today's entries.

6.1 One habit when ordering the extract

What the extract shows depends on the option chosen. When dealing with an error, ordering it including cancelled matters is the baseline. Only then do you see, alongside what currently stands, entries that once existed and were erased. The cancellation problems in section 5 may be entirely invisible without it.

7. What it costs to leave an error alone

The awkward thing about register errors is that nothing happens day to day. Live in the place and it never shows. The trouble surfaces only at the moment you try to move the property, and that moment usually comes with a fixed schedule attached.

  • Sale. A description mismatch surfacing just before closing pushes the schedule or reopens the contract terms. The buyer's financing timetable shakes with it
  • Lending. A bank may suspend review or demand further documents over a mismatch between the register and the ledger
  • Succession. Once a generation passes, the number of parties grows, which also means the number of people whose consent may be needed
  • Loss of evidence. The documents proving what was originally correct, and the memories of the people involved, scatter with time
  • Arrival of third parties. This is the most concrete risk. If a new right holder enters the register meanwhile, the set of people whose consent section 4 requires grows by one
  • Shift in bargaining position. Discovering it just before an urgent transaction puts you under time pressure, and asking for cooperation from that position is a weak place to stand

A note on time limits. How limitation periods or exclusion periods apply to register problems is not asserted in this article. Outcomes turn on the nature of the right and the facts, and the relevant provisions and criteria were not verified here. If timing could be an issue in your situation, treat that fact itself as the signal that professional advice is needed.

Separately from limitation questions, though, the risk that third parties multiply is proportional to time, and that follows directly from the structure of the provisions above. That is why checking as soon as you notice matters.

8. The order to work through on discovering an error

Turned into a sequence, the material above looks like this. Each step is a gate deciding whether you move to the next one.

  1. Capture the current state. Order the extract including cancelled matters. Keep the issued document, not a screenshot
  2. Separate description from rights. Write down first whether this sits in the description section or in sections A and B
  3. For a description issue, compare against the ledger. Establish which of the register and the ledger is wrong. If it is the ledger, that becomes the prior task
  4. Write down since when it has been wrong. From the start, or because a fact changed later. Correction and change part here
  5. Form a view on whose mistake it was. Does it look like the registrar's fault, or an error in the information filed
  6. List every other right holder recorded on the register. Whether that list is empty decides how heavy the procedure will be
  7. Gather the underlying documents. Contracts, succession papers, judgments, whatever proves what is correct
  8. Ask the competent registry office about the filing type and attachments. Check fees and processing time at this stage too
  9. Bring in professional advice at the point consent is involved. Do not decide alone while that list is not empty
  10. If a transaction is under way, discuss the schedule first. Seeking consent while the clock runs is the worst position to seek it from

8.1 What not to do

  • Filling in a form before separating the type. It comes back over the counter
  • Trying to fix only the register when the ledger is the thing that is wrong. The filing has nothing behind it
  • Skipping the consent question while another right holder sits on the register
  • Filing as a correction something that was right and later changed
  • Believing amounts and timelines found online without verifying them
  • Deferring it to the next transaction after finding it. A third party can arrive in the interval
  • Deciding an individual case on this article alone. This is a map of the forks, not an answer

Frequently asked questions

The area on the register differs from reality. Can it be fixed right away?

There is an order to follow. Items in the description section, such as area, lot number and structure, have a source record in the land or building ledger. So the first step is to compare the register against the ledger and see which side is wrong. If the ledger is wrong, correcting it at the authority in charge has to come first, since asking to fix only the register leaves the filing without supporting material. The exact filing type and attachments differ case by case, so confirm with the competent registry office and take professional advice where the answer is not obvious.

If the registrar made the mistake, do I still have to file?

Article 32 paragraph 2 provides that where the registrar finds an error or omission caused by the registrar's own fault, the registrar shall correct it ex officio without delay. Under the proviso to the same paragraph, though, consent is required where a third party with a registered interest exists. And since the provision speaks of errors the registrar finds, the practical starting point is bringing the error to the registry office and having it examined.

What is the difference between a correction registration and a change registration?

The moment of error differs. A correction fixes something wrong from the very beginning. A change reflects a fact that was correct and altered afterwards. Getting the distinction wrong sends the filing straight back. For reference, where a building is divided, partitioned or merged, or a matter registered under article 40 changes, article 41 paragraph 1 requires the registered owner of the building to apply within one month from the fact.

If a mortgagee is on the register, does that block any correction?

It cannot be said flatly that nothing can be done, but it is clear that you cannot decide alone. The Act requires the consent of a third party with a registered interest for ex officio correction by the registrar (article 32 paragraph 2), for cancellation of a registration (article 57 paragraph 1) and for restoring a cancelled registration (article 59). The third party here means a right holder on the register who risks suffering loss if the registration is allowed. What remains open when consent cannot be obtained varies greatly by case, so professional advice is needed.

Can a registration that was wrongly cancelled be brought back?

A procedure for restoring an unlawfully cancelled entry does exist. It is described as a registration whose purpose is to restore an unlawfully cancelled entry so that it holds the same effect as if the cancellation had never occurred. But since article 59 points to the consent of a third party with a registered interest, or a certified copy of a judgment assertable against them, restoration is likewise not a matter you settle by yourself. The detailed requirements were not fully verified here, so check the current text and take advice on your own case.

## 0. Three questions to answer before filling in any form

1. What is wrong — the description (area, lot number, land category, structure) or a right (owner, share, mortgage)?
2. Whose mistake was it — the registrar's, or an error in the information the applicant supplied?
3. Is there a third party — is any other right holder recorded who could be harmed?

Do not fill in a form before these three answers exist. Picking the wrong type wastes the whole filing.

## 1. Two kinds of error, side by side

| Aspect | Error in the description | Error in a right |
|---|---|---|
| Typical examples | Area, lot number, land category, structure, floor count, building name | Owner name, resident registration number, address, ownership share, maximum secured amount of a mortgage |
| Where it appears | The description section of the certified register extract | Section A (ownership) and Section B (rights other than ownership) |
| Is there a separate source record | Yes. The land ledger or building ledger acts as the source record | Usually not. Contracts, judgments and succession papers are the basis |
| Scope of harm | Misperception of area or location | The ownership relation or the priority of security itself is shaken |
| Third party involvement | Relatively limited | Mortgagees, provisional attachment creditors and others are often involved |
| Practical difficulty | Comparatively simple once the ledger is corrected first | Varies widely with consent, cooperation and whether litigation is involved |

The point: the two share the label of a register error and nothing else. A description error is close to reconciling the register with a source ledger. An error in a right changes who holds what.

## 3. Which registration are you actually asking for

| Term | When it applies | One line definition |

## 4. Consent of interested parties — only what was verified

- Article 32 paragraph 2: in an ex officio correction by the registrar, consent is required if a third party with a registered interest exists
- Article 57 paragraph 1: where cancellation of a registration is applied for and a third party with a registered interest in that cancellation exists, that party's consent is required
- Article 59: for restoration of a cancelled registration, consent of a third party with a registered interest is likewise required, or a certified copy of a judgment that can be asserted against that party
- A third party with a registered interest means a right holder on the register who risks suffering loss if the registration in question is allowed

In short, where the register records a right holder other than you, these provisions do not open a path to fixing things alone. What options remain when consent cannot be obtained varies by case and calls for professional advice.
