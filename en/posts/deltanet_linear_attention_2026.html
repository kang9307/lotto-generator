What Linear Attention Buys and What It Gives Up

Compressing the past into a fixed-size state makes cost linear, but exact recall gets expensive

2026-07-30 Tech & IT

Introduction: linear does not mean cheaper, it means buying something else

The name linear attention invites a misreading. People hearing it for the first time usually take it as a cheaper way to do the same thing. What actually happens is different. It is not the same thing made cheaper. It is a different thing bought instead. Miss that distinction and every judgement downstream goes wrong.

The starting point is a lineage walkthrough. A recent article traced the path from softmax attention to linear attention, then through DeltaNet and Gated DeltaNet to Kimi Delta Attention, one step at a time. It is a good explainer, and re-summarising that lineage is not the purpose here.

This piece answers two questions. First, what exactly does linear attention make cheaper. Second, what does it give up in exchange. A third question follows for practitioners: is the kind of work I do able to absorb that price.

Let me commit to the direction of the conclusion up front. What full attention buys with all that money is a right of total re-inspection - the right to look at any token again, exactly as it was. Linear attention sells that right and buys a single summary card. The card can be revised forever, but it never grows. So work that needs only the gist survives almost intact, and work that must lift the original wording breaks first. What the delta rule family did was push that wall back, not remove it.

Identifying the techniques succeeded. All three methods the source article covers were located along with their papers, and this article uses only what is verifiable in those abstracts. No equations were transcribed. Concepts are explained in words, with very short pseudocode only where it earns its place. Figures whose measurement conditions are absent are labelled as such and never quoted bare. Anything that could not be confirmed is left standing in the text as unconfirmed. The cache-optimisation axis was already covered in KV cache sharing and attention compression, so it is not repeated here.

2. What full attention buys - a right of total re-inspection

Start with the cost. In a sequence of length T, the number of query-key pairs grows on the order of T squared. During training that is compute directly; during generation it means every new token has to inspect the entire history again. Which is why the cache holding that history grows in proportion to length.

Here a common misreading appears. The cost gets read as waste. But it is not waste, it is a purchase. Of what?

  • Total re-inspection - while generating token thirty thousand, the key and value of token seventeen can be read as they originally were. Not a compressed summary. The original.
  • Selectivity - softmax concentrates weight on the relevant positions. What to look at can be re-chosen at every step.
  • Normalisation - the weights sum to a fixed quantity, so the scale of contributions holds up as history lengthens.
  • Deferred querying - you do not have to know what will be asked at the time the input goes in. The past can be searched after the question is fixed. This property is the most valuable in practice and the easiest to lose.

That last item is the one this article keeps returning to. The moment you fold into a fixed-size state, what gets kept is decided before the question is seen. The model has to build its summary without knowing what will be asked next. That this is fundamentally hard is what explains the entire lineage.

3. The linearisation idea - fold into a recurrence and pin the state

The idea itself is startlingly simple. Replace softmax with a dot product of kernel feature maps and the order of multiplication becomes free to change. The linear transformer abstract puts it as using the associativity property of matrix products to reduce complexity from quadratic to linear in the sequence length. It adds that this formulation permits an iterative implementation and reveals the relationship to recurrent neural networks.

In words: instead of storing every past key and value, keep laying them into one matrix. Sweep the tokens once, updating a state of constant size at each step. When the sweep ends, the past sits inside that single state. Its size is the same at the end as at the beginning.

# Conceptual pseudocode - comparing only what each approach carries
# Not an implementation. Normalisation, multiple heads and positions omitted

## Full attention: carries all of the past
cache = []                            # grows in proportion to length
for t in 1..T:
    cache.append( (key[t], value[t]) )
    out[t] = weighted_sum(query[t], cache)   # re-reads the whole cache each step
# state size = proportional to length      per-token cost = length read so far

## Linear attention: carries one fixed-size state
S = zeros(d_value, d_key)             # size is independent of T
for t in 1..T:
    S = update(S, key[t], value[t])   # keeps writing over the same place
    out[t] = read(S, query[t])        # never re-reads a cache
# state size = fixed                  per-token cost = independent of length

# ★ In the loop below there is no way to look at key[1] through key[t-1] again.
#   Only what survives in S can be seen. That is the price paid for the cost cut.
#   Written as a condition it becomes this.
#     if needed_fact still lives in S       -> correct answer
#     if needed_fact was written over       -> plausible wrong answer

What is gained is clear. Per-token cost during generation stops depending on history length. The cache does not grow, so memory is not dragged along by length either. At long contexts this difference sometimes stops being a matter of degree and becomes a matter of possible versus impossible.

But something disappears alongside it. In the primary source's phrasing, linearisation gained its efficiency by discarding softmax's normalisation and selectivity. And something more fundamental sits in the last comment of the pseudocode above. There is no mechanism to look at the past again at all. Only what remains in the state can be read.

How to read the figure 4000x

The linear transformer abstract states that its models are up to 4000x faster on autoregressive prediction of very long sequences. An arresting number, and widely quoted. Which is exactly why the conditions need checking.

  • Conditions that are stated - the task type autoregressive prediction, the qualitative phrase very long sequences, and the hedge up to.
  • Conditions absent from the abstract - model size, the actual sequence length, hardware, batch size, and the configuration of the baseline implementation. Five empty columns.
  • So this article uses the figure as evidence of direction only. It reads as the gap widens sharply for sequential generation over very long sequences, and not as we will see 4000x.
  • The same rule applies to Mamba's 5x throughput. The abstract states that multiple in the context of fast inference, but batch size and hardware are not in the abstract.

This habit matters because gains in the linear family are unusually sensitive to conditions. The size of the gain depends on sequence length. At short contexts the gap is nearly nothing; it widens as context grows. A speed claim with no length attached carries especially little information in this family.

4. What the DeltaNet family fixed - overwriting and forgetting

Now to the substance of the lineage. And this lineage reads best as a record of attacking one problem three different ways. The problem is single: how do you use a fixed-size state frugally.

a. Addition is not assignment

The primary source frames it precisely: addition is not assignment. Meaning what? Pure linear attention adds new information into the state. But if two keys are not perfectly orthogonal, the added value mixes with what was there. Write twice to the same place and it is not overwritten, it is superimposed. The article describes writing an already-written value as leaving the state producing twice that value.

Put in memory terms: what we wanted was assignment, and what happened was accumulation. Every update leaves traces of the previous one, so the state gradually gets dirtier. This is the write interference problem.

b. The delta rule - write only the error

DeltaNet's idea answers exactly that. Do not write the whole value; write only the difference from what the current state would already produce. Query the state with that key, compute the error between what came back and what should be there, and apply only that much. If it is already stored well, almost nothing is written; if it is off, only the gap is corrected.

What the abstract verifiably states is this. Linear attention transformers and state-space models have been suggested as linear-time alternatives but still underperform transformers especially on tasks that require in-context retrieval; more expressive variants replacing the additive update with the delta rule have been found to be more effective at associative recall; yet existing algorithms for training such models do not parallelise over sequence length and are therefore inefficient on modern hardware. The paper's contribution is solving that parallelisation with a hardware-efficient algorithm.

That last clause matters practically. A good idea that will not parallelise during training cannot be scaled. It is why every step of this lineage ships an algorithm and kernels alongside the architecture. Architectural ideas and hardware mapping do not separate.

c. Gating - add wholesale erasure to targeted repair

Gated DeltaNet adds forgetting. Its abstract is clear on the reasoning. Recent work explored two distinct mechanisms, gating for adaptive memory control and the delta update rule for precise memory modifications, and the authors observe that these are complementary: gating enables rapid memory erasure while the delta rule facilitates targeted updates.

In words: the delta rule is strong at revising a specific entry but weak at clearing out a whole block that has simply gone stale. The gate handles that by controlling how much to retain. Think of it as the ability to let go of a previous topic quickly when the topic changes.

There are no concrete figures in the abstract. It states only that the architecture consistently surpasses existing models like Mamba2 and DeltaNet across benchmarks including language modelling, common-sense reasoning, in-context retrieval, length extrapolation and long-context understanding. Scores, model sizes and training token counts are not verifiable at abstract level. So this article quotes the direction of the advantage and not its magnitude.

d. Per-channel decay - slice the gate finer

Kimi Delta Attention makes that gate finer-grained. In the primary source's account, what used to be a single value governing retention for the whole state becomes a different rate per dimension. Then one channel can be cleared while another is retained.

What the abstract verifiably states: KDA is an expressive linear attention module that extends Gated DeltaNet with a finer-grained gating mechanism, whose purpose is more effective use of limited finite-state RNN memory. Its bespoke chunkwise algorithm attains hardware efficiency through a specialised variant of Diagonal-Plus-Low-Rank transition matrices, substantially reducing computation compared to the general formulation while staying more consistent with the classical delta rule.

The word to notice is limited. The paper itself argues efficiency within the premise that memory is limited. That makes something clear about the whole lineage: it is a competition to use the container well, not to enlarge it.

5. The wall that stays - exact recall is bounded by state size

This section is the centre of the article. The aim is to establish that the preceding improvements are impressive but of a fixed kind.

The most direct evidence is the Repeat After Me paper. Its abstract groups models using a fixed-size latent state that does not depend on sequence length under the name generalized state space models, and states that such models are promising for inference-time efficiency but limited compared to transformers on tasks requiring copying from the input context. It then reports a theoretical analysis of string copying and states that it proves a two layer transformer can copy strings of exponential length while these models are fundamentally limited by their fixed-size latent state.

The word fundamentally is what counts. This is not because the implementation is immature nor because training was insufficient. It is the statement that a container of fixed size holds a fixed amount. The abstract goes on to report that when pretrained large language models were evaluated, transformer models dramatically outperformed state space models at copying and retrieving information from context, and concludes that taken together the results suggest a fundamental gap between the two families on tasks of practical interest.

What is striking is that the same diagnosis appears independently in several places.

  • The DeltaNet paper writes in its first sentences that linear-time families still fall short of transformers especially on tasks that require in-context retrieval. A paper proposing an improvement takes that weakness as its starting point.
  • The Mamba paper notes that many subquadratic-time architectures have not performed as well as attention on important modalities such as language, and identifies the key weakness as an inability to perform content-based reasoning. Its fix is selectivity, letting state-space parameters be functions of the input.
  • The Gated DeltaNet paper opens by saying that linear transformers' performance in retrieval and long-context tasks has been limited.
  • The Kimi Linear paper states the purpose of its improvement as more effective use of limited finite-state memory. It does not dispute that the memory is limited.

Four papers point at the same wall from different angles. And each contribution pushes that wall back. The delta rule keeps the same container less dirty. Gating clears out what is no longer needed, fast. Per-channel decay divides what to clear from what to keep more finely. All of them are ways to use the same container better, and not one of them enlarges it.

An analogy - the filing cabinet and the summary card

Full attention is like carrying the entire filing cabinet of originals. Heavy, space-hungry, but later any document can be pulled out in its original form.

Linear attention is one summary card, revised over and over. Light and fast. The delta rule is the technique for erasing and correcting an entry already written on the card, gating is the technique for wiping stale entries wholesale, and per-channel decay is the technique for wiping different entries at different rates. The better the technique, the richer the card. Yet the card never once got bigger.

So ask about something not written on the card and there is no answer. Worse, the model does not say there is no answer. It assembles a plausible one from similar entries on the card. In practice that is the real danger. The failure does not stay silent, it disguises itself.

6. Hybrids are not a compromise, they are the conclusion of this trade

The word hybrid often sounds like splitting the difference because nobody could decide. Not here. It is what falls out when you actually do the arithmetic of the previous section. And the evidence sits directly in the abstracts.

  • The DeltaNet paper states that two hybrid models were tried: one interleaving sliding-window attention layers every other layer, the other adding two global attention layers, and that these hybrids outperformed strong transformer baselines.
  • The Gated DeltaNet paper states that hybrid architectures combining its layers with sliding window attention or Mamba2 layers achieved both improved training efficiency and superior task performance.
  • The Kimi Linear paper states that pretraining was based on a layerwise hybrid of KDA and Multi-Head Latent Attention, and claims that a hybrid linear attention architecture outperformed full attention under fair comparisons for the first time.

The number to read here is two. That only two global attention layers, rather than all of them, sufficed to beat a strong baseline says something.

  • Exact recall is not needed in every layer. A handful of layers recovers a large share of it.
  • Most of the cost is carried by the linear layers. Only a minority of layers grow a cache, so growth of cost with length is heavily suppressed.
  • So a hybrid is not recall and cost split in half, but the price paid only where recall is needed. That reading is far more accurate.
  • Kimi Linear's reported KV cache reduction of up to 75% comes from exactly this structure. It means roughly a quarter of full attention, and the fact that it is not zero must be read too. The remaining quarter is the layers supplying recall.

Which fixes the values to ask about whenever the word hybrid appears. These three effectively set the ceiling on recall performance.

  1. How many global attention layers. Two versus eight is an entirely different story.
  2. How they are spaced. Clustered at the front, spread evenly, or placed at the back.
  3. How large the window is in layers that use one. A sliding window is not global. It cannot see outside the window.

How to find and read those values inside a technical report was covered separately, from the report-reading angle, in how to read an LLM technical report. This article concentrates on why those values set the ceiling on recall. If the transformer basics themselves are the gap, the relevant stage of the machine learning self-study roadmap is the faster route.

7. How to read a 1M context claim

The spread of linear families arrived alongside inflation in advertised context lengths. Which makes reading those numbers a practical skill.

First narrow what the number means. A context length means the input fits. It does not mean accuracy holds at that length. It is a capacity figure, not a performance figure.

The best evidence for that distinction is the RULER paper. Summarising its abstract: the widely adopted test of retrieving one planted piece of information from long distractor text is indicative of only a superficial form of long-context understanding. So the authors built a synthetic benchmark with configurable sequence length and task complexity, varied the types and quantities of planted items, added new categories for multi-hop tracing and aggregation, and evaluated 17 models across 13 tasks.

The findings are the crux. Despite nearly perfect accuracy on the plain retrieval test, almost all models exhibited large performance drops as context length increased, and while all of them claimed context sizes of 32K or greater, only half could maintain satisfactory performance at 32K. A model supporting 200K also showed large room for improvement as input length and task complexity rose.

These findings must not be transplanted onto the linear family wholesale. Nothing in the abstract restricts RULER's evaluation set to a particular architecture family, and no such split was verifiable within what this article checked. So this is not a defect of linear models, it is a general long-context problem. That said, a fixed-size state takes that problem harder in principle - that part follows from the evidence in section 5. Keeping the two facts unmixed is the accurate move.

The same rule applies to Kimi Linear's figures. Up to 6 times decoding throughput at 1M context is a throughput claim. It is not a claim that accuracy holds at that length. The two axes must be read apart.

For a multiple with an empty conditions column, reading direction only and never carrying it across as an absolute is the sole safe treatment.

8. A procedure for telling whether your workload is recall dependent

Here the perspective shifts. Up to now it was architecture; from here it is what I can actually do.

Bluntly, the choice is usually not mine. Most practitioners do not pick an attention mechanism. They pick a model, or in truth they use the model the company contracted for. And when the provider switches architecture, it changes without notice. Version numbers move, but what changed inside requires opening a report, and often there is no report.

That narrows the practical action to one thing. Determine whether your workload is recall dependent, and put a matching regression gate in place ahead of time. Then when the architecture changes, you find out first.

Step 1 - score the recall dependence

# Determining whether a workload is recall dependent - conceptual procedure
score = 0

if output must contain source strings verbatim:        score += 3
if the answer is scattered across two or more places:  score += 2
if several distractors look like the answer:           score += 2
if a wrong answer still looks plausible:               score += 2
if inputs routinely exceed 8k tokens:                  score += 1

if score >= 5:
    recall dependent
    # Position-sweep gate is mandatory. Check for global attention layers and their count.
    # Force an output format that allows comparison against the source.
elif score >= 3:
    borderline
    # Sample-check the quoted parts by hand. Fix the sampling rate in writing.
else:
    gist oriented
    # Take the cost benefit of the linear family and enjoy it.

# ★ The score is not the point. Not skipping any of the five questions is the point.

Each of the five, unpacked. The order carries meaning - higher is more decisive.

  • Are source strings needed verbatim - clause numbers, amounts, dates, identifiers, function signatures, config keys. If there are values that are unusable when a single character is wrong, the task is recall dependent. No amount of summary quality substitutes.
  • How many places is the answer scattered across - one place means find and stop. Stitching three places together needs retrieval and tracing at once, and difficulty rises steeply. That is why RULER made multi-hop tracing a separate category.
  • Are there several look-alike distractors - the most underrated factor. Contracts with repeating near-identical clauses, codebases full of similarly named functions, tens of thousands of identically formatted log lines. On a summary card those entries blend into each other easily.
  • Is a failure visible - summaries fail plausibly. Quotations get caught by comparison against the source. Invisible failure is the more dangerous kind. Which makes forcing output into a comparable shape a defence in itself.
  • Is the input long - it comes last for a reason. Length is not a standalone indicator. Short inputs can be recall dependent and long ones can be gist oriented. Length is the variable that amplifies the other four.

Step 2 - build a position-sweep gate on your own documents

If the verdict is recall dependent, do not stop at trusting a public benchmark score. Your documents differ in distribution: density of distractors, document structure, patterns of term repetition, all different.

# A recall regression gate built from our own documents - conceptual procedure
# Verified on our data, not on a public benchmark score

for length in [8k, 32k, 128k, 256k, 512k]:
    for position in [front 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, back 95%]:
        doc = build_haystack(our_corpus, length)
        doc = insert_target(doc, position, target_span)
        ans = ask(doc, question_requiring_verbatim_quote)
        ok  = exact_match(ans, target_span)   # verbatim match, not summary match
        record(length, position, ok)

# Write the pass rule down before looking at any number
#   pass : accuracy >= 0.98 at every position
#   warn : 0.90 <= accuracy < 0.98 at any single position
#   fail : accuracy < 0.90 at any single position
#
# ★ Before trusting the gate, run it once with the target span deliberately removed.
#   If that does not raise fail, the gate is measuring nothing at all.
#   Every green result up to that point meant nothing.

Four points in this harness are not negotiable.

  • Sweep the position - place the target span at the front, the middle and the back. In a fixed-size state, earlier information has more opportunity to be written over. Testing only the back never catches that failure.
  • Sweep the length - a score at one length tells you nothing about where the threshold is. What we want is not a score but the token count at which it collapses.
  • Score by verbatim match - grade by semantic similarity and plausible wrong answers pass. To measure recall, the grader has to be a recall grader.
  • Write the pass rule first - set the threshold after seeing results and people invariably set it where they pass. That is not a question of integrity but of cognition.

Step 3 - prepare for the architecture changing

  • Log the model version - without a record of which version produced which score, a regression can be spotted but not attributed.
  • Run the gate on a schedule - provider models change independently of your release cycle. Results can move in a week when you changed nothing.
  • Put retrieval in front for recall-dependent work - rather than making the model swallow the whole document, cut out the relevant span and pass that. This routes around the state-size constraint through pipeline design, and it is usually the cheapest option.
  • Force citation - require the supporting span to be reproduced verbatim alongside the answer, then check mechanically that the span really exists in the source. That single layer turns recall failure from silent into audible.
  • Leave gist work alone - putting a recall gate on classification, routing, sentiment or conversational continuity adds cost and returns nothing. Gates are a resource too.

Conclusion: a trade inherited, not chosen

Start with what this lineage actually demonstrated. Folding into a recurrent form with a fixed-size state makes cost linear in sequence length and per-token generation cost independent of length. The craft of using that state improved substantively across three stages: the delta rule writing only the error, gating clearing what is no longer needed, and refinement clearing at different rates per channel. And a claim has now appeared that a hybrid placing global attention in only some layers outperformed full attention under fair comparison. All of that is verifiable at abstract level.

What could not be confirmed is written down too. The primary source states that two of these are used by the latest Qwen and Kimi model families, but which version carries which configuration on the Qwen side was not verified against a primary Qwen document. The Kimi side has a basis, since the paper itself states the layerwise hybrid construction. Also, the magnitude of Gated DeltaNet's advantage could not be quoted because the abstract carries no figures, and among every multiple cited in this article not one came with batch size and hardware attached. The fact that the original transformer abstract contains no quadratic-cost statement is likewise left standing.

Three things for practitioners to take away.

  1. Linear does not mean cheap, it means buying a summary. Full attention can look at every token again; linear attention only sees the summary. The delta rule and gating are ways to build a much better summary, and not ways to turn a summary back into the original.
  2. Whether your workload is recall dependent is the only thing you actually get to decide. The architecture is given. Verbatim need, scatter of the answer, presence of look-alike distractors, visibility of failure. Settle those four, then install the matching gate.
  3. A context length figure is capacity, not performance. Without a per-length accuracy trend, a position sweep and verbatim scoring, a length claim has not yet said anything. And that verification belongs on your own documents, not on a public benchmark.

One last line. This trade was inherited, not chosen. Very few practitioners sit in the seat where attention mechanisms get decided. But determining which side of the trade your work stands on, and installing something that tells you when that determination was wrong, is available to anyone, and it is effectively the whole job. Cost announces itself through the invoice. Recall announces itself to no one.
