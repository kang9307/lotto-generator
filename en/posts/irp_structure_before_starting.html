Korea's IRP: The Structure to Know Before You Open One

Severance benefits land in the same account you pay into yourself, a deduction is not an exemption, and breaking the account early can cost more than everything it ever gave back

Aug 16, 2026 Finance & Economy

This article is provided for information only and does not recommend enrolling in or purchasing any specific product. Tax benefits and eligibility depend on individual circumstances, and the final confirmation should follow guidance from Korea's National Tax Service and your financial institution.

Every year-end tax settlement season in Korea brings the same line back around: open an IRP, put money in, get a few hundred thousand won back. It is not wrong. It just describes half of what happens inside the account. The other half is when that money returns to your hands, and how it is taxed when it does.

This piece neither urges nor discourages opening one. It looks at how the account is built. Once the structure is clear, the question of whether to open one usually resolves itself. Skip the structure and go by the refund figure alone, and you can end up breaking the account three years later to fund a lease deposit, paying more in tax than the credits ever returned.

The IRP is a Korean statutory scheme, so what follows is written against Korean law. Readers elsewhere will recognise the family resemblance: a 401(k) in the United States and iDeCo in Japan are likewise tax-advantaged accounts that trade liquidity for deferral. The mechanics below are specific to Korea and should not be read across to any other country's rules. The rates, ceilings and article numbers were confirmed on 16 August 2026; tax law changes, so check with the National Tax Service before you actually contribute. A separate piece covers pension savings and IRP from the tax-saving angle, and another covers pension savings funds in detail. This one stays on the structure of the IRP itself.

1. One account holding two kinds of money

Most of the confusion around the IRP starts here. There is one name on the account, but two different kinds of money arrive in it.

  • Severance benefits — the money paid when you leave a job. You do not contribute it; it is transferred in. For tax purposes it belongs to the retirement income family
  • Personal contributions — what you put in yourself, monthly or in a year-end lump. This is the side the tax credit attaches to

The two show up as a single balance, but they are taxed differently on the way out. Money that arrived as severance carries deferred retirement income tax; money you contributed carries the credits you have already claimed. There is therefore no single answer to the question of what IRP tax looks like. It splits four ways, depending on which pot leaves and by which route.

Fix that distinction early and everything below falls into place. The year-end refund story is a story about personal contributions. The story about being told to open an account when you resign is a story about severance. Both simply run inside one person's account at the same time.

2. The account you end up opening when you leave a job

Anyone who knows the IRP only as a tax-refund vehicle is caught off guard at resignation, when a notice arrives telling them to open one in order to receive their severance. That is not a sales push. It is a statutory route.

Articles 17(4) and 19(2) of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act direct severance benefits to an individual retirement pension account designated by the member. Not every case falls under it; the Korea Ministry of Government Legislation's plain-language guidance lists the exceptions.

  • Leaving employment after age 55 and receiving the benefit then
  • A severance amount of 3 million won or less
  • Repayment of a debt secured against the benefit
  • Death of the employee
  • Departure from Korea after leaving employment
  • Cases where another statute requires the benefit to be deducted

The practically important point is that the transfer itself is not a taxable event. Retirement income tax is not withheld when severance moves into the IRP; it is pushed out to the moment of actual withdrawal. That deferred amount is what the law calls deferred retirement income. Taking severance in cash and leaving it in the account are therefore not equivalent choices in tax terms.

The flip side is that once the money is inside, it lives by the account's rules — withdrawal restrictions, the range of products it may hold, and fees alike. Treating an IRP that received severance as a pass-through for money you already consider yours is where the misunderstandings begin.

3. The tax credit: what it applies to, and how much

This is the most common reason people open the account and the part most often misread. The misreadings run two ways: believing you get back what you put in, and believing the ceiling applies to the IRP on its own.

The governing provision is Article 59-3 of the Income Tax Act (pension account tax credit). It credits 12 percent of contributions to a pension account against calculated global income tax, and 15 percent for a resident whose global income is 45 million won or less (or whose total wage and salary income is 55 million won or less, where that is the only income). It then puts two layers of ceiling on the contributions that count.

Read the table this way. The 9 million won figure is the ceiling on recognised contributions, not the refund. Fill it inside the 15 percent band and 1.35 million won is credited against calculated tax; in the 12 percent band it is 1.08 million won. One income threshold changes the outcome of an identical contribution.

There is a further condition people skip. A tax credit needs tax to work against. It is subtracted from calculated tax, so if other deductions have already driven your determined tax close to zero, filling the ceiling brings nothing back. That is why straining to max out contributions in a low-income year is less advantageous than it looks. Judge it against your own income and cash-flow plan, and where the sums are large or the income mix is complicated, a tax professional is the right place to take it.

4. A deduction is not an exemption

Looked at through the year-end refund alone, the account seems like pure gain. But the nature of the benefit is closer to deferral than to forgiveness. Tax not paid now reappears when the pension is drawn — at a lower rate.

Article 129(1)(5-2) of the Income Tax Act sets the withholding rate on pension income from a pension account according to the recipient's age.

  • Under 70 — 5 percent
  • 70 to under 80 — 4 percent
  • 80 and over — 3 percent
  • Pension income under a lifetime contract paying until death — 4 percent

Local income tax is added separately, which is why practical guidance usually quotes figures between 3.3 and 5.5 percent. Against the rates applied to employment income that is low, and the gap is the account's real advantage. The design also rewards drawing later, since the rate falls with age.

Money that arrived as severance is computed differently again. Article 129(1)(5-3) applies, to deferred retirement income drawn as pension, 70 percent of the non-pension withholding rate where the payout year is within 10 years and 60 percent beyond 10 years. The direction is clear: spreading severance over a longer period reduces the burden relative to taking it at once.

One more. Article 14(3)(9) of the Income Tax Act keeps pension income from a pension account out of aggregate global income where the annual total is 15 million won or less. Cross that line and it either moves into global taxation or, under Article 64-4 of the same Act, may be computed by applying 15 percent to that pension income as an elective method. The larger the retirement drawdown, the more the tax turns on how many years you spread it over rather than the total.

5. What happens if you break it early

This is where the account can hurt most, and it is the part that passes quickest in a sales conversation.

Take money out without meeting a statutory ground and it counts as a non-pension withdrawal. Article 129(1)(6) of the Income Tax Act sets a 15 percent withholding rate on that other income, and with local income tax added the figure quoted in practice is 16.5 percent.

What is easy to miss is what the rate applies to. It is not everything you put in.

  • Contributions that received the tax credit — you give back in proportion to the benefit taken
  • Investment gains — the returns whose taxation had been deferred inside the account
  • Contributions that never received the credit — amounts above the ceiling, or never claimed, fall outside this base

Put the two rates side by side and the loss becomes visible. Claim credits in the 12 percent band and pay 16.5 percent on the way out, and you hand back more than you received. Even in the 15 percent band a gap remains. Add the years during which that money could not be used elsewhere and the arithmetic worsens. That is precisely why this account should not be thought of as savings you can dip into.

6. Statutory withdrawals are computed differently

Not every early withdrawal is treated alike. Article 18 of the Enforcement Decree of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act sets out the grounds for early withdrawal from an individual retirement pension. Meet one and the computation differs from a plain closure.

Article 14 of the same Decree, which covers defined contribution plans, shows what kind of situation the scheme has in mind.

  • A member without a home purchasing a residence in their own name
  • A member without a home meeting a jeonse or lease deposit for housing purposes
  • Medical expenses exceeding 125 thousandths of the member's annual total wages for care lasting six months or more for the member or a dependant
  • A declaration of bankruptcy within five years counting back from the application date
  • A decision to commence individual rehabilitation within five years counting back from the application date
  • Damage suffered in a disaster
  • Repayment of principal and interest on a loan secured against the benefit, on grounds published by the Minister of Employment and Labor

The list has a common thread. Every item is a situation in which ordinary life has broken down. A better rate appearing elsewhere, or a large purchase coming up, is not on it. That is the design intent.

And the point that bites hardest in practice sits slightly to one side. Where no ground applies, the option of taking only part of the balance tends not to open at all. Even if the amount you need is a fraction of the account, the path of least resistance is closing it, and then the whole balance enters the non-pension computation. Factor that in when deciding how much to contribute. Whether your circumstances qualify, what documents are needed, and how each source of funds is computed are questions to put to the financial institution holding the account first.

7. What the account can and cannot hold

An IRP is not a product. It is a container. Opening it does not set anything in motion; you have to decide separately what goes inside. Skip that and you end up with an account sitting in cash for years.

Two broad categories

  • Principal-and-interest guaranteed — deposits, rate-guaranteed insurance and the like. Principal is protected; returns do not exceed the stated rate
  • Performance-based — funds, ETFs and similar. Outcomes move with markets, and loss of principal is possible

Risky assets carry a ceiling

Retirement pension accounts carry one rule other accounts do not. The whole balance cannot go into risky assets; a share must be held in safe assets. The current frame caps risky-asset investment at 70 percent in defined contribution and individual retirement pension accounts, with the remaining 30 percent held in deposits and similar safe assets. Buying Korean listed shares directly inside an individual retirement pension account is also not permitted.

As of August 2026 this ceiling is itself under discussion. Reporting has followed the financial authorities' push to remove the 70 percent limit in consultation with the relevant ministries, but differences with the ministry holding the power to amend the regulation remain, and it had not taken effect through 2026. In other words, anyone opening an account now is still under the 70/30 structure, and since this figure can change, confirm it with your financial institution before issuing investment instructions.

Choosing what to hold sits outside this article. The reason no product is named here is simple: it depends on the years remaining and the size of swing you can absorb, and that is not something anyone else can settle for you.

8. Fees: a small percentage meeting thirty years

Account management fees appear as figures after a decimal point and slide past the eye. But this account assumes a horizon of twenty or thirty years, so the small percentage is attached for an unusually long time.

Fees are generally charged along two lines.

  • Operation management fee — for managing the investment of the reserve
  • Asset management fee — for custody and administration of the account and its assets

There is a further cost outside the account: the expense ratio of whatever fund or ETF sits inside it. That comes out of the product, separately from account fees. To see the real burden you have to look at both layers. A low headline account fee changes nothing if the holdings inside carry heavy expenses.

Rates differ by institution, by account type and by source of funds. Some providers apply different rates to severance money and to additional personal contributions, or waive one side, so when comparing two disclosure sheets you have to line up like source against like source. Actual rates and charging methods vary by firm and change over time, so take the numbers from each provider's disclosures and Financial Supervisory Service guidance directly.

Comparison then reduces to three questions. Which source is charged, when is it deducted, and what happens if the account moves to another provider. The last one drops out of view most often, yet the longer an account is meant to last, the likelier a move becomes.

9. How it differs from a pension savings account, and how the combined ceiling binds

The two accounts have different names, but for the tax credit they are bound together. Miss that and you open two accounts hoping to fill two ceilings, for nothing.

Restating the structure of Article 59-3(1): pension savings contributions are recognised only up to 6 million won a year. That recognised amount is then combined with retirement pension account contributions, and anything above 9 million won a year is treated as nil. So a pension savings account tops out at 6 million on its own, and the 3 million won band above it can only be filled through a retirement pension account.

  • Eligibility — a pension savings account has no income requirement; an IRP is for people with income or those receiving severance benefits
  • What it can hold — the IRP carries the risky-asset ceiling and bars direct purchase of Korean listed shares. Pension savings accounts are regulated differently depending on type
  • Withdrawal — taking part of an IRP outside the statutory grounds is effectively difficult. This is what most sharply separates the two in daily experience
  • Receiving severance — only the IRP does this. A pension savings account is not used as a severance destination

The detailed comparison lives in the pension savings fund guide and the pension savings and IRP tax-saving guide. To see retirement income as a whole set of layers, the National Pension guide and the retirement preparation guide are the better fit, and if what you actually need is a vehicle for medium-term money, the differently shaped ISA guide is worth reading alongside.

10. Who it suits, and who it does not

The advantage and the cost are welded together here. The tax benefit exists as payment for locking money up; it is not a feature you can detach and keep. So the axis of judgement is not returns but whether you can leave this money alone until 55.

11. Frequently asked questions

Can anyone open an IRP in Korea?

Someone with income can open one voluntarily, and it also gets opened in order to receive severance benefits. The latter follows Articles 17(4) and 19(2) of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act, with exceptions such as leaving employment after 55 or a severance amount of 3 million won or less. Whether you can open the account and whether the tax credit will return anything are separate questions.

If I contribute 9 million won, do I get 9 million won back?

No. The 9 million won figure is the ceiling on recognised contributions. Twelve percent of it (15 percent in the qualifying income band) is credited against calculated tax. If your determined tax is already low, filling the ceiling does not bring that much back.

Can I withdraw part of the balance if I suddenly need cash?

Only through the door set by Article 18 of the Enforcement Decree of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act. Where the grounds do not fit, the path tends toward closing the account, at which point the credited contributions and the investment gains enter the non-pension computation. Check with your financial institution before assuming your case qualifies.

Is a pension payout completely tax free?

Not removed, deferred and applied at a lower rate. Article 129(1)(5-2) of the Income Tax Act sets withholding of 5, 4 and 3 percent by age (4 percent for a lifetime contract), with local income tax added separately. Article 14(3)(9) keeps annual pension income of 15 million won or less out of aggregate taxation, so crossing that line changes the method.

What happens to the account if I change employers?

The account belongs to the individual, not the employer. Treatment and fee application can differ by source of funds, and changing provider is a completely different event, in tax terms, from closing the account. If you are moving it, confirm that the process runs as a transfer and not a termination.

12. Closing: three questions to answer first

The account is neither a good product nor a bad one. It is a container built to reward people whose circumstances match it. The decision therefore comes from answers about yourself, not from comparing products.

  1. Can this money stay untouched until 55? If you are not sure, contribute less. Filling the ceiling is not the goal
  2. Do you actually owe tax this year? A credit needs tax to subtract from
  3. Have you decided what goes inside? An empty container does nothing on its own

Answer those three and the rest is execution. If one of them has no answer yet, that is exactly the reason to postpone the decision.

13. What could not be confirmed, and sources

What could not be confirmed

Stated plainly. The text of each subparagraph of Article 18 of the Enforcement Decree of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act could not be opened. The list in section 6 therefore reproduces Article 14 of the same Decree (grounds for early withdrawal from a defined contribution plan), and no claim is made that the grounds applying to individual retirement pensions are identical in scope. Whether your circumstances qualify has to be confirmed against the provision and your provider's guidance. The article number behind the 70 percent risky-asset ceiling in the retirement pension supervisory regulation could not be pinned to source text either, so the substance of the rule is given without a citation, and the abolition debate is described only as far as it has been reported. The statutory basis for the local income tax added to pension and other income was likewise not confirmed, so the 3.3 to 5.5 percent and 16.5 percent figures are used with a note that they are practical quotations combining the Income Tax Act rate with local income tax. Fee rates by provider vary by firm, source of funds and date, and are not the kind of number this article should supply.
