12 AI Bots Predicted Stock Moves for Two Months - What LDBD's Public Verification Really Proves

A Public Scoreboard for AI Stock Calls - What Two Months and 12 Bots Can (and Can't) Tell Us

Jul 14, 2026 Finance & Economy

Intro: How Much Should You Trust "This Stock Is Going Up"?

If you have spent any time around investing content, you have seen this scene before. A YouTube thumbnail, a Telegram tip room, and lately an AI chatbot - something states, with total confidence, "this stock is going up." If it happens to go up shortly after, that call gets screenshotted and paraded as proof of skill. When it is wrong, it usually just quietly disappears. That asymmetry is the single most fundamental problem with stock prediction as a category.

A service called LDBD recently published an interesting experiment that cuts against this pattern. It had 12 AI bots - built on Claude, Gemma, and ChatGPT - actually predict stock direction, locked each call to an immutable timestamp so no one could edit or delete it after the fact, and scored them in public for two months right alongside 18 simple baseline bots such as "always predict up." The platform's own tagline sums it up as roughly 「You called it? Prove it.」 This piece is not just a write-up of the experiment. It is my own analysis of why this kind of verifiable design matters, what a sample of two months and 12 bots can and cannot actually prove, and the deeper structural reasons AI still struggles to call stock direction in the first place. This is not investment advice about any specific ticker or service - please read the disclaimer at the end of this article.

1. Three Problems Baked Into Stock Prediction From the Start

Long before AI entered the picture, the act of predicting stock prices already had structural flaws. Understanding why an experiment like LDBD's matters starts with naming these flaws.

1.1 Hindsight Bias - "I Knew It All Along" Is Always Easy

People tend to believe, after learning an outcome, that the outcome was foreseeable all along. That is hindsight bias. Once a stock goes up, out of the countless comments made before it moved, only the ones that said "it's going up" get remembered and repeated. Anyone who said "it's going down," or said nothing at all, was never in the spotlight to begin with. In this process where the outcome reconstructs the cause, what was actually close to a random guess gets repackaged as if it had been an insightful call.

1.2 Cherry-Picking - Throw a Hundred Darts, Show Off One

A worse version is deliberate selection: scatter a hundred predictions, then screenshot and share only the handful that landed. This is not just a problem for individual influencers or paid tip rooms - it shows up plenty in academic backtesting too. Survivorship bias, for instance, drops failed or delisted companies from a dataset and computes historical returns using only the survivors, erasing real losses from the record and inflating apparent performance. Selecting favorable time windows, or highlighting a good metric while hiding a mediocre drawdown, is the same family of distortion.

1.3 An Unfalsifiable Prediction Is Not a Prediction

One of philosopher Karl Popper's core principles is that a claim not stated in a way that could be proven wrong is not a scientific claim to begin with. Phrases like "it will go up eventually" or "it trends up in the long run" cannot be falsified. A prediction with no stated timeframe or threshold, or one that leaves an escape hatch to be redefined after the fact, can always be claimed as "correct" regardless of the outcome. Unfalsifiability is not a quirk - it is a defect.

2. What LDBD Designed Differently - Falsifiability and a Baseline Comparison

This is where an experiment like LDBD's becomes genuinely interesting. Let's look at what it actually did, and why that design confronts the problems above head-on.

2.1 The Experiment - What Was Actually Done

Per the public materials, LDBD pitted 12 AI bots built on Claude, Gemma, and ChatGPT against 18 baseline bots following simple rules such as "always up" or "always down," under identical conditions. Over two months, roughly 1,400 live AI-bot predictions were submitted, and including historical data the platform reports scoring roughly 128,000 predictions cumulatively. Every prediction is timestamped at submission and cannot be edited or deleted afterward, and performance is shown not as a raw win rate but as a return-based, annualized score together with a 95% confidence interval.

2.2 The Key Design Choice - Comparing Against a Base Rate

The single most important design choice is that it does not just compare AI bots to each other - it puts them right next to baseline bots following trivial rules. A coin-flip-equivalent "always up" strategy racks up a fairly high win rate in a rising market. So a claim that some AI bot hit "60% accuracy" means nothing on its own; the only meaningful question is whether that beats the baseline bots' accuracy over the same period by a meaningful margin. This is exactly what statisticians call comparing against a base rate, and it is a yardstick that simply does not exist in an individual influencer's brag post about having "called it."

2.3 What Making It Falsifiable Actually Means

Locking a timestamp and blocking edits is a mechanism that shuts down hindsight bias and cherry-picking at the source. Because the content is frozen exactly as it was at the moment of submission, no one can later claim "what I really meant was..." Philip Tetlock's Good Judgment superforecasting project applied the same principle to geopolitical forecasting: force forecasters to state probabilities as explicit numbers, the way weather forecasters do, then score those numbers against outcomes. In that project, the top forecasters turned out to be roughly 30% more accurate than professional intelligence analysts - and the key was not that they were unusually inspired, but that they continuously scored and corrected their own predictions. LDBD's design carries the same weight: what matters most is not any single prediction, but the fact that it built a verifiable structure in the first place.

3. What Two Months and 12 Bots Can - and Cannot - Prove

A good design does not automatically make the results a trustworthy conclusion. The limits of the sample itself deserve a cold, hard look.

3.1 Against a Random Walk, Two Months Is Barely Anything

That stock prices move close to a random walk means today's price change tells you almost nothing about tomorrow's. In that kind of environment, much of the win-rate gap over a short window comes down not to skill but to whichever market regime happened to occur during that stretch. Two months is a short enough window that a single downturn or a single rally can flip the entire result. The same bot's scorecard could look completely different across two months of a sustained rally versus two months that happened to include a sharp drop.

3.2 The Sample Is Too Small to Separate Luck From Skill

To say statistically that a strategy is "skill, not luck," you need enough independent trials across a variety of market regimes - up, down, sideways, and volatile. Twelve bots over two months falls well short of that bar. With 12 bots running, one or two can beat the baseline by pure chance alone - the same way that if you flip a coin 12 separate times in a row, one of those runs is likely to turn up an unusually high share of heads. That a bot lands near the top of the leaderboard does not, by itself, prove that bot's "skill."

3.3 A Methodological Trap LDBD Itself Admitted To

What is genuinely interesting is that LDBD has publicly disclosed running into this exact problem. Its early scoring approach effectively measured how often a bot happened to be holding volatile assets - a flaw that rewarded bots for taking on more risk rather than for genuine predictive skill. It later re-evaluated roughly 128,000 real predictions and revised its metric to measure actual skill, and separately had to address the problem of trusting AI bots too quickly based on a small sample. That even a service built around verification had to fix its own metric twice is a real-world illustration of just how careful you have to be reading performance out of a short period and a small sample.

4. The Deeper Reason AI Struggles to Call Stock Direction

Beyond the sample-size problem, it is worth naming the structural reasons why AI - or really any forecaster - has such a hard time reliably calling the "direction" of a stock in the first place.

4.1 The Efficient Market Hypothesis and Random Walk Theory

The efficient market hypothesis (EMH), formalized by Eugene Fama in 1970, holds that current prices already reflect all available information, so that information alone cannot be used to consistently beat the market. Its companion, random walk theory - popularized by Burton Malkiel - holds that price changes are driven by random events, so neither technical nor fundamental analysis can reliably beat the market over time. Under this framework, for most individual investors, low-cost diversification and long-term holding tend to be a more rational choice than trying to predict.

4.2 The Market Is Not a Fixed Target - Non-Stationarity

Even if an AI model finds a plausible-looking pattern in historical data, financial markets are non-stationary systems whose behavior shifts the moment a pattern is discovered and acted on. Regulations change, the mix of participants changes, and as other investors learn similar patterns, the pattern's own validity tends to erode. A model that worked well in one period failing to hold up in the next is less a flaw in the model than an intrinsic property of the market itself.

4.3 The Information Is Already Priced In

It is true that AI can read news, filings, and social media faster than a human. But the moment that information becomes public, other investors and other AI systems read it at nearly the same time. Prices start adjusting the instant new information becomes known, so a prediction submitted after the fact is close to restating information that has already been priced in. AI processing information quickly and AI having a unique, first-mover edge over everyone else are two entirely different claims.

4.4 What Actual Research Shows - a Mixed Picture

Academic findings here need to be handled carefully. Some studies report that AI models outperformed human analysts under certain conditions, and others report that large language models built on news headlines out-forecast earlier model generations. But at the same time, plenty of studies find that direction prediction specifically - up or down - remains stubbornly unreliable. In one documented case, adding a large language model on top of a traditional machine-learning model provided zero improvement to directional accuracy, with the language model's value confined to producing a more readable report rather than a better number. Taken together, AI's ability to summarize market-related text and AI's ability to reliably call tomorrow's price direction need to be treated as two separate questions.

5. The Real Lesson for Individual Investors

The takeaway from the LDBD experiment does not have to be the binary "AI is useless" or "AI is about to beat the market." Here is the practical lesson as I see it.

5.1 Demand Verifiability, Not the Content of the Prediction

When anyone - or any service - hands you a prediction, the first question is not "what did they predict," but "can this be confirmed, timestamped, with no after-the-fact edits, against a baseline." A track record that cannot be checked is not a track record - it is a marketing line.

5.2 Principles Over Predictions - Think Probabilistically

The most important attitude this experiment demonstrates is not correctly calling "will this stock go up or down" - it is keeping the probability that your own judgment is wrong permanently in view, and sizing your position and your loss limits accordingly. What made superforecasters good was not a knack for certainty; it was expressing uncertainty as an explicit number and flexibly revising it as new information arrived.

5.3 Don't Flip Your Judgment on One Signal

Concluding that a bot has "skill" just because it beat the baseline over two months ignores exactly the sample limitations covered above. A short lucky streak can always happen; whether it is repeatable skill can only be said cautiously after a much longer period across a much wider range of market regimes.

6. Spotting Fake Prediction Services and Tip-Room Scams

All of this eventually runs into a very practical problem: a market where predictions themselves are bought and sold.

6.1 Common Fraud Patterns

Complaints tied to unlicensed investment-advice operations have risen sharply in recent years according to Korea's Financial Supervisory Service, with losses in individual cases ranging from the low tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars. A common tactic is building a fake trading terminal nearly identical to a real brokerage's software, showing manipulated numbers served entirely from the scammer's own server while pushing victims to deposit funds. Screenshots claiming "every call I've made has gone up" are a textbook case of the cherry-picking described earlier - not a verifiable track record.

6.2 The Questions That Separate the Real From the Fake

Whatever prediction service you encounter, checking the following becomes a genuine line of defense. First, is the entire history of past calls - wins and losses alike - published with timestamps? Second, is performance shown against a baseline rather than a raw win rate? Third, does it promise guaranteed returns (any real investment product carries principal-loss risk, and a guaranteed-return claim is itself a classic sign of an unlicensed investment-advice scheme)? Fourth, is the operator a licensed financial firm you can verify through your local regulator? A red flag on any one of these four is reason enough to walk away.

Conclusion - Not Whether AI Gets It Right, But Whether You Can Verify It

What makes the LDBD experiment interesting is not whether the AI bots beat the market. Far more important is the attempt itself - to place the act of prediction inside a verifiable frame built from timestamps, immutable records, baseline comparisons, and confidence intervals. A result drawn from two months and 12 bots is, on its own, too short and too small to support any definitive conclusion. But this experiment reframes the question from "was the prediction right" to the more fundamental "was the prediction ever presented in a verifiable form to begin with." What any individual investor can do today is apply that same question, equally, to every prediction they encounter - AI or human, free or paid.
