Asking AI About Legal Issues: What Is Allowed and What Crosses the Line Under the Korean Attorney-at-Law Act

Examining Article 109 and the Practical Boundary Between Understanding Legal Terminology and Delegating Dispute Judgment

Introduction: Legal Inquiries and Generative AI - The Crossroads Between Concept Understanding and Delegated Judgment

As generative artificial intelligence rapidly integrates into daily workflows and personal problem-solving routines, posing questions to AI models across diverse technical, economic, and practical domains has become second nature. The legal field has proved no exception to this broad cultural and technological shift. Today, a growing number of individuals routinely turn to conversational AI platforms to ask how a particular clause in a residential lease contract should be construed, whether an employer's unilateral disciplinary action constitutes an unfair labor practice under Korean labor statutes, or how the procedural timeline of a civil lawsuit unfolds when an unexpected dispute emerges.

However, the practice of law is subject to stringent statutory constraints that differ fundamentally from general software documentation, coding assistance, or multilingual translation. Under the legal architecture of the Republic of Korea, the legal system establishes rigorous penal sanctions designed to protect the public from unqualified practitioners and preserve the integrity of the judicial process. The central pillar of this protective framework is Article 109 of the Attorney-at-Law Act (Penal Provisions). Because of this formidable statute, some observers fear that typing any legal question into an AI interface might cross into illegality, while others mistakenly assume that generative models can be used without restriction to draft pleadings, calculate dispute damages, and dictate courtroom strategies as if they were licensed advocates.

The practical reality does not conform to such binary extremes. Between harmless informational queries and illicit legal practice lies an expansive landscape with nuanced boundaries. On one side sits the legitimate, highly beneficial exploration of statutory terms, institutional definitions, and procedural overviews. On the other side sits the dangerous delegation of substantive dispute resolution, where an unlicensed mechanism effectively replaces professional counsel in deciding the rights and obligations of concrete adversaries. This article undertakes a rigorous examination of the statutory text and constituent elements of Article 109 of the Attorney-at-Law Act, providing readers with the analytical tools needed to distinguish between understanding legal terminology and delegating case judgment.

  • Exploring dictionary definitions of obscure legal jargon or general procedural sequences in civil litigation
  • Searching for relevant statutory provisions or examining the literal phrasing of standard contract templates
  • Requesting AI to assess fault ratios, predict win rates, or calculate monetary damages in an active dispute
  • Relying completely on automated generation to draft formal complaints, answers, or settlement agreements for filing

Statutory Text and Penalties of Article 109 of the Attorney-at-Law Act

Any disciplined legal analysis must begin with the exact statutory text enacted by the legislature. As confirmed through the Korean National Law Information Center, Article 109 of the Attorney-at-Law Act (Act No. 17828, as amended) provides as follows:

Article 109 (Penal Provisions)
Any person who falls under any of the following items shall be punished by imprisonment for not more than 7 years or by a fine not exceeding 50 million KRW. In this case, the fine and imprisonment may be concurrently imposed.
1. A person who, not being an attorney-at-law, receives or promises to receive money, hospitality, or other profits, or causes or promises to cause a third party to receive them, and handles or brokers appraisal, representation, arbitration, settlement, solicitation, legal consultation, drafting of documents related to legal affairs, or other legal affairs regarding the following cases:
  (a) Litigation cases, non-litigation cases, family conciliation, or family adjudication cases;
  (b) Cases involving appeals to administrative agencies, such as administrative appeals, requests for examination, or objections;
  (c) Investigation cases handled by investigative agencies;
  (d) Inquiry cases handled by statutory investigation agencies established pursuant to Acts and subordinate statutes;
  (e) Other general legal cases;
2. A person who violates Article 33 or 34 (including cases where they apply mutatis mutandis pursuant to Article 57, 58-16, or 58-30).

The primary element demanding careful observation is the severity of the prescribed statutory penalty. Article 109 establishes a maximum term of 7 years of imprisonment or a fine of up to 50 million KRW, and explicitly authorizes courts to sentence both imprisonment and financial penalties concurrently. Within the Korean criminal law framework, this represents a severe sanction, underscoring the legislature's uncompromising determination to prevent unlicensed actors from conducting remunerated legal affairs.

The Three Core Requirements of Article 109 Item 1 - All Three Must Concurrently Exist

In statutory interpretation governed by the principle of legality and strict construction (nulla poena sine lege), criminal liability requires the simultaneous satisfaction of all statutory elements. Under Article 109 Item 1 of the Attorney-at-Law Act, an offense is established only if all three constituent requirements are concurrently met. If any single requirement is absent, criminal liability under this specific penal provision cannot be sustained.

These three essential requirements are structured as follows:

  1. Status Requirement (Actor): The person engaging in the conduct must not be a licensed attorney-at-law under Korean law. If a licensed attorney admitted to the Korean Bar handles legal affairs within authorized professional limits, Article 109 is inapplicable.
  2. Economic Requirement (Remuneration): The actor must receive or promise to receive money, hospitality, or other profits, or arrange for a third party to receive them. A purely gratuitous act that confers zero economic or material benefit fails to satisfy this remuneration requirement.
  3. Conduct Requirement (Handling or Brokering Legal Affairs): In connection with the cases specified in sub-items (a) through (e), the actor must handle or broker appraisal, representation, arbitration, settlement, solicitation, legal consultation, drafting of legal documents, or other legal affairs. This requires substantive involvement in legal rights and duties rather than mere academic exposition.

Detailed Breakdown by Requirement: Non-Attorney Status, Remuneration, and Handling Legal Affairs

The practical operation of these three constituent requirements involves refined legal distinctions. To allow readers to assess their own situations against the statutory architecture, each element must be analyzed in depth.

First, consider the non-attorney status requirement. The Attorney-at-Law Act restricts the professional, remunerated conduct of legal affairs exclusively to individuals who hold certified attorney qualifications and maintain active registration with a local bar association and the Korean Bar Association. The modern complexity introduced by generative AI is that the model itself is not a human actor (natural person). Under existing Korean criminal jurisprudence, an algorithmic model cannot serve as a direct subject of criminal prosecution. However, the commercial entities that design, operate, and monetize AI platforms, as well as intermediaries who deploy automated tools to offer services to third parties for gain, are natural persons or corporate bodies. Where such operators lack bar admission, the non-attorney status element becomes directly relevant.

Second, consider the remuneration (profit-seeking) requirement. Article 109 Item 1 is not a blanket ban on sharing legal knowledge or assisting others out of goodwill. Rather, the statute specifically targets the unauthorized commercial exploitation of legal affairs. The statutory phrase encompasses not only money and hospitality but also 「other profits」. This broad formulation captures monetary service fees, recurring subscription charges, advertising revenues, equity grants, and any tangible or intangible counter-performance carrying economic value. Conversely, when an end user queries an open-source model or an entirely free service to locate statutory citations, the conventional requirement of direct remuneration is absent from the user's transaction. However, this distinction does not grant universal immunity, as business monetization models continue to undergo regulatory examination.

Third, consider the conduct requirement of handling or brokering legal affairs. The statutory text enumerates appraisal, representation, arbitration, settlement, solicitation, legal consultation, and document drafting, complemented by the omnibus clause 「other legal affairs」. Established Supreme Court jurisprudence interprets legal affairs as any activity that exercises legal judgment or produces, alters, or terminates legal rights and obligations regarding a concrete dispute or question. Simply explaining the wording of Civil Act Article 186 is general information; conversely, assessing whether Buyer A can forfeit the deposit paid by Seller B under Clause 5 of a specific real estate purchase agreement constitutes substantive legal consultation.

  • Citing statutory articles, verbatim Supreme Court holdings, and administrative rules operates primarily as general information
  • Analyzing the factual allegations of an active dispute to declare win rates or assess legal liability constitutes legal consultation
  • Drafting finalized complaints, answers, or criminal accusations tailored to the factual claims of a party constitutes legal document preparation
  • Connecting an individual client to a designated attorney in return for a referral fee constitutes unlawful brokering under the statute
  • Case law firmly maintains that legal affairs are determined by substantive involvement in legal rights rather than superficial labeling
  • The crucial dividing line is whether the generated output serves as an abstract explanation or as an individualized legal prescription

Fundamental Difference Between General Legal Information and Delegated Case Judgment

Synthesizing the legislative purpose and doctrinal interpretation of Article 109, the decisive benchmark that users must maintain when interacting with AI is the profound difference between 「understanding concepts and systems」 and 「delegating case judgment」.

The pursuit of conceptual understanding directly aligns with constitutional rights to information and access to justice. Inquiring into the technical distinction between opposing power and preferential payment rights under the Housing Lease Protection Act, reviewing the procedural difference between provisional attachment and provisional injunction in civil execution law, or studying the formal stages of arrest review proceedings under the Criminal Procedure Act is functionally identical to consulting a legal treatise or an official statute book. Generative AI models that summarize authentic statutes and accredited commentaries enhance public legal literacy and serve a legitimate educational function.

By contrast, delegating the substantive judgment of an active dispute introduces grave practical and procedural dangers. Posing prompts such as "My landlord has withheld my deposit; can I change the door locks tomorrow without facing criminal liability?" or "The opposing driver asserts an 80% fault ratio; what settlement amount should I demand to guarantee victory?" is not a search for general knowledge. Such queries ask the machine to analyze concrete facts, formulate courtroom strategy, and dictate irreversible legal actions. That process represents substantive legal consultation and the handling of legal affairs.

Scope of Covered Cases: Broad Structure from Sub-item (a) to Sub-item (e)

The scope of matters governed by Article 109 Item 1 is delineated across sub-items (a) through (e), covering a broad spectrum of legal situations. Grasping this scope is vital for determining whether a particular issue involves a regulated legal case.

The statute enumerates the following five categories:

  • Sub-item (a): Litigation, non-litigation, family conciliation, or family adjudication cases — Captures all formal court proceedings, including civil actions, criminal trials, domestic relations disputes, and non-contentious matters.
  • Sub-item (b): Appeals to administrative agencies, such as administrative appeals, requests for examination, or objections — Covers administrative litigation prerequisites, tax objections, labor commission relief applications, and expropriation protests.
  • Sub-item (c): Investigation cases handled by investigative agencies — Encompasses all criminal investigations conducted by the police, prosecution offices, or the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials.
  • Sub-item (d): Inquiry cases handled by statutory investigation agencies — Includes formal inquiries conducted by bodies established by statute, such as the Fair Trade Commission, Financial Supervisory Service, or National Human Rights Commission.
  • Sub-item (e): Other general legal cases — Represents an expansive catch-all provision encompassing all social matters involving the creation, alteration, transfer, or extinguishment of legal rights and duties.

Importantly, the Korean Supreme Court has construed sub-item (e) (「other general legal cases」) with considerable breadth. It is not confined to disputes that have already materialized into formal lawsuits before a judicial bench. Private contract controversies, commercial settlement negotiations, and creditor-debtor reconciliations that carry a probability of developing into litigation fall squarely within general legal cases. Therefore, the assumption that an issue is not a legal case simply because formal litigation has not yet been filed is legally untenable.

Practical Precautions and Safe Usage Principles from the User Perspective

In light of these legal structures, how can citizens, business managers, and corporate staff use generative AI safely and productively when confronted with legal questions? The fundamental guiding principle is to strictly position AI as an auxiliary research tool for organizing facts and terminology, rather than as an authoritative decision-maker.

The following practical rules represent sound, defensible practices:

  • 1. Use AI to construct chronological fact summaries: Rather than demanding a legal conclusion, request the AI to organize dates, events, text communications, and contractual milestones into an objective timeline. This is completely safe and immensely valuable.
  • 2. Identify applicable statutory frameworks: Use AI to determine whether a workplace dispute is governed by the Labor Standards Act or the Commercial Act, and locate relevant article numbers for further personal review.
  • 3. Prepare structured agendas for attorney consultations: To maximize the efficiency of billable consultation hours, ask AI to draft a checklist of essential questions and required evidence (contracts, receipts, transcripts) to bring to counsel.
  • 4. Maintain vigilant skepticism against legal hallucinations: AI models frequently generate fabricated case numbers or cite repealed statutory provisions to preserve fluent syntax. All citations must be checked against authentic sources on the National Law Information Center.
  • 5. Refrain from direct submission of AI-generated pleadings: Submitting AI-generated complaints or briefs to a court without rigorous legal scrutiny can lead to catastrophic losses due to unverified factual concessions or flawed causes of action.
  • 6. Anonymize personal and confidential data: When drafting prompts, thoroughly mask real names, resident registration numbers, financial accounts, and exact addresses to protect sensitive privacy interests.

Ultimately, the responsible deployment of AI in legal contexts is not about asking the model to decide how to win a lawsuit. It is about using automated tools to articulate one's factual situation clearly, enabling licensed attorneys to deliver precise, effective counsel. Ultimate legal responsibility rests with the individual, and the substantive protection of rights is achieved only through qualified professional representation.
