Durable Workflows With Postgres 2026 - Building Reliable Background Jobs Without a Separate Queue

Do you really need Kafka? Build reliable background jobs with just the Postgres you already run.

May 31, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Do You Really Need Kafka or Temporal for Reliable Workflows?

"To process background jobs reliably, you need a message queue." Most developers accept this almost reflexively. The moment we have to send an email, run post-payment processing, fire a notification, or move data through a pipeline, we immediately reach for a dedicated tool like Kafka, RabbitMQ, or Temporal. Then we stand up a separate cluster for it, assign people to operate it, and add yet another monitoring surface.

But it is worth pausing to ask: does it really have to be that way? In 2026, more and more teams are arriving at the opposite conclusion. The Postgres you already run is enough to build durable workflows. No new infrastructure, no new failure points, no new learning curve forced on the team.

This article takes that possibility seriously, especially from the perspective of small teams that must move fast with few people. We will look at how a Postgres-based workflow achieves reliability, what the core patterns are, and above all when Postgres is enough and when a dedicated tool is actually warranted. The short version: the answer is neither "Postgres for everything" nor "Kafka is the right call." It depends on the nature of your data and your scale.

The one-line takeaway. In most small-to-mid services, the reliability of background jobs comes not from a separate broker but from Postgres transactions. Enroll the job in the business transaction, distribute workers with SKIP LOCKED, and make handlers idempotent, and you get durability with zero new infrastructure.

1. What Is a "Durable Workflow"? Defining Durable Execution

Before going further, let us pin down the term. What is a "durable workflow"? The key is right there in the word: durability. It is the guarantee that once a job has started, it will eventually run to completion, or at the very least will not be lost and will be retried, no matter whether the server dies, the process restarts, or the network drops.

More concretely, a durable workflow has these properties:

  • No job loss: Once accepted, a job never disappears through any failure. It is safely written to disk.
  • At-least-once execution: A job runs at least once. If a worker dies mid-flight, another worker picks it up.
  • Resumable after interruption: Even if execution stops, it can continue from the last recorded point or safely retry from the start.
  • Persistent state: A job's progress lives in durable storage, not in volatile memory.

Here comes the decisive insight: these properties overlap almost exactly with the ACID guarantees a relational database already provides. "Committed means it survives" is durability (D). "Concurrent work does not corrupt each other" is isolation (I). "All or nothing" is atomicity (A). Much of what a workflow engine labors to build, Postgres simply offers through features it has refined for over thirty years.

2. Why Postgres? The Database You Already Have Is Enough

So why Postgres specifically? It comes down to three things: transactions, durability, and simplicity.

2.1 Transactions: The Foundation of Reliability

Postgres's strongest weapon is the transaction. The key is that you can bundle a change to business data and the enrollment of a background job into a single transaction. For example, if you process "create the order, then enroll a confirmation-email job" in one transaction, the two succeed together or fail together. It becomes structurally impossible for the order to exist while the email job is missing. With a separate broker, that exact gap appears, the one where "the DB commit succeeded but publishing the message failed." Put the job inside Postgres and the gap itself vanishes.

2.2 Durability: Committed Means On Disk

Through the WAL (Write-Ahead Log), Postgres safely writes committed data to disk first. Even if the server dies suddenly, committed jobs are recovered. Those "last few records" an in-memory queue might lose during a failure, Postgres does not lose. Job reliability becomes equal to database reliability.

2.3 Simplicity: Only One System to Operate

This may be the biggest practical benefit. Put the job queue in Postgres and you do not add a system to operate. Backups, monitoring, incident response, and security patches are all absorbed by the one database you already maintain. No new broker to learn, no new failure modes, no new on-call burden. For a small team, this "it all ends in one place" simplicity is often far more valuable than a throughput number.

3. Five Core Patterns of Postgres-Based Workflows

Enough theory. Here are the five core patterns, organized in my own way, that keep recurring when you build reliable workflows on Postgres. Understand these five and you are practically halfway there.

3.1 The Job Table

The starting point for everything. Create a table that holds each unit of work as a row. Add columns for status (pending/running/done/failed), payload (JSON), retry count, and scheduled run time. Because a job is just a data row, you get free querying, filtering, and aggregation through SQL.

3.2 Transactional Outbox

The pattern that bundles a business change and a job enrollment into the same transaction. Instead of publishing an outbound event directly, you first record it in an outbox (job) table in the same database. A separate worker reads that table and handles the actual publishing. This eliminates the dual-write problem where "the DB committed but the event was lost."

3.3 SELECT FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED

The locking technique that lets multiple workers grab jobs from the same table without contention. Rows already locked by another worker are skipped, and only the next unlocked row is taken. Workers no longer wait on each other or pick up the same job twice, enabling clean parallel distribution. This is the heart of a Postgres job queue.

3.4 Idempotency

An at-least-once guarantee also means "the same job may run twice." So job handlers must be designed to be idempotent, producing the same result even when run twice. The standard approach is to enforce an idempotency key (for example, order ID plus job type) as a unique constraint to block duplicate side effects.

3.5 Retry and Backoff

Transient failures (network errors, external API timeouts) are natural. Keep a retry count and a next-attempt time on the job row, and on failure reschedule with exponential backoff. Once a set limit is exceeded, move the job to a dead-letter state for a human to review. These four pieces (retry, backoff, ceiling, dead-letter) form the standard failure-management setup.

4. Code Example: Building a Simple Job Queue

The patterns become far clearer in real code. Let us sketch the simplest possible job queue with SQL and pseudocode. First, the job table.

CREATE TABLE jobs (
    id           BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
    kind         TEXT        NOT NULL,            -- job type (e.g., send_email)
    payload      JSONB       NOT NULL,            -- job data
    status       TEXT        NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending', -- pending/running/done/failed
    run_at       TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),     -- scheduled run time
    attempts     INT         NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,  -- number of attempts
    max_attempts INT         NOT NULL DEFAULT 5,
    idem_key     TEXT        UNIQUE,              -- idempotency key
    created_at   TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- Partial index so workers find the next job fast
CREATE INDEX idx_jobs_ready
    ON jobs (run_at)
    WHERE status = 'pending';

Job enrollment happens inside the same transaction as the business logic. This is the heart of the transactional outbox.

BEGIN;
  -- 1) the business change
  INSERT INTO orders (user_id, amount) VALUES (42, 19900);
  -- 2) enroll the job in the same transaction
  INSERT INTO jobs (kind, payload, idem_key)
  VALUES ('send_email', '{"order_id": 1001}', 'email:order:1001');
COMMIT;  -- the two commit together or roll back together

A worker pulls jobs with pseudocode like the following. This is where SKIP LOCKED appears.

loop:
    begin transaction
      job = grab and lock the next ready job with SKIP LOCKED
      if no job: wait briefly, then continue
      set job status to 'running', attempts += 1
    commit

    try:
      run handler(job.payload)   # designed to be idempotent
      set job status to 'done'
    on failure:
      if attempts >= max_attempts: set 'failed' (dead-letter)
      else: run_at = now() + backoff, status back to 'pending'

The key point is that all of this is done with plain SQL and transactions. No new infrastructure, no new protocol. You assemble reliability out of tools you already know.

5. Handling Concurrency With SKIP LOCKED

Of the five patterns, the one that feels most like magic is SKIP LOCKED. It is worth a closer look.

Imagine several workers trying to grab work from the same job table at once. Use a naive SELECT ... FOR UPDATE and worker B, trying to take a row worker A has locked, will block until the lock is released. The more workers you add, the worse the congestion of waiting on one another. They effectively serialize.

Add SKIP LOCKED and the behavior changes completely.

SELECT id, kind, payload
FROM jobs
WHERE status = 'pending' AND run_at <= now()
ORDER BY run_at
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
LIMIT 1;

Now when worker B hits a row worker A has already locked, it does not wait; it simply skips it and grabs the next unlocked row. As a result, N workers take N different jobs concurrently, without collisions and without duplicates. Because lock waits disappear, throughput scales nearly linearly as you add workers.

SKIP LOCKED flipped the question from "can you use Postgres as a job queue?" to "is there any reason not to use Postgres as a job queue?"

Thanks to this single locking option, the "contention-free job distribution" that external brokers boast about can be reproduced right inside Postgres, without giving up an inch of transactional safety.

6. Postgres vs Dedicated Tools: A Comparison

That said, Postgres does not replace every dedicated tool. A fair comparison is in order. Here are four representative tools along the axes of complexity, throughput, and best fit.

Table 1. Workflow and Messaging Tool Comparison

RabbitMQMediumHighMessaging-centric, complex routing

The message running through the table is this: Postgres has the lowest complexity while covering throughput up to "medium." And the throughput most services actually need sits right inside that "medium" band. Kafka and Temporal are powerful, but that power comes with a price tag of operational complexity. The problem is that many teams pay that complexity cost up front, today, for throughput they "might need someday."

7. When Is Postgres Enough, and When Do You Need a Dedicated Tool?

We have reached the central question. Where is the boundary? Here are the signals I use as decision criteria.

7.1 Signals That Postgres Is Enough

  • Throughput is in the range of tens to a few thousand per second, and even spikes stay within it.
  • Jobs are tightly coupled to data changes in the same database as the enrollment (transactional coupling pays off).
  • You have few operators, so adding another system is costly.
  • Workflow steps are relatively simple (sending, post-processing, notifications).

7.2 Signals That You Need a Dedicated Tool

  • Throughput persistently exceeds tens to hundreds of thousands per second (Kafka territory).
  • Multiple consumer groups must independently consume the same event (fan-out, Kafka).
  • Workflows run over several days and need complex branching, compensation, and replay (Temporal).
  • Events must be replayed into a long-retained log (Kafka).

My recommendation is clear: start with Postgres, and move when the "dedicated-tool signals" above actually light up. Conversely, laying down Kafka before any signal has fired is usually premature optimization.

8. Five Real-World Application Scenarios

Let us move the abstract principles into concrete scenes. Here is how a Postgres workflow fits five background jobs common in real services.

  • Email/SMS sending: Signup welcome, order confirmation. Bundle the send job with the signup or order transaction and the "signed up but no email" incident disappears. External-API failures are absorbed by retries.
  • Post-payment processing: Receipt issuance, point accrual, settlement records after authorization. An idempotency key blocks duplicate accruals, and per-step jobs safely retry partial failures.
  • Data pipelines: Nightly aggregation, report generation, external data sync. Schedule via the run_at column, and split heavy work into small unit jobs that workers process in parallel.
  • Notifications (push/webhook): Bulk push delivery, webhooks to external systems. Multiple workers split the load with SKIP LOCKED, and failed webhooks retry with backoff.
  • Scheduling: Subscription renewal, reminders, expiration handling. Put a future time in run_at and a worker naturally picks it up at that moment. No separate cron infrastructure needed.

What these five share is that none of them demands Kafka-class throughput or Temporal-class workflow complexity. And these five make up most of the background work in most services.

9. Performance and Scalability Limits

For balance, let us be honest about the limits of a Postgres workflow too. This pattern does not scale forever.

  • Throughput ceiling: A job queue is ultimately write load on the database. Once jobs sustain tens of thousands per second, they contend for resources with the database's day job (service queries). This is where a dedicated broker shines.
  • Table bloat and vacuum: As done and failed jobs accumulate, the table swells and indexes grow heavy. Periodic archiving (deleting or moving completed jobs) and autovacuum tuning are essential. Neglect it and queries slow down.
  • Polling load: If workers poll an empty queue at short intervals, unnecessary queries pile up. Adding notification-based wake-ups with LISTEN/NOTIFY relieves this.
  • Impact on the day job: Queue load can introduce latency into core service transactions. When load grows large, splitting the queue into a separate Postgres instance is one option.

The point is that a Postgres workflow is not "infinite scale" but rather a solution that "covers a usefully wide band simply." You only need to recognize the moment you cross beyond that band and be ready to move.

10. Applying It in a Startup Environment: Simpler Infra, Lower Cost

This discussion is especially persuasive in a startup environment, and for good reasons.

First, staffing structure. Early-stage startups usually have a handful of full-stack developers who also own infrastructure. Standing up and operating a Kafka cluster or Temporal server is a full person's job in itself. Absorb the job queue into a single Postgres and the number of systems to operate drops and the on-call burden lightens.

Second, cost. The cost of running managed Kafka or a separate workflow cluster on the cloud is not negligible. In an early stage with still-small traffic, paying that cost today for "scale someday" eats into your runway. Put the job queue inside the Postgres you are already paying for and the added infrastructure cost converges to zero.

Third, speed. A startup's edge often comes from speed of execution. If the days or weeks spent learning and integrating a new broker can be replaced by the few hours it takes to write a job queue in SQL you already know, that time goes straight into product.

Practical advice. If you are at seed to Series A, start with a Postgres job queue. There is no rush to adopt Kafka or Temporal until traffic is proven and the "dedicated-tool signals" have fired. The complexity cost of early adoption almost always outweighs its benefit.

11. The Risk: The "Postgres for Everything" Trap

To be fair, the warning must also run the other way. The moment "Postgres is enough" mutates into "Postgres for everything," this article would be encouraging a different premature decision.

The typical traps of Postgres maximalism look like this:

  • Ignoring the limit signals: Refusing to move out of pride ("we do it all on Postgres") even when throughput is clearly hitting the ceiling. By then the day-job database slows down too.
  • Underestimating operational burden: Putting the queue in Postgres reduces infrastructure, but adds new operational items: vacuuming, archiving, index management, monitoring. It is not "free," just a "different cost."
  • Forcing in workflow complexity: Trying to mimic a multi-day, multi-step compensation workflow with a single job table turns the code into spaghetti. That is the point to look seriously at Temporal.
  • Resource contention with the day job: Insisting on the same instance even as core service transactions slow down under queue load.

The healthy attitude is simple. Postgres is an excellent default, not a permanent answer. It is almost always right as a starting point, but it must be paired with the discipline to read the limit signals honestly and move on time.

12. Conclusion + Three Things You Can Apply Today

Back to the opening question. Do you really need Kafka or Temporal for reliable workflows? For most small-to-mid services, the answer is no. The real source of reliability is not a flashy broker but the Postgres transaction, and we already hold it in our hands. Treat jobs as data, bundle them with transactions, distribute them with SKIP LOCKED, and neutralize duplicates with idempotency, and you have durability on par with a message broker.

At the same time, this is not a dogma of "always Postgres." The key is the balance of recognizing the moment scale and complexity cross a threshold and moving to a dedicated tool on time. Let me close with three things you can apply today.

  1. Enroll jobs in the same transaction as the business change. This one thing structurally eliminates the most common incident: lost jobs.
  2. Implement worker distribution with SELECT FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED. You get contention-free parallelism right inside Postgres, with no external broker.
  3. Design every job handler to be idempotent. Making duplicate execution harmless in an at-least-once world is the last piece of reliability.

The next time the thought "it is a background job, so I need a queue" comes to mind, pause and ask: do I really need new infrastructure, or can the Postgres I already have do this more simply and more safely?

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. Can Postgres handle durable workflows?

Yes. Postgres guarantees ACID transactions and on-disk durability, so combining a job table with SKIP LOCKED, idempotency keys, and retry columns yields a reliable job queue and workflow engine. For small-to-mid services in the tens-to-thousands TPS range, it handles most background work without a separate broker.

Q2. Do I need Kafka, or can I use Postgres?

Kafka fits streaming above tens of thousands per second, multi-consumer-group fan-out, and long-retained event logs. But ordinary work like email, post-payment processing, and notifications is well served by a Postgres queue. Start with Postgres and move when limits appear.

Q3. What is SELECT FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED?

A Postgres locking option that, when multiple workers grab jobs at once, skips rows already locked by another worker and takes the next unlocked row instead. It enables contention-free parallel distribution with no lock waits or duplicate processing.

Q4. When should I use Temporal instead of Postgres?

Use Postgres when workflows are simple, you lack staff to run a separate cluster, and you already use Postgres. Choose Temporal for long-running multi-day workflows that need complex branching, compensation, and deterministic replay.

Q5. How reliable is a Postgres job queue?

Bundle job enrollment with the business transaction and job loss disappears structurally. Add idempotency keys to neutralize duplicates and retry plus dead-letter to manage failures, and you reach reliability on par with a message broker. The transaction is the foundation of durability.
