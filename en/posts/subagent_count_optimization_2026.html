How Many Subagents Is Enough

Designing agent count, seen through a roster that grew to 22 and was cut back to 17

Jul 28, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: adding one more agent is far too easy

Creating an agent definition takes minutes. You give it a name, write one line about what it does, pick the tools it may touch, and attach a system prompt. Repeat that every time a new workflow appears and each individual decision looks perfectly reasonable on the day you make it. The trouble arrives only after those decisions accumulate. At some point you start hesitating over which definition should handle a request, and then you discover that two of them describe the same job in different words.

In July 2026 a developer published their agent operating rules as a repository and included a case study on growing the roster to 22 definitions and then cutting it to 17. The interesting part is not the reduction itself but something close to a confession near the top of that document: no record was written at the time of the consolidation, so the reasons had to be reconstructed three months later. Nobody felt the need to document why a definition was added, and nobody documented why it was folded either. That is the clearest possible sign that the count was never designed. It was a residue of decisions rather than a decision in itself.

One caveat before going further. This is a single practitioner's operational account, accumulated while running many projects alone, and it does not transfer cleanly to organizations with different sizes or workloads. There is nothing universal about the number 17. What this article is actually about is not the number but two questions that generalize: what genuinely degrades when the count rises, and on what evidence do you decide to fold something back. The degradation of selection accuracy as candidates multiply is not one person's anecdote. It has been measured repeatedly, and from that point onward the discussion can rest on evidence rather than intuition.

1. From 22 to 17: start by looking at what was folded

The five definitions that disappeared were handled in noticeably different ways. That difference matters, because when the reason for folding differs, the method has to differ too. Removing something unused and merging something duplicated share neither the same evidence nor the same aftercare.

Folded definitionDispositionRecorded reason
Content generationReplaced by two skillsFixed procedure, repetitive work needing no contextual judgment
Security reviewMerged into code auditBoth read-only, with overlapping scope
Cron monitoringMerged into health checkChecking cron execution is part of assessing server health
PlanningMerged into executionKeeping them separate produced no practical benefit
External chatHibernated rather than deletedSeventy days of zero traffic, resumable on demand

Read the table down its middle column and one thing stands out. Four of the five were duplication or misclassification, and only one was genuine disuse. The mental image most people carry into a conversation about agent count is unused definitions piling up in a closet, but that is not where the cost came from. The expensive ones were being used. They were just being used alongside something that did nearly the same thing.

The distinction changes the remedy. An unused definition at least sits quietly, while an overlapping one enters the candidate set on every request and clouds the judgment. So framing cleanup as headcount reduction points in the wrong direction. The goal is to make the candidate set mutually distinguishable, and a smaller count is a byproduct of achieving that, not the objective.

One sentence from the case study is worth keeping. When two definitions overlap within the same domain at the same permission level, the cost of maintaining the separation exceeds its benefit. The qualifier about permission level is doing real work there. If the domains overlap but one side is read-only while the other can write, that split is not a division of labor. It is a safety mechanism, and the reason to keep it survives the audit.

2. Why the count grows: it accumulates rather than gets decided

The recurring phrase in the retrospective is that the author created one more whenever one seemed needed. That reads less like an admission of laziness and more like a description of structure. There was no gate to pass on the way in, and there was no trigger at all on the way out.

An asymmetry in how costs appear drives the rest. Laid out plainly:

  • The cost of adding is immediate and small — one file, a few minutes. Almost no visible expenditure.
  • The real cost of adding arrives later — one more candidate at selection time, one more description occupying context, one more set of rules to keep current.
  • The cost of folding is immediate and large — you have to touch something that works, migrate the absorbed rules, and accept the risk of regression.
  • The benefit of folding arrives later and diffused — selection gets slightly more accurate and the maintenance surface shrinks slightly. Neither is felt at any single moment.

Immediacy and magnitude are arranged in exact opposition, so left alone the count increases monotonically. What is needed here is not a ceiling. A numeric rule such as no more than ten invites workarounds the moment an eleventh is genuinely warranted, and it leaves three overlapping definitions untouched in the meantime. What is needed instead is a decision sequence to pass when creating and a set of signals that tell you when to fold. The second half of this article covers both.

3. First cost: as the count rises, choosing correctly gets harder

Of all the costs of a growing roster, selection accuracy is the one that has been measured most carefully. Picking the right candidate out of a set turns out to get harder as the set grows, and this has been reported repeatedly across independent studies.

A June 2026 study of enterprise agent routing scaled the candidate pool from 10 up to 110 while holding the requests constant. The result was unambiguous. When requests were not sufficiently specific, routing F1 dropped by 16 to 23 percentage points across every model tested. The decomposition is even more instructive. The authors split the loss into a retrieval gap, where the right candidate never surfaces, and a confusion gap, where it surfaces and still loses. Even the oracle ceiling that assumes perfect retrieval fell by 10 percentage points. Handing the model the correct candidate is not enough, because telling the candidates apart is itself the hard part.

The tool side shows the same direction. A 2025 study proposing retrieval-based preselection reported that exposing a large tool catalogue all at once left selection accuracy at 13.62%, while narrowing candidates by semantic search first raised it to 43.13%. In the same experiment prompt tokens fell by more than half. Showing everything was a loss on both accuracy and cost.

ObservationConditionReported change
Routing F1 decline10 to 110 candidates, under-specified requestsDown 16 to 23 percentage points
Residual loss under perfect retrievalOracle ceiling with the right candidate suppliedDown 10 percentage points
Recovery via shortlistingEmbedding-based preselectionUp 10 to 11 percentage points
Tool selection accuracyFull exposure versus retrieval preselection13.62% to 43.13%

Transplanting these numbers directly onto 17 versus 22 would be an exaggeration. Those experiments scaled candidates into the dozens and hundreds, and there is no evidence that a roster of roughly twenty definitions suffers a decline of the same magnitude. What travels is not the absolute figure but the direction and the mechanism. First, more candidates make choosing harder. Second, that difficulty is not fully removed by better retrieval. Third, what amplifies it is less the raw count than how similar the candidates are to one another.

The third point is the most useful in practice, because it implies that eight near-identical definitions can be worse than twenty clearly distinct ones. Counting definitions is therefore secondary. Counting distinguishability comes first.

4. Second cost: tokens and latency

The second cost shows up on the invoice. An engineering write-up from a team that built a multi-agent research system offers rough multipliers: agents use roughly four times the tokens of a chat interaction, and multi-agent systems roughly fifteen times. The same document also reports the case where that spend pays for itself, describing a configuration with a lead model and subordinate models that outperformed the single-model baseline by 90.2% on their internal research evaluation.

Put the two figures side by side and the conclusion becomes conditional. The same write-up names three favorable conditions: heavy parallelization, information exceeding a single context window, and interfacing with numerous complex tools. It states the opposite just as plainly, noting that the approach does not suit domains where everyone must share the same context or where dependencies between participants run deep, and it explicitly places most coding work in that category. The reason so many attempts to split a coding agent into several get reverted is contained in that sentence.

Latency gets discussed less than tokens but is felt more. Delegation is a round trip. A subagent begins from a clean slate in its own context window, which is exactly the property that keeps the main conversation uncluttered and, simultaneously, the reason it must reassemble the context it needs from scratch. It rereads files, rebuilds situational awareness, and returns a summary. Attach that round trip to a short task and the overhead dominates the work.

The way a growing count feeds this cost is combinatorial rather than multiplicative. In order:

  • Each added definition puts one more description permanently in front of the router — small individually, but read on every selection decision.
  • The more similar the candidates, the higher the misrouting probability — exactly as section 3 describes.
  • Each misroute costs one full round trip — the wrong agent gathers context, returns an off-target summary, and the work is reissued.
  • So the felt cost is misroute probability multiplied by round trip cost — which is why heavier delegation environments should be more conservative about count.

5. Third cost: overlapping roles create failure modes

The third cost is the least visible. Overlapping roles do not merely spend a little extra. They manufacture new categories of failure.

A study that systematically classified multi-agent failures addresses this directly. The researchers hand-annotated 150 execution traces to derive fourteen distinct failure modes, grouped into three categories: system design issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification failures. Inter-annotator agreement was reported at a kappa of 0.88, and a follow-up dataset expanded to more than 1,600 traces across seven frameworks. The line that recurs in its conclusions is that a large share of failures originates in system design rather than model capability. In other words, some failures survive an upgrade to a better model.

There is a concrete illustration in the public record too. The multi-agent research write-up mentioned earlier reports that short, vague instructions caused subagents to repeat one another's searches. One would dig into a historical episode while another investigated the current situation, producing waste that was simultaneously misaligned and duplicated. The remedy they adopted was to send each task an objective, an output format, guidance on which tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries. Note that boundaries is what appears as the fix. The answer to role overlap is almost always a boundary sentence.

The industry has not converged on a single position here. On one side sits the strong claim that most people should not build multi-agent systems at all, on the grounds that splitting context splits decisions, split decisions conflict, and a single-threaded agent carries you further than expected. On the other side sits the summary that multi-agent designs do work when the task is read-heavy, parallelizable, and valuable enough to pay for the overhead, with an explicit caveat that write-heavy work is riskier because outputs collide.

There is no need to reconcile the two. What matters when translating either into practice is not which side is right but which condition your work falls under. Is it reading or writing? Does it parallelize? Must everyone share the same context? Answer those three and the split-or-not decision largely follows.

6. Criteria for creating, and criteria for not creating

From here the article turns into judgment tooling. First, deciding whether to create a definition at all. The default that the case study proposes is blunt: it opens by saying that in most cases the answer is not to create one. That default exists because of the asymmetry in section 2. Not creating is the easier decision to reverse.

SituationVerdictAlternative
Procedure is fixed and no situational judgment is neededDo not createDemote to a skill or a script
Same domain and same permission level as an existing definitionDo not createExtend the existing definition
Needed exactly onceDo not createHandle it inline as an instruction
You keep launching the same worker with the same instructionsCreatePromote the repeated instruction into a definition
Side exploration floods the main conversation with logsCreateIsolate in its own context and receive only the summary
Permissions need to be narrowedCreateA read-only, verification-only definition
You want cheaper or faster model routingCreateA separate definition with the model pinned

The top three rows and the bottom four are different in kind. The top are reasons not to create and the bottom are reasons to create, and those four map closely onto the benefits the official documentation attributes to subagents: preserving context, enforcing constraints, reusing configuration, and controlling cost. Turned around, a definition that matches none of those four is hard to justify.

In practice the following sequence resolves most cases.

  1. Is the procedure fixed? — If so, stop here and demote it to a skill or script. Handing work with no autonomous judgment to an agent spends tokens on the performance of deciding.
  2. Does it overlap an existing definition? — If the domain and permission level match, extension is the answer. Creating instead guarantees a hit on the overlap checks below.
  3. Does it match one of the four benefits? — Context preservation, constraint enforcement, reuse, cost control. Any one of them is a legitimate reason.
  4. If none apply, do not create it — Because it might be nice to have is not a reason.
  5. If genuinely unsure, create it as temporary with an expiry — The case study parks provisional definitions in a separate path with a date and judges promotion or deletion two weeks later. A temporary thing without an expiry becomes permanent.

7. How to detect overlapping roles

Overlap does not reveal itself at creation time. It surfaces after accumulation, which is why a periodic sweep is needed. Four checks, ordered from cheapest to most involved.

7.1 The human test: if a person cannot decide in three seconds, neither can the model

Place the two description lines side by side and route five recent requests by hand. If even one request causes hesitation, that pair is an overlap candidate. Tool design guidance makes the same argument, observing that when capabilities overlap or purpose is vague, confusion about which one to invoke follows. There is no basis for expecting a model to split a boundary that a human cannot.

7.2 Vocabulary overlap in the descriptions

Extract only the verbs and their objects from each description and list them. If review, inspect, audit, and check are all attached to the same object, those are functionally the same job. In practice this check trips surprisingly often, because definitions usually start from genuinely different concerns and then lose that distinction the moment the concern is compressed into a sentence.

7.3 Subset relationships between tool and permission sets

Treat the allowed tool lists as sets and compare them. If one is a subset of the other, the narrower one is a candidate for absorption. The exception noted earlier applies here, though. If the narrower one is read-only while the broader one can write, that containment is not duplication but an intentional safeguard. Keeping verification separate from execution has an independent justification.

7.4 Patterns visible in invocation logs

Where logs exist, they hold the strongest evidence. Look for three patterns.

  • Pairs that always fire back to back in the same session — likely one job split in two, doubling the round trip for nothing.
  • A human immediately invoking the other one right after the first runs — the most direct signal that automatic selection chose wrong.
  • Definitions a human always names explicitly — meaning the description is not functioning as routing input at all.

8. A checklist for merging and retiring

Having found overlap, the next question is how to process it. The verdicts by situation:

Observed situationVerdictAction
Fixed procedure, no judgment requiredDemoteMove to a skill or script and archive the definition
Same domain, same permission levelMergeAbsorb into the more accountable side, migrating every rule
Overlapping domain, different permissions (read vs write)KeepSeparation is a safeguard; strengthen the boundary sentence only
Extended period with zero callsHibernateArchive rather than delete, and record the reason
Frequent misroutingHoldRewrite the description first and observe; merge if it persists
Used only by its authorHoldDocument the usage conditions or fold it
A temporary definition still residentJudgeSet a deadline and either promote or delete

Order matters when actually performing a merge. Combining the operating rules from the case study with ordinary practice gives five steps.

  1. Absorb into the more accountable side, not the more privileged one — choosing the absorber by breadth of permissions drags permission expansion along with it. Choose by who owns the outcome in that domain.
  2. Migrate every rule sentence from the folded side — this is where merges leak most often. Deleting a definition also deletes the exception handling and prohibitions living inside it, and that loss returns weeks later as a regression.
  3. Rename into an archive instead of deleting — when version control records it as a rename, history survives and a recovery path remains. The cost gap between needing something you deleted and not needing something you archived is wide.
  4. Record the reason at the moment you fold it — the case study document exists precisely because this rule did not. Reconstructing three months later loses the details.
  5. State explicitly whether restoration is permitted — without that marker, the definition quietly comes back the next time it looks useful, and the duplication you just removed returns with it.

Three of the five steps are about record-keeping, which is telling. The difficulty of merging is not technical. The real cost is that without a written trail of what was folded and why, you end up making the same judgment again from scratch.

9. One description line decides the selection

The description field is not documentation for humans. It is routing input. The official documentation is explicit: delegation happens when a task matches a subagent description, and the best-practice list includes the instruction to write detailed descriptions. It goes further, suggesting phrasing that encourages proactive use when you want delegation to happen without being asked. Write it carelessly and the definition exists but is never chosen.

The same principle is observed on the tool side. Tool design guidance reports that even small refinements to descriptions produce dramatic improvements, and recommends namespacing to make boundaries visible. Five working rules follow.

  • Write when to call it rather than what it does — reviews code helps routing not at all. Use immediately after code is modified does.
  • State triggers concretely — right after, on failure, before deploy. A moment in time simplifies the judgment.
  • Cut overlap with an exclusion sentence — a single line saying what this definition does not cover creates the boundary against its siblings. It is the cheapest available remedy for overlap.
  • Do not let two definitions share the same verb — if review, audit, and inspect all attach to the same object, the vocabulary needs splitting. Words that confuse a human confuse the router.
  • When misrouting appears, fix the description before the model — reversing that order means running the expensive experiment first.

One more distinction: writing a long description and writing a detailed one are not the same thing. Detail means more information that feeds the judgment, and length is merely a side effect. A description that grew longer by accumulating adjectives actively blurs the boundaries between candidates.

10. Maintenance signals: what tells you it is time to fold

Folding needs triggers. The signals worth checking on a schedule:

SignalHow to read itFirst action
Zero calls over a long windowNo demand, or its existence has been forgottenRevise the description once and extend the window
Always invoked by explicit nameAutomatic selection is not workingRewrite the description with concrete triggers
Another definition invoked right afterThe selection was wrongRedefine boundaries or merge
Two definitions editing the same filesThe permission boundary has collapsedConsolidate write access on one side
Rules conflicting with another documentThe single source of truth has brokenSwitch to referencing the shared document
Temporary definition resident with no expiryThe promotion decision was skippedSet a deadline and promote or delete

One trap deserves attention here. Absence of calls must not be read immediately as absence of need. As section 9 argues, a weak description means the definition exists without ever being selected. So zero traffic can be evidence of no demand or evidence of a failed description. Separating the two requires the procedure of revising the description once and extending the observation window before folding. The choice to hibernate rather than delete the seventy-day-silent definition in the case study reads the same way.

Review cadence will vary by organization, but splitting signals by type makes it manageable. Log-derived signals can be extracted automatically, so run them often; signals like description overlap or rule conflicts require reading and are better swept quarterly. What matters is not the length of the interval but putting a place on the calendar where folding decisions are allowed to happen. Without that place, folding never happens at all.

11. So how many is right?

This question does not have a numeric answer. Nothing here implies that 17 is correct, and even the conclusion that 22 was excessive holds only under that person's conditions. Change the conditions and the appropriate count changes with them.

  • With more people, a higher count is natural — different owners need different definitions for maintenance to work at all. That structure differs from one person holding everything.
  • Where separation of duties is a compliance requirement, merging becomes the violation — in environments that must split verification from execution, apparent overlap is not a reason to combine.
  • Where parallel read work dominates, the bottleneck is concurrency, not count — reducing definitions changes nothing you can feel.
  • Where domains divide cleanly, a large count causes little harm — as section 3 showed, similarity rather than count is what creates the difficulty.
  • The heavier the round trip, the more conservative you should be — expensive delegation makes wrong choices expensive too.

What stays constant regardless of count is the set of verdicts. Demote fixed procedures into skills, merge matching domain and permissions, suspect the description before concluding disuse, and fix descriptions before swapping models. Those four sentences apply whether you hold five definitions or fifty.

If you insist on a ceiling, there is a better criterion than a number: can one person look at the whole list and explain each definition's boundary without stumbling? The instant someone says this one is kind of like that one, that is the place to clean up. The strength of this criterion is that it yields different answers for different organizations, and that is not a defect. It is a reflection of the fact that the question was context-dependent to begin with.

Conclusion: folding is harder than adding

This article started from the numbers 22 and 17, but it does not end at a number. What the case really exposes is a structural fact: there was no gate on the way in and no trigger on the way out. So the count was accumulated rather than chosen, and the reasoning behind the folding had to be reconstructed three months after the fact.

Changing that structure takes three things. First, a decision sequence at creation time: demote fixed procedures to skills, extend rather than duplicate when domains match, and create only when one of the four benefits applies. Second, a periodic overlap sweep: the human test, vocabulary overlap, tool-set containment, and log patterns catch most of it. Third, record-keeping rules for folding: write the reason at that moment, archive by renaming rather than deleting, and mark whether restoration is permitted.

It is also worth separating what the evidence supports from what it does not. Selection accuracy declining as candidates multiply has been observed repeatedly, and so has the partial recovery from narrowing candidates before the choice. What remains unverified is the size of that decline at a roster of roughly twenty definitions. No public measurement at that scale turned up during research for this article. So this article recommends no specific number. The honest formulation is different: count is an observation rather than a target, and what needs managing is the distinguishability between candidates.

As of July 2026 the tooling around subagents keeps making definitions easier to create. The easier creation gets, the more important it becomes to establish the rules for folding first. The line from that case study, that scaling up was easy and deciding what to fold was hard, reads like a sentence more people will meet as the tools improve.
