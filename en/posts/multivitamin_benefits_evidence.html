Multivitamins and the Evidence - Why the Answer Changes With the Outcome You Measure

This topic produced not one answer but a different answer in each column. What was reported for mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer and cognition, why people with and without a deficiency are asking different questions, and the conditions under which harm was reported

Aug 16, 2026 Health & Wellness
Stated up front: this article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Whether to take any supplement should be decided in consultation with a doctor or pharmacist. It is not written to promote or discourage any product. Every dose mentioned below is a value used in the trial or public recommendation being described, not intake guidance directed at any individual. If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, currently smoking, living with a chronic condition, or taking any medication, speak with a doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement.

Introduction: where the idea of taking one as insurance actually stands

Ask people why they take a multivitamin and one answer comes back more than any other. Nothing hurts, exactly, but the diet is not perfect, so just in case something is missing, one tablet covers it. The word insurance follows naturally. Nothing to lose, possibly something to gain.

That calculation contains two testable claims. One is that there is a benefit. The other is that there is no cost. What makes this compound unusual is that both have actually been asked. Most supplements rest on a handful of short trials in a few dozen people. Multivitamins have trials that randomized tens of thousands of people and followed them for over a decade.

The result is more interesting than either slogan. It was not one answer. The answer changed depending on which outcome was measured. In some columns the question is close to settled, in others it is still contested, and under certain conditions harm was reported. Neither it works nor it is useless summarizes this evidence accurately.

So this piece neither recommends nor discourages. The aim is to separate the columns and show which claim sits on which height of evidence. If you want individual compounds sorted the same way, the arginine piece and the boswellia piece make useful comparisons.

1. What makes this different from other supplements - people were actually asked

Supplement discussions usually fail to reach a conclusion because the evidence sits low. Most of it is cell culture and animal work, and where human data exist they often involve a few dozen people over a few weeks. From there, you cannot say much beyond the mechanism is plausible.

Multivitamins are different. Since the 1990s they have been treated as a primary prevention question in public health, and that brought publicly funded, large randomized trials into existence. The scale alone shows the difference in layer.

  • Physicians Health Study II — 14,641 US male physicians, mean age 64.3, randomized to a multivitamin (Centrum Silver) or placebo and followed for a median of 11.2 years. Funded by US National Institutes of Health grants; study agents and packaging were supplied by BASF and Pfizer, and the report states the funders had no role in design, conduct or analysis.
  • COSMOS — 12,666 women aged 65 and over plus 8,776 men aged 60 and over, 21,442 people in total, in a two-by-two factorial design testing cocoa extract and a multivitamin, followed for a median of 3.6 years. Primary funding came from Mars Edge, and the multivitamins were donated by Pfizer and GSK Consumer Healthcare.
  • Pooled analysis of three US cohorts — not randomized, but very large. US National Cancer Institute researchers followed 390,124 generally healthy adults for more than 20 years, reported in JAMA Network Open in 2024.
  • US Preventive Services Task Force — its 2022 statement re-examined the evidence including 52 studies published since the 2014 review.

The funding is worth recording deliberately. COSMOS involved manufacturer money and donated product; Physicians Health Study II was built on public research funding. Both were double-blind and randomized, which is a genuinely strong design, but who paid for what is worth carrying into the reading. It matters most when the cognition results come up.

2. The evidence ladder - the answer differs by outcome

Claims about the same product sit at very different heights. Sorted by outcome, the picture looks like this.

Read the table vertically and something stands out. It is not that the topic has no answer; it is that each question has its own answer. Asking whether multivitamins are good for you is a question shaped so that it cannot receive one.

3. Mortality, cardiovascular disease and cancer - the three columns asked at the largest scale

These are the columns the insurance metaphor aims at most directly: living longer, protecting the heart, avoiding cancer. They are also the columns examined with the largest samples.

3.1 Mortality and cardiovascular disease - two different designs reached the same answer

COSMOS followed 21,442 people for a median of 3.6 years, totalling 77,331 person-years. Composite cardiovascular events occurred in 429 people on the multivitamin and 437 on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.98 (95% CI 0.86 to 1.12). Total mortality gave a hazard ratio of 0.93 (0.81 to 1.08); the point estimate pointed downward, but the confidence interval comfortably crossed one. Physicians Health Study II separately reported no effect on major cardiovascular events. What defines this column is that two independently designed trials pointed the same way.

The observational evidence did not diverge. The 2024 JAMA Network Open analysis followed 390,124 generally healthy adults in the NIH-AARP, PLCO and AHS cohorts for over 20 years and found no reduction in total mortality among daily users, nor any difference in deaths from cancer, heart disease or cerebrovascular disease.

Why this column is unusually solid: three things overlap. First, samples in the tens to hundreds of thousands give the power to detect even small differences. Second, two different designs, randomized trials and long-running cohorts, arrived at the same conclusion. Third, death is hard to miss as an outcome, so there is little room for measurement error.

One trap is easy to fall into here. The people in these trials were mostly adults whose nutritional status was not poor. COSMOS participants were 60 and older but free of major cardiovascular disease and recently diagnosed cancer; Physicians Health Study II enrolled US male physicians. The question asked was what happens when you add more to people who are already replete. What happens when you add to people who are short was not asked.

3.2 Cancer - a small difference that appeared once, and what happened next

Cancer is the only column where a result reached statistical significance in the direction of benefit, which is why it gets cited. That makes it worth reading the numbers precisely.

In Physicians Health Study II the hazard ratio for total cancer was 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.998, P=0.044. The upper bound of that interval is 0.998, essentially touching one: a result that crossed the significance threshold by a hair. In the same paper prostate cancer showed a hazard ratio of 0.98 (0.88 to 1.09, P=0.76), no difference, and cancer mortality 0.88 (0.77 to 1.01, P=0.07), directional but not significant. No site-specific cancer showed a clear reduction.

COSMOS then asked the same question. Invasive cancer occurred in 518 people on the multivitamin and 535 on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.97 (0.86 to 1.09), P=0.57. It did not replicate. One caveat travels with that: COSMOS ran for a median of 3.6 years against 11.2 in Physicians Health Study II, and cancer is an outcome that takes time to surface.

The USPSTF issued an insufficient evidence (I) statement for multivitamins in 2022, which reflects exactly this situation. It means the data are not adequate to weigh benefits against harms. It does not mean a finding of no effect. Summaries routinely blur those two, and they are not the same statement.

4. Cognition - the column where answers have diverged most

Multivitamins returned to the news almost entirely because of this column, and it is also the one to read most carefully. Three substudies within COSMOS examined cognition, they used different assessment methods, and they produced somewhat different results.

  • COSMOS-Mind — participants aged 65 and over, assessed by telephone over three years. Global cognition scores were higher than placebo, and the investigators described the difference as equivalent to slowing cognitive ageing by roughly 1.8 years across the three years. This substudy was funded by the US National Institute on Aging.
  • COSMOS-Web — 3,562 participants assessed with a web-based neuropsychological battery over three years. The primary outcome, immediate recall, was better than placebo at one year and on average across three. However, memory retention, executive function and novel object recognition showed no difference.
  • COSMOS-Clinic — 573 participants assessed in person. The mean difference in global cognition over two years was 0.06 standard deviation units (95% CI -0.003 to 0.13), which did not reach statistical significance.
  • Meta-analysis of all three — across roughly 5,000 non-overlapping participants (573 plus 2,158 plus 2,472), global cognition differed by 0.07 standard deviation units, P=0.0009. The investigators interpreted this as about two years of cognitive ageing.

There is also evidence pointing the other way. A 2013 report in Annals of Internal Medicine from the Physicians Health Study II cognition substudy assessed 5,947 men aged 65 and older by telephone up to four times over as long as 12 years and concluded that the data do not support multivitamin use to prevent cognitive decline. That is a considerably longer window than the three years in COSMOS.

What to hold alongside this column: First, the effect size is 0.07 standard deviation units. Statistically significant and noticeable in daily life are different claims, and a phrase like two years is an interpretation, not a measurement. Second, the substudy using in-person testing did not reach significance, meaning this is a range where changing the instrument changes the result. Third, the COSMOS parent trial received manufacturer funding and donated product; being randomized and double-blind does not erase that. Fourth, if cognitive decline is the actual concern, other things come first. Correctable causes such as vitamin B12 deficiency genuinely exist, and that is a matter for testing.

5. People with and without a deficiency are not asking the same question

One distinction runs under every column above. Miss it and the results get summarized backwards.

What the large trials asked was whether generally well-nourished adults who take extra get less chronic disease. That answer was largely neutral. It says nothing at all about what happens when someone genuinely short of a nutrient replaces it. The second question usually has a clear answer, and it belongs to treating deficiency rather than to the supplement debate.

The second group is not rare either. In Korea, the 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey classified 17.9 percent of people aged one and over as having inadequate intake, defined as calcium, iron, vitamin A and riboflavin all below the estimated average requirement. That share rose from 8.4 percent in 2014, a 2.1-fold increase in a decade, and it clusters in particular groups: 23.5 percent of teenage boys and 25.6 percent of women in their twenties.

5.1 Situations where checking comes first

What follows is not a list of reasons to take a supplement, but a list of situations where checking should replace guessing. Either way the decision belongs to you and your clinicians.

  • Absorption itself is blocked — after stomach or small bowel surgery, or with atrophic gastritis, what you swallow and what you absorb diverge.
  • A medicine depletes a specific nutrient — long-term metformin can lower vitamin B12, and American Diabetes Association guidance suggests considering periodic assessment, particularly where anaemia or peripheral neuropathy is present.
  • An entire food source is absent from the diet — a fully plant-based diet removes the main dietary source of vitamin B12.
  • Sun exposure is structurally limited — vitamin D deficiency is commonly documented in Korea as well.
  • Total intake is simply low — dieting, older age or illness reduce overall food volume, and several nutrients fall together.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy — covered separately below, because that situation sits in a different column.

What they share is that none can be settled by reading an ingredient panel. Whether you are short is established through symptoms and testing, which makes the practical next step a consultation rather than a product comparison.

5.2 A situation in a different column - folic acid before pregnancy

Having repeated that the evidence is unsettled, fairness requires naming precisely where it is not. Folic acid around the time of conception sits at a different level of evidence from anything else in this article.

The MRC Vitamin Study, published in The Lancet in 1991, was a factorial randomized trial at 33 centres in seven countries. It enrolled 1,817 women at high risk because of a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect and assigned them to folic acid, other vitamins, both or neither. Among 1,195 completed pregnancies with known status there were 27 defects, 6 in the folic acid arms and 21 in the others: relative risk 0.28 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.71), a 72 percent reduction. In the same trial the other vitamins showed no significant protective effect (relative risk 0.80, 0.32 to 1.72).

Policy followed. In its 2023 reaffirmation the US Preventive Services Task Force gave a grade A recommendation that people planning or capable of pregnancy take a daily supplement containing 0.4 to 0.8 mg of folic acid, specifying the timing as starting at least one month before conception and continuing through the first two to three months of pregnancy. The recommendation does not apply to those with a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect or at very high risk from family history or certain antiseizure medicines; those situations need individual counselling.

Two things to hold about this column: First, the figures above are values written into a public recommendation, not dosing advice from this article. Whether, what and when to take is settled in obstetric care. Second, this evidence is that one specific nutrient prevents one specific outcome at one specific time. It is not evidence that multivitamins in general are beneficial. That the same trial found no effect for the other vitamins makes the distinction plainly.

The pattern generalizes. The narrower the specification of population, nutrient and outcome, the firmer the evidence; the broader the claim about everyone, everything and health in general, the blurrier it becomes. That rule runs through the whole topic.

6. Where harm was reported - smokers and beta-carotene

Now the other half of the insurance metaphor, there is no cost. This column has actual data.

Trial Population and sample Intervention Reported result

(NEJM 1996)		 18,314 people at high lung cancer risk from smoking history or asbestos exposure Beta-carotene 30 mg plus retinyl palmitate 25,000 IU daily Lung cancer incidence up 28 percent, deaths up 17 percent · stopped early, January 1996

(Finland)		 29,133 male smokers Beta-carotene 20 mg daily Lung cancer up 18 percent, deaths up 8 percent

(JAMA 2011)		 About 35,500 healthy men Vitamin E 400 IU daily (vitamin E alone arm) Prostate cancer up 17 percent (65 to 76 cases per 1,000 over 7 years)
Cochrane systematic review
(2012, CD007176)		 78 randomized trials, 296,707 participants pooled Antioxidant supplements (beta-carotene, vitamins A, C, E, selenium) Concluded beta-carotene and vitamin E appear to increase mortality, and higher doses of vitamin A may do so

Reading this table precisely matters. These are high-dose single or paired compound trials, and they are not equivalent to the small amounts in an ordinary multivitamin. Transferring the numbers directly onto a daily tablet overstates the case.

Equally, the data do not disappear. CARET closed before schedule because the disease it set out to prevent occurred more often in the treatment arm. A follow-up six years after the intervention stopped found the differences had lost statistical significance while the direction persisted. This forms part of the basis for the USPSTF recommending in 2022 against beta-carotene and vitamin E for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer, a grade D recommendation. Notably, that is the only item in this whole topic graded in the direction of do not use.

There is a case showing how this fed back into an actual product. The AREDS formulation, designed to slow progression of age-related macular degeneration, originally contained beta-carotene. The follow-up trial AREDS2 replaced it with lutein and zeaxanthin. At ten years the replacement version performed better at limiting progression to late macular degeneration, and the observation that beta-carotene raised lung cancer risk within the trial was cited alongside as grounds for the substitution. It is a rare instance of a component being removed from a formulation on the basis of actual data.

If you smoke or recently did: check whether the ingredient panel lists beta-carotene, and discuss whether to take it with your own clinicians. This is not a judgement this article can make for you.

7. What to actually check on the label - upper limits, fat solubility, and medicines

A few axes are genuinely usable when standing in front of a shelf. Not ingredient counts or marketing copy, but the things that sit on the safety side.

7.1 Start from the fact that there is no standard

The US National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements states plainly that there is no standard multivitamin and no standard list of ingredients. Products carrying the same name differ in what they contain and how much. That means you cannot call multivitamins good or bad as a single category, and strictly speaking the trial results above apply to the specific products those trials used. That both COSMOS and Physicians Health Study II used Centrum Silver should be read in that light.

7.2 Water-soluble and fat-soluble behave differently in excess

This is where the idea that more cannot hurt breaks down. Water-soluble vitamins such as C and the B group are largely excreted in urine when in surplus. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K accumulate in fatty tissue and the liver, so sustained high intake builds up.

  • Vitamin A — the axis where accumulation matters most. Chronic excess has been associated with dry skin, joint and muscle pain, fatigue and abnormal liver test results. Preformed vitamin A as retinol is the form whose risk to a developing fetus underpins its upper limit, and beta-carotene is distinguished from that risk.
  • Vitamin D — excess can push blood calcium upward, which is why an upper limit is set.
  • Stacking products — a multivitamin plus individual vitamins plus fortified foods plus a protein supplement means the total for one nutrient rises quietly. Each label may look unremarkable while the sum tells a different story.
Upper limits genuinely exist: in Korea the Ministry of Health and Welfare and the Korean Nutrition Society set tolerable upper intake levels within the Dietary Reference Intakes for Koreans, revised on a five-year cycle under the National Nutrition Management Act. The most recent revision, the 2025 edition, reviewed 40 nutrients including 13 vitamins and 15 minerals, and set an adequate intake and upper limit for choline for the first time. This article deliberately gives no figures. What an upper limit means depends on age, condition and other sources of intake, so the right move is to read the product labelling and its precautions and consult a doctor or pharmacist.

7.3 Where medicines overlap

Interactions between supplements and drugs are not visible on an ingredient panel. Within what has been documented, the main axes are these.

  • Warfarin and vitamin K — vitamin K is a cofactor for making clotting factors and warfarin works by blocking its recycling, so the two push directly against each other. The vitamin K content of a multivitamin is generally described as below the level that disturbs anticoagulation, but there are case reports of INR falling after starting one and returning after stopping. In one prospective controlled study all nine patients whose baseline vitamin K status was low dropped below the therapeutic range, against one of seven with normal levels. In other words, the same product produces different responses depending on a person's baseline state.
  • Metformin and vitamin B12 — long-term use can lower B12, with risk reported to rise with duration and dose. American Diabetes Association guidance suggests considering periodic assessment.
  • Products containing iron — there are conditions in which iron excess is a problem, so whether iron is included is worth checking.
  • Starting or stopping — the moments when interactions actually cause trouble are more often the start and the stop than steady use. Telling whoever adjusts your medication is the operative step.

Korea also has a formal channel for this. An adverse event for a health functional food means an undesirable and unintended sign, symptom or disease suspected to have been caused by the product. The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and the Korea Food Safety Information Service collect these reports into an integrated database. Over the most recent five years, 4,168 reports were received, with the number trending upward each year. Reported symptoms were mostly diarrhoea, abdominal pain, hives, nausea and constipation, and a handful of product categories including nutrient supplement products accounted for around 60 percent of reports. In other words, rare but real.

8. Where the evidence is firmer - what comes from food, and diet and exercise

An old puzzle needs naming here. The observational evidence that people who eat plenty of fruit and vegetables are healthier is thick. Extract a compound from that observation, put it in a tablet and test it, and the benefit disappears or occasionally reverses. Beta-carotene followed exactly that path: it looked good in vegetables, and giving it at high dose to smokers produced more lung cancer.

Several explanations run alongside each other.

  • Food is not the sum of its compounds. Fibre, water and other constituents arrive together, and absorption kinetics differ.
  • High doses can behave unlike small ones. The amount in a tablet is sometimes an order of magnitude away from dietary intake.
  • Observational studies come with the people attached. People who eat more vegetables differ in other habits too. Randomization removes that, which is why benefits repeatedly evaporate in trials.

So the practical conclusion is not a passive one. For the same goals, tools with considerably firmer evidence already exist.

  • How the diet is built — the most reliable way to raise nutrient intake as a whole, and structurally unlikely to breach an upper limit. Specifics are set out separately in healthy diet and nutrition.
  • Regular physical activity — sits above supplements in evidence for cardiovascular, metabolic and cognitive outcomes alike.
  • Stopping smoking — telling, given that every harm signal in this article emerged in smoking populations.
  • Checking rather than assuming — turning in case something is missing into I checked whether something is missing makes every subsequent decision easier. More practical than any table here.
  • Sleep and alcohol — both sit ahead of supplements for cognition and metabolism.

9. What this article could not confirm

For accuracy, the gaps are collected in one place.

  • A large randomized multivitamin trial in a Korean population was not found. The trials above are mostly based in US and European populations, and results do not necessarily transfer where diet and baseline nutritional status differ.
  • Product-by-product comparison is not attempted. With no standard ingredient list, no basis for ranking products was found.
  • Whether the cognition findings hold beyond the 3.6 years of COSMOS was not established. Only that the contrary result, the Physicians Health Study II cognition substudy, ran considerably longer at 12 years.
  • Whether people in the 17.9 percent with inadequate intake see different chronic disease outcomes after correcting it with a multivitamin is beyond this article. No large trial asking that question directly was found.
  • Specific numeric upper intake levels were deliberately omitted, as values whose meaning depends on individual circumstances.

Not confirmed means neither safe nor dangerous. It means the answer is not in yet.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Will taking a multivitamin every day help me live longer?

All-cause mortality is the clearest column here, and the answer is not yes. COSMOS gave a hazard ratio for total mortality of 0.93 (0.81 to 1.08), not a statistically significant difference, and a cohort analysis following 390,000 people for over 20 years found no reduction in death overall or from cancer, heart disease or cerebrovascular disease. That is not a finding of harm, and it is a separate matter from correcting a genuine deficiency.

Q2. Is the research on memory trustworthy?

The research is real and also the least settled column. Pooling three COSMOS substudies across roughly 5,000 participants gave a global cognition difference of 0.07 standard deviation units (P=0.0009), but the 573-person substudy using in-person testing did not reach significance, and the web-based study improved immediate recall only, with no difference in memory retention or executive function. Conversely, the Physicians Health Study II cognition substudy followed 5,947 men for 12 years and did not support a benefit.

Q3. Is there any reason to take one without a deficiency?

This distinction is the core. The large trials asked about generally well-nourished adults and returned a neutral answer; they said nothing about replacing what is genuinely short. With 17.9 percent classified as having inadequate intake in Korea's 2023 survey, the second group is not rare. Whether you fall short is established by testing rather than guesswork, so decide with a doctor or pharmacist.

Q4. Should smokers avoid beta-carotene?

That high-dose beta-carotene may be harmful in smokers is a rare firm signal, and one to confirm with your own clinicians. CARET stopped early with lung cancer up 28 percent and deaths up 17 percent, and ATBC reported 18 and 8 percent. The high doses used in those trials are not the same as small amounts in a general product, but they form part of the basis for the USPSTF grade D recommendation on beta-carotene.

Q5. Are there medicines that should not be combined?

Do not judge this alone; take your medication list to a pharmacist or doctor. The best documented axis is warfarin and vitamin K, with evidence that people whose baseline vitamin K status was low are particularly sensitive. Vitamin B12 in long-term metformin users is also worth checking. Trouble more often arrives at the start or the stop than during steady use, so telling whoever manages your prescriptions is the operative step.

Conclusion

Back to the insurance metaphor. It carried two claims: there is a benefit, and there is no cost.

The first splits by outcome. For mortality and cardiovascular disease, trials of tens of thousands and cohorts of hundreds of thousands pointed the same way, and that way is no basis for preventive use. For cancer, one trial produced a small difference that barely crossed the threshold and the next did not replicate it. For cognition, recent data lean toward benefit but the effect size is small and results shift with the assessment method. Meanwhile, in tightly specified situations such as folic acid before pregnancy, the evidence is established.

The second claim fails conditionally. A trial giving high-dose beta-carotene to smokers closed ahead of schedule; fat-soluble vitamins accumulate, which is why upper limits exist; and some medicines, warfarin among them, push in the opposite direction. No cost is not a statement you can leave as a default without checking.

The question that remains, then, is not whether to take one but which column you are standing in. Short or replete. Matching a condition to avoid. Taking something that overlaps. None of those can be settled by staring longer at an ingredient panel, and once they are checked every later decision becomes easier. You are the one who decides, and what fills in the information that decision needs is clinical assessment and testing.

Stated again: this article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Whether to take any supplement should be decided in consultation with a doctor or pharmacist. The figures above are values reported in the cited trials and public recommendations, not intake guidance directed at any individual. In particular, if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy, currently smoking, taking any medication including anticoagulants, or living with a chronic condition, decide only after speaking with a professional. If you suspect an adverse reaction, stop and seek care; in Korea, the health functional food adverse event reporting channel is available.
