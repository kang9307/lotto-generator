Slack Basics — The Places You Get Stuck in Your First Week

Threads, mentions, notifications, search, huddles, and the 90-day wall

August 9, 2026 Tech & IT

The first-week walls are always the same ones

You get the invitation email, create the account, and find a sidebar already stacked with channels where messages scroll past faster than you can read them. What happens next is remarkably predictable. Someone tells you to reply in the thread, and nobody explains why posting straight into the channel is wrong. Every channel pings until you shut all notifications off, and then you miss the one mention that was actually addressed to you. You scroll for ten minutes hunting a message you definitely saw last week. And one day you search for a conversation from three months ago and nothing comes back at all.

This article covers exactly those places. There is no shortage of tours that walk down Slack's interface panel by panel, and you can read every one of them and still hit all four walls above. What separates people who fight Slack from people who barely think about it is not the number of features they know but a handful of rules. Conversation has three layers — channels, DMs, and threads. Notifications move in lockstep with mentions. Search has operators. And the free plan has a wall made of time: 90 days. Understand these four and the rest of the product is safe to learn by clicking around.

Every plan boundary, headcount and price in this article reflects August 2026, with Slack's official help center (slack.com/help) as the primary source. Slack adjusts these values regularly, so any decision involving money should be checked against the numbers on the official pricing page at the moment you decide.

Channels, DMs, threads — where you write is the first rule

Slack conversation is built in three layers. A channel is an open room organized around a topic or a team. A DM (direct message) is a private conversation with specific people. A thread is the bundle of replies hanging off one particular message in a channel. If email is your mental model, a channel is a mailing list where reply-all is the default, and a thread is the string of replies under a single subject line.

The rules for choosing between them are short.

  • New topics go in the channel body. A fresh agenda item, an announcement, the opening of a question — anything everyone in the channel should see starts here.
  • Anything that continues an existing conversation goes in its thread. Answers, additions, objections, results — all of it. The moment you answer with a new channel message, topics begin to interleave.
  • Conversations that concern only one person move to a DM. But work locked inside DMs can never be searched by the team. When a DM produces a decision the team needs, bring the conclusion back to a channel.
  • Public channels are the default. Private channels and DMs are searchable only by their participants. Work with no reason to hide belongs in public channels, where it becomes the organization's searchable memory.
  • Read the channel naming conventions. Most organizations prefix channels by purpose — announcements, teams, projects, watercooler. In your first week, click the channel topic and description before posting; it tells you what the room is for.

Why threads matter — it is structure, not etiquette

The insistence on threads can feel like fussiness. It is not a manners issue; it is a structural issue about notifications and records. Reply in the channel body and you interrupt the timeline of every person watching that channel. With just two or three overlapping conversations, the channel becomes a place where unrelated topics cross mid-sentence, and whoever tries to revisit the discussion a month later cannot reconstruct it. Reply in the thread and everything inverts: only the participants of that conversation are notified, the exchange stays bundled under the original message, and when search surfaces it later, it reads start to finish as one piece. When a thread reaches a conclusion the whole channel should hear, tick also send to channel under the reply box. And the Threads item at the top of your sidebar collects new replies from every thread you have joined, so answering in a thread never means losing track of it.

@mentions and notifications are one system

Slack's default behavior compresses into one sentence: ordinary channel messages pile up silently, and only the things that point at you make noise. Out of the box, what triggers a notification is a DM, a mention of you, and any keywords you have registered. Which means you cannot understand notifications until you understand mentions.

  • @name — notifies that person. If your message needs an answer, attach one. A question without a mention can scroll silently past the other person's screen.
  • @here — notifies only the members of the channel who are currently active. Use it when you need hands that are online right now.
  • @channel — wakes every member of the channel, away or not, off-hours or not. Reserve it for announcements everyone genuinely must see. In a channel of dozens, using it casually is the fastest way to be resented.
  • @everyone — a workspace-wide call that works only in the default channel containing everyone. As a newcomer you will essentially never need it.
  • An emoji reaction is a reply without a notification. A single check-mark emoji says received more quietly than a message saying so, and many teams have formalized exactly this as their read-receipt convention.

Seen from the other side, spending mentions carefully is what keeps the whole team's noise floor down. One person using @channel for non-urgent matters is enough to push every member toward muting the channel — after which even the genuinely urgent announcements stop landing.

Before you switch everything off — quiet without missing things

The answer to notification overload is not the master switch; it is layering. Slack stacks channel-level settings on top of global settings, and time-based settings on top of both. Work through the three layers and you arrive at a state that is quiet but reliable.

  • Narrow the global setting. In your notification preferences, move the trigger from everything to mentions and direct messages. Notifying on all new messages is sustainable only in a tiny team with two or three channels.
  • Open exceptions for the channels that matter. For the channels you truly must follow, click the channel name and set that channel alone to all new messages. Slack's official help recommends exactly this shape: narrow globally, then set per-channel exceptions.
  • Register keyword notifications. Put the spellings of your name, the services you own, and the terms of your job into your keywords, and you get alerted even when nobody mentions you. Keywords are not case-sensitive, but only exact matches trigger.
  • Set a notification schedule that matches your working hours. Outside the window you define, notifications pause automatically. Blocking after-hours pings with structure outlasts blocking them with willpower.
  • Use pause for short bursts of focus. Turning on do-not-disturb silences notifications for the period you choose, and a small indicator next to your name tells everyone else that your notifications are off.
  • Mute the channels you only skim. A channel you cannot quite leave can be muted: it stays in your sidebar without ever making a sound, and mention badges still show, so muting is not a total blackout.

Search operators — stop scrolling, start querying

Hunting old conversations by scrolling is a habit to drop in week one. A bare word in the search box sweeps the entire workspace, but an operator collapses the scope instantly. Of the search modifiers Slack's official help documents, these are the ones that earn their keep in practice.

  • Quotation marks for phrases — wrap several words in quotes to match that exact phrase.
  • A dash (-) to exclude — put it before a word to drop results containing it. Combine it with operators, as in -in: or -from:, to exclude a whole channel or person.
  • in:channel — search only inside a specific channel, or your conversation with a specific person.
  • from:name — only messages sent by a specific person.
  • with:name — search the threads and DMs that person is part of.
  • has:emoji-code — messages carrying a specific emoji reaction. If your team marks done with a check mark, this becomes powerful.
  • has:pin — only messages pinned to a conversation.
  • is:saved — only the items you saved for later.
  • is:thread — narrow the scope to messages inside threads.
  • before:date / after:date — cut the results at a date boundary.
  • on:date / during:month — results from a specific day, month, or year.
  • Combining them is the real skill. What did this person say in that channel last month — in: plus from: plus during: on one line answers it in seconds.

You do not need the whole list. If just from:, in: and the date operators sit under your fingers, the daily where-did-I-see-that tax shrinks dramatically. One caveat, covered below: on the free plan the search horizon itself is cut to the last 90 days.

Huddles — try the headphones icon before booking a room

When something has bounced back and forth three times in writing without resolving, talking is faster. Slack's tool for that moment is the huddle. Click the headphones icon at the top of any channel or DM and a live audio conversation opens right there; switch on the camera or share a screen if you need to. The difference from a meeting tool is the absence of ceremony — no link to generate, no invitation to send.

  • It starts from one icon. Open a huddle in a channel and any member of that channel can drop in, or you can invite specific people directly.
  • Participant limits depend on the plan. Per Slack's official help, the free plan allows two participants — one-to-one only — while paid plans allow up to 50, of whom 25 can have video on at once.
  • Screen sharing supports two people at the same time, which is exactly what code review or document-against-document comparison needs.
  • A huddle carries its own thread and canvas. Decisions made out loud can be captured as notes on the spot, and the record stays attached to the conversation after everyone leaves.
  • It does not replace formal meetings. Scheduled large meetings and calls with outside guests belong to whatever video-conferencing tool your organization uses. A huddle is the tool for got two minutes right now.

Status — so absence is not mistaken for silence

In an office, an empty chair is visible. On Slack, unless you say something, you are simply a person who is not answering. The status feature is the minimum device for preventing that misreading. Click your profile photo, set a status, and an emoji plus a short line appears next to your name.

  • State the reason and the return time. One line — out for a client visit, back at 3pm — lets the other person decide whether to wait or find someone else.
  • Set a duration with it. Statuses can clear themselves automatically. A lunch status still showing at end of day is precisely what teaches people to ignore statuses.
  • The green dot and the status are different things. The dot is an automatic indicator of app activity; the status is what you write. A grey dot does not block messages — they deliver, and get read later.
  • Some states set themselves. Joining a huddle attaches a headphones indicator, and in organizations with a calendar app connected, an in-a-meeting status can appear automatically.
  • Combined with do-not-disturb it becomes complete. Set a focus status and pause notifications together, and your screen-side absence carries its own explanation.

Files and canvases — from a flowing channel to a page that stays

Sharing a file is as simple as dragging it onto the message box and adding a line of context. The problem is that a channel is a flowing medium: yesterday's file is twenty messages upstream by lunch. Slack ships several devices for pinning things against the current.

  • Pins — fasten an important message or file so it opens straight from the top of the channel. Later, has:pin in search connects back to them.
  • Bookmarks — hang frequently opened external links — the schedule, the dashboard, the spec — as buttons in the channel's top bar.
  • Save for later — your private scrapbook, invisible to others. Right for stacking messages you must come back to, like a to-do list.
  • Canvas — a document attached to the channel. It does not flow away like messages; it is a page you keep editing, which suits onboarding notes, meeting minutes, and checklists — the current state of the channel. Canvases attached to channels and DMs are available on the free plan; standalone canvases, unattached to any conversation, belong to paid plans.
  • Promote long discussions into a canvas. Copy a thread's conclusion into the canvas as a habit, and even in a free workspace with the 90-day horizon, the essential information survives as a document.

The free plan's 90-day wall — know it before things vanish

Of everything on this page, this is the limit people learning Slack in a free workspace meet last and feel hardest. On the free plan, only the last 90 days of messages and files can be viewed and searched. Slack's official help states that older content is hidden — and that messages and files more than one year old are permanently deleted. So the hiding at 90 days is reversible by upgrading, but the reprieve is not unlimited. Workspace owners choose between two retention modes: keep everything for one year, or delete after 90 days.

For a newcomer the rows that matter in practice are the first two. If you work in a free workspace, these habits are your defense.

  • Copy conclusions into a canvas or an external document. Anything you will need past 90 days should not live only in channel messages.
  • Keep file originals outside Slack. A file uploaded to Slack is a means of sharing, not a vault. Keep the original in the team's document store and drop a link in Slack.
  • When search returns nothing, do not doubt your memory. On the free plan, a conversation from three months ago failing to appear is the product working as designed.
  • On a paid workspace you can forget this section — mostly. Some organizations set their own retention policies, so if old messages are missing, the cause may be policy rather than plan.

Workspaces and orgs — why there can be more than one invite link

If you joined the company Slack and are now confused about why the neighboring department's channels do not appear in your search, the missing concept is the workspace. A workspace is one self-contained space holding its own channels and members, and your Slack account joins each workspace separately. Large companies on Enterprise-grade plans often run multiple workspaces gathered under a single organization — which is why one employer can hand you several invite links, and why the sidebar lets you switch between spaces.

  • First, know which workspace you are in. The name at the top of the sidebar is your current space, and search and channel lists operate inside it by default.
  • Working with outsiders has two doors. Bring the other person into your workspace as a guest, or share a channel with their organization through Slack Connect.
  • Guests are a paid-plan feature. Per Slack's official help, single-channel guests — access to exactly one channel — are free, up to five per paid member, while multi-channel guests are billed like regular members.
  • Slack Connect shares only the channel, with each side keeping its own workspace. If the other company already runs Slack, this is cleaner to manage than issuing guest accounts.
  • Externally shared channels are visibly marked. A channel connected to another organization carries an indicator — a reminder that what you type there leaves your company. In your first week especially, check before you post.

What this article does not settle

The following items are deliberately left open. They vary by time, country, or organization settings, and only official pages or your admin console can settle them.

  • Local-currency pricing. Even Slack's Korean pricing page quotes US dollars (Pro at 7.25 dollars per person per month billed yearly, 8.75 monthly; Business+ at 15 yearly), and the billing currency and tax handling are fixed only at checkout. The official pricing page and your billing screen are the final word.
  • Whether the free plan carries a separate GB storage cap. What the official help states is the 90-day viewing horizon and permanent deletion after one year; a storage figure in gigabytes was not confirmed.
  • Whether huddles have a time limit. The participant limits (2 free, 50 paid) are documented; a duration cap was not confirmed.
  • A cap on how many canvases a free workspace can create. What is confirmed is the boundary: channel and DM canvases on all plans, standalone canvases on paid plans.
  • The plan-by-plan scope of AI features (summaries, search answers, and the like). Slack has been moving this boundary with its plan revisions, so check the official pricing page and your admin console.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Do messages older than 90 days disappear on the free plan?

You can view and search only the last 90 days; older content is hidden, and upgrading brings it back. But Slack's official help states that messages and files more than one year old are permanently deleted, so hiding is not indefinite storage. Owners choose between deleting after 90 days and keeping content for one year.

Q2. Should I reply in a thread or post a new channel message?

Anything that continues an existing conversation belongs in the thread. A new channel message interrupts everyone and interleaves topics. A thread reply notifies only the participants and keeps the conversation in one block. If the whole channel needs the conclusion, tick also send to channel under the reply box.

Q3. How do I reduce notifications without missing things?

Narrow global notifications to mentions and DMs, then open per-channel exceptions only for the channels you must follow. Register your name and the terms you own as keyword notifications so unmentioned references still reach you, and set a notification schedule so everything outside working hours goes quiet automatically.

Q4. What is the difference between @channel and @here?

@channel wakes every member of the channel regardless of away status; @here reaches only currently active members. Need online hands right now — @here. An announcement everyone must see — @channel. Need an answer from one specific person — mention that person by name.

Q5. How many people fit in a huddle?

Per Slack's official help, the free plan allows 2 participants (one-to-one) and paid plans up to 50, with 25 cameras on at once and two simultaneous screen shares. Start one from the headphones icon in any channel or DM — no meeting link required. Figures are as of August 2026.

Closing

There is not much you genuinely need to know about Slack. Continuations go in threads. Notifications move as one system with mentions and keywords. Search has operators. The free plan has a 90-day wall. And the workspace is the unit of space. Learn those five and the remaining features are safe to discover by clicking.

Reduced to two first-week tasks: first, arrange notifications in the four layers — narrow globally, open channel exceptions, add keywords, set the schedule. Second, build the habit of lifting conclusions out of the channel's current into a canvas or a document. Nearly everything people regret about Slack later traces back to bells left ringing and records left to drift away.
