GEO, Measured With a Control Group - A Real-World Case Study

Can you actually get AI to recommend you, and can you prove it? A look at a controlled GEO experiment

2026-07-02 Tech

Introduction: "Can We Get AI to Recommend Us?"

If you run a blog or a small-business website, you've likely noticed a shift. People who used to type a query into Google and click a blue link now ask ChatGPT or Perplexity something like "recommend a good X near me" and stop right there, satisfied with the AI's answer. No matter how well-crafted your content is, if the AI doesn't mention you in that answer, the click never happens. This is the problem that gave rise to GEO (Generative Engine Optimization).

The trouble is that most GEO content offers a checklist of "do this and AI will cite you" without ever showing whether it actually worked. A recent experiment shared on GeekNews (news.hada.io) tackled that gap directly. Two businesses with similar baseline exposure were picked; one received GEO treatment while the other was left untouched as a control group, and their AI-answer visibility was tracked over two weeks. This post uses that experiment as a starting point to analyze, from my own perspective, what GEO actually measures and changes — and where the measurement method itself falls short.

One clarification up front: this is not a straight report of the source experiment. I use its numbers as a launch point, cross-check the concept of GEO against industry sources as of July 2026, and work through what bloggers and small-business operators should actually apply, and how far this kind of small-scale experiment should be trusted.

1. The Experiment - What Was Measured and How

First, here is my own reconstruction of the experiment's design, not a copy of the source text.

1.1 Target and Metric

The experiment repeatedly ran 100 fixed queries of the "recommend a dental clinic in [neighborhood]" type against four generative AI systems with web search enabled — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity — and defined the mention rate as the share of answers in which a specific clinic was named. This mention rate was recalculated every two weeks to observe change over time. The key point is that the metric is not ranking or clicks, but "how often does the AI actually mention me in its answer."

1.2 The Control Group Design

The most notable part of this experiment is that it used a control group. Two clinics with similar baseline mention rates (around 11%) were selected. One underwent GEO/AEO-style content work — patient-question-specific information pages, structured data, richer staff bios — while the other received no treatment at all. Two weeks later, the treated clinic's mention rate rose from 11% to 27%, an increase of roughly 16 percentage points, while the untouched control stayed essentially flat, moving from 11% to 10%.

1.3 Why the Control Group Matters

Most GEO articles offer a single before-and-after story: "we did this, and citations went up." The problem is that AI models are constantly updated, and mention rates can fluctuate due to seasonality or unrelated buzz. Without a control, you cannot tell whether a change came from your optimization or from something else entirely. By comparing a treated site against an untreated one under the same time window and conditions, this experiment rules out much of the "overall citation rates were trending up anyway" alternative explanation. That is what sets it apart from typical GEO success stories.

2. What GEO Actually Is - How It Differs From SEO

Before going further into the experiment, let's pin down the concept. Searching for GEO turns up related terms like AEO (Answer Engine Optimization) and LLMO (LLM Optimization); the emphasis differs slightly, but the underlying goal is largely the same.

2.1 A Different Success Metric

Traditional SEO targets high rankings and clicks on a search results page. GEO instead targets being mentioned or cited the moment an AI generates its answer. Users often read the AI's answer and feel satisfied without ever clicking through, so "cited but not clicked" is a common outcome. That's why GEO's success metric shifts toward citation rate, mention rate, and share of AI voice rather than clicks.

2.2 Not a Full Replacement for SEO

Calling GEO a replacement for SEO overstates the case. Most generative AI search today relies on a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architecture: when a query comes in, the system first retrieves candidate documents from the existing web index, then selects trustworthy ones to inform its answer. That upstream retrieval step leans heavily on conventional web search and domain-authority signals, according to a consistent observation across industry sources. A page that is barely indexed or has very low domain authority may never even enter the candidate pool an AI draws from. In practice, the realistic approach is to layer GEO-specific structuring on top of a solid SEO foundation, not to treat them as competitors for the same budget.

2.3 A Different Content Format

SEO has historically emphasized long-form content, keyword density, and backlinks. GEO tends to favor short, clear definition-style paragraphs, facts organized as lists or tables, and structure made explicit through schema markup. Because AI often extracts and reuses individual passages (chunking) rather than reading an entire document, multiple GEO guides recommend writing sections that make sense independently, in short, self-contained chunks.

3. How Mention Rate Is Measured - The Methodology

The method used in this experiment is broadly consistent with how the industry approaches GEO measurement.

3.1 Repeated Measurement Against a Fixed Query Set

A representative set of queries is defined in advance — in this case, 100 combinations of location and business type — and the same queries are periodically re-run across multiple AI platforms to compare responses. Other industry sources describe similar methodologies: tens of high-frequency questions repeatedly run across multiple AI search channels (ChatGPT Search, Google AI Overviews, Perplexity, Gemini, Copilot) on a regular cadence, tallying whether the target is mentioned each time. Keeping the query set fixed is what makes comparisons across time periods meaningful.

3.2 Calculating the Mention Rate

Mention rate is typically defined as "the share of total queries in which a specific brand or page was mentioned in the response." This experiment recalculated that figure biweekly and compared it as a time series. Some approaches go further, tracking rank order or whether an actual citation link was included, but the base unit is usually a binary mentioned/not-mentioned tally aggregated into a rate.

3.3 Strengths and Blind Spots of This Method

The strength is that it's relatively easy to reproduce, and pairing it with a control group gets you closer to a causal claim. The blind spot is that AI output is non-deterministic. The same query asked twice on the same day can yield different answers, and a silent model update can swing mention rates for reasons that have nothing to do with your optimization. With a small sample (100 queries, two weeks of observation), it becomes harder to separate random noise from genuine improvement. The experiment's own author acknowledged this, explicitly labeling it a "single observation round."

4. Practical Steps - What to Actually Optimize

Here are the practices that come up consistently across this experiment and multiple GEO guides. I'm focusing on general principles rather than anything industry-specific.

4.1 Structured Content

Multiple sources agree that clear headings (H2/H3), short paragraphs, and definition-style opening sentences that lead with the key point make content easier for AI to extract and cite. Content organized as lists, tables, statistics, or quotes also appears to get reflected in AI responses more often. As a side effect, this also tends to produce content that's simply easier for a human to skim — a rare case where SEO practice and readability point the same direction.

4.2 Citable Facts and Evidence

AI models find it easier to cite sentences with clear supporting evidence. Concrete numbers, dates, and named sources are more likely to get picked up than vague claims, according to common guidance. This cuts both ways, though — it places real accountability for accuracy on the content creator. If an incorrect figure gets cited verbatim in an AI answer, the damage to credibility can spread further than a normal factual error would.

4.3 Entities and Schema Markup

It's recommended to clearly define entities — brand, people, location, product — and expose them in machine-readable form via structured data (JSON-LD) such as Article, FAQPage, or LocalBusiness. Structured data also helps with rich results in traditional search engines, so the effort pays off twice.

4.4 FAQ Format

Providing content in a question-and-answer format that mirrors how real users phrase queries is a frequently recommended GEO pattern. The logic is straightforward: since user queries are natural-language questions, content that already contains matching question-answer pairs is easier for an AI to lift directly. The FAQ section at the bottom of this article applies the same principle.

4.5 Trust Signals and Freshness

Author information, clearly stated publish/update dates, citations of authoritative external sources, and periodic content refreshes are widely observed to function as trust signals. There is also a persistent observation that AI search carries a recency bias — pages that are updated regularly appear to get selected as citation candidates more often than pages left untouched for a long time.

5. What This Means for Bloggers and Small Businesses

From here on, this is my own interpretation, beyond the source experiment. Let's look at what this trend means from the perspective of an individual blog or small-business site.

5.1 Local Service Businesses Will Feel It Most

I don't think it's a coincidence that this experiment targeted a location-based service business — a dental clinic. Queries like "best coffee shop near [station]" or "clinic near [neighborhood]" that combine location and business type are exactly the kind of query shifting quickly toward AI search. For these businesses, clearly providing structured information — location, hours, reviews, specialties — seems likely to produce visible change in a relatively short window.

5.2 Content Blogs Are a Longer Game

A general information blog like BrainDetox, by contrast, has no locality and faces far more competing content. I doubt a single-page cleanup would produce a dramatic two-week swing here. Instead, I expect the effect to accumulate more slowly, as structural consistency across the entire domain — category taxonomy, internal linking, site-wide schema adoption — builds up over time. Taking this experiment's two-week, two-sample result and generalizing it to "clean up your site and citations will double in two weeks" for blog operations would be an overreach.

5.3 Without Measurement, You're Just Guessing

The biggest lesson here is the methodology itself. It's common to make GEO changes and then conclude, based on a vague feeling, that things "seem better." Building the habit of maintaining a fixed set of test queries and periodically checking mention rates across AI platforms — as this experiment did — at least lets you verify direction for yourself. You don't need a paid tracking tool to start; writing down 10-20 representative queries and manually asking each AI chatbot, logging the results, is enough of a starting point.

6. Limits of This Measurement Method - How Much Should You Trust It?

Sharing an experiment is not the same as endorsing it uncritically. Here are the limitations that apply to this specific case, and to GEO measurement in general.

6.1 Sample and Duration Limits

This case involves two clinics observed over two weeks — a small-scale experiment. The author explicitly stated that a single observation round cannot establish causation and promised follow-up observation to check reproducibility. A control-group comparison with a sample of one cannot rule out the possibility that the result was simply coincidence. The same treatment would need to be repeated across other locations and industries, with a similar pattern recurring, before this becomes anything close to a generalized conclusion.

6.2 Non-Determinism and Model Updates

AI models are updated at times outside the user's control, and the same query can produce differently worded answers each time. It's difficult to fully separate a mention-rate change caused by content work from one caused by a shift in the model itself. Using a control group substantially mitigates this problem but does not eliminate it entirely — the comparison is only valid to the extent both subjects were exposed to the same model updates during the observation window.

6.3 Ceiling Effect for Already-Visible Sites

A site with an already-high mention rate has less room to grow further (a ceiling effect). Both subjects in this experiment started from a relatively low 11%, which left plenty of headroom. Well-known brands or large sites might not see nearly as dramatic a shift from the same treatment.

6.4 Variance Across Platforms

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity each search and cite differently, and some platforms show a tendency to favor certain sources over others. Collapsing everything into a single blended mention rate can hide platform-level variance, so breaking results out by platform gives a more accurate picture whenever possible.

Conclusion: GEO Without Measurement Is Just Marketing Copy

GEO is a real, substantive shift. It's hard to deny that AI-generated answers are replacing a meaningful share of search behavior, and that being cited (or not) inside those answers has become a new form of visibility competition. What makes the experiment discussed here meaningful is the methodology itself — measuring the effect with a control group. The treated clinic moved from 11% to 27% while the untreated clinic barely moved from 11% to 10%; at least in this one case, that gap looks more like the result of the treatment than pure coincidence.

Still, given the limits of a single sample and a two-week window, generalizing this into a rule — "do GEO work and your citation rate will more than double in two weeks" — would be premature. My conclusion is this: the practical steps of GEO (structured content, schema markup, FAQ formatting, trust signals, regular updates) are things you can apply right now with essentially no downside. But when it comes to verifying whether they worked, building the habit of measuring with a fixed query set and a control group, as this experiment did, is the only real defense against being swept up by marketing claims. Without measurement, you're left guessing — and guessing is no more reliable in the age of AI search than it ever was.
