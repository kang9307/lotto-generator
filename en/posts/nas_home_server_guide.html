How to Choose a Home NAS - The Complete Buying Guide by Spec, Not Brand

Bays, Usable RAID Capacity, CPU, Network and Power Explained

Jul 26, 2026 Tech

Introduction: Why Picking a NAS Is Unusually Hard

Search for a NAS and the first thing you get is a ranked list of specific models. The problem is that those rankings were built without knowing what you intend to do. Recommending a six-bay powerhouse to someone who only wants phone photo backup, or an entry-level box to someone who plans to stream 4K to three TVs at once, wastes money in both directions. A NAS is not a phone where the newest model is automatically the right answer. It is a device where the correct choice only narrows once you have done the capacity math and identified your bottleneck.

There is a second complication. A NAS is a machine you will leave powered on around the clock for four or five years. That means the long-run factors - expansion path, capacity you can actually use, electricity cost, and noise - shape satisfaction far more than the purchase price does. This guide recommends no brand and no specific model. Instead it lays out five decision criteria - bay count, RAID level and usable capacity, CPU and RAM, network bandwidth, and noise and power - each with the arithmetic attached, then closes with a form-factor comparison table, use-case scenarios, and a pre-purchase checklist. The goal is to make the decision for you: if this is your situation, buy A; if that is your situation, buy B.

1. Criterion One: Bay Count Buys an Expansion Path, Not Capacity

A bay is a slot for a drive. The most common mistake here is sizing the bay count around the capacity you need today. Drives can be added later or swapped for larger ones, but if you run out of bays you have to buy an entirely new enclosure. Bay count is therefore a purchase of headroom over the next three to five years, not of capacity.

A single-drive unit loses your data the moment that drive dies. For anything beyond scratch storage, the standard advice is at least two bays. Two bays in a mirror survive one drive failure, and from four bays upward a parity layout starts to balance capacity efficiency against fault tolerance.

1.1 Calculate the Capacity You Need First

Do not guess. Build the number in this order.

  • Current data: photos, video, documents and existing backups added together, in TB
  • Annual growth: how much you added over the past year. If you have no idea, use 0.1 to 0.5 TB per year for a photo-centric single user, and 1 to 4 TB per year if you shoot 4K or collect media
  • Horizon: typically five years
  • Required capacity ≈ (current data + annual growth × 5) ÷ 0.8 - that trailing 0.8 reserves the 20 percent of free space a filesystem wants

If you sit at 3 TB today and grow by 1 TB a year, then (3 + 5) ÷ 0.8 = 10 TB is your five-year usable target. Add the parity overhead described below and you get the raw capacity you actually have to buy.

1.2 Confirm the Drives Are CMR

Two drives of the same capacity can record data very differently. SMR (shingled magnetic recording) overlaps tracks for a better cost per terabyte, but throughput can collapse under random writes and heavy rewrite workloads. The catch is that a RAID rebuild is precisely a heavy rewrite workload. For continuous NAS duty, and especially for parity arrays, CMR (conventional recording) is the recommended choice. If the product page does not say, check the manufacturer datasheet for recording method alongside the annual workload rating in TB per year and the warranty term.

2. Criterion Two: RAID Level and the Real Usable Capacity

RAID groups drives to buy availability - the property that service continues when a drive dies. You pay for it in capacity handed over to parity or mirroring. For a home NAS the realistic options are these.

LevelMin drivesUsable capacityFailures toleratedBest fit
Single (no RAID)1N × D0Scratch data you can re-download anytime
RAID 1 (mirror)2D (50% on 2 bays)1Two-bay backup targets; simple to recover from
RAID 53(N-1) × D1Four bays or fewer, drives under 8 TB, backups in place
RAID 64(N-2) × D2Large drives (8 TB and up) or higher bay counts
RAID 104 (even)(N/2) × D1 per mirror pairVirtual machines and databases - random I/O
Vendor hybrid (SHR-style)2Roughly one or two drives of parity removed1 to 2Mixing drives of different capacities

In the table N is the drive count and D is the capacity of the smallest drive. Mix capacities under traditional RAID and everything is levelled down to the smallest member, so the excess is wasted. Vendor hybrid RAID schemes exist specifically to reclaim that waste.

2.1 Catalog Capacity Is Not Usable Capacity

Subtracting parity is only the first step. Three more deductions follow.

  1. Units: a vendor terabyte is 1012 bytes while the operating system reports tebibytes at 240 bytes, so roughly 9 percent vanishes on screen (a factor of about 0.909).
  2. System reserve: filesystem metadata and, on some products, a few gigabytes of per-drive system area come off the top.
  3. Free space: filling a filesystem causes fragmentation and performance decay. Copy-on-write filesystems in particular are widely recommended to keep 10 to 20 percent free.

Rolled together, a practical approximation looks like this.

usable capacity ≈ (post-parity raw TB) × 0.909 × 0.8

Worked example. Four 8 TB drives in a four-bay chassis under RAID 5 give 32 TB raw and 24 TB after parity. Multiply by 0.909 for about 21.8 TiB, then by 0.8 for free space and you land at roughly 17.4 TiB you can use comfortably. You bought 32 TB and it feels like 17. Switch the same set to RAID 6 and post-parity 16 TB becomes about 11.6 TiB. Knowing this in advance is what keeps you from running out of space within a year. If your five-year target was the 10 TB computed earlier, working backwards through a four-bay RAID 5 says each drive needs to be at least 5 to 6 TB to leave any margin.

2.2 Choosing Between RAID 5 and RAID 6

The deciding factor is rebuild time. When a drive fails you insert a replacement and the array reads every remaining drive in full to reconstruct the data. The larger the drives, the longer that runs and the more concentrated the load on the survivors. If a second drive dies mid-rebuild, or an unrecoverable read error surfaces, RAID 5 loses the entire array. Storage practitioners have argued for years that as drive capacity grows, the probability of hitting a read error while streaming an entire drive stops being negligible.

The general recommendation therefore lands here. Drives of 8 TB or larger, or five bays and up: use RAID 6 or a dual-parity hybrid. Drives under 8 TB in four bays or fewer, with a real backup in place: RAID 5 remains practical. With two bays a mirror is effectively your only option, and one cited upside is that its simple structure leaves room, in a worst case, to pull a single drive and read it in another machine.

3. Criterion Three: Size CPU and RAM by What You Will Run

3.1 CPU: Moving Files, or Converting Them?

NAS CPU demand splits into exactly two camps.

  • File sharing and backup only: if you push files over SMB or NFS and run scheduled backups, a low-power ARM chip or entry x86 part is plenty. CPU is not the bottleneck in this role.
  • Media transcoding, containers, virtual machines: if you convert codecs on the fly for clients that cannot play them, run a stack of Docker containers, or host VMs, an x86 part with a hardware video encoder and decoder built in is effectively mandatory.

For transcoding, hardware acceleration support matters more than core count. The major media server platforms officially document offloading video encode and decode to an integrated GPU, and that path handles far more streams at far lower power than software conversion on the CPU. Recent low-power x86 integrated graphics have been measured converting three to four simultaneous 4K HDR streams down to 1080p while drawing only around ten watts. Entry-level ARM designs, by contrast, can be limited in both supported codecs and available software.

That said, the best transcode is the one that never happens. When the playback device reads the original codec directly, the CPU is essentially idle. If you can standardise your playback devices, you can drop a CPU tier.

3.2 RAM: Floors by Use Case

  • 4 GB: the floor for file sharing and backup. Adding containers eats it quickly
  • 8 GB: the practical recommendation for a media server plus a handful of containers
  • 16 GB: the figure commonly cited for a four-bay box running containers seriously or using a ZFS-based filesystem
  • 32 GB and up: multiple virtual machines, or a homelab leaning hard on ZFS caching

The widely repeated 1 GB of RAM per 1 TB of storage for ZFS line is a rule of thumb, not a hard requirement. It persists because cache hit rate maps directly to performance, so more memory does help. When buying, the more consequential question is whether the RAM is replaceable. Memory soldered to the board cannot be upgraded when your workload grows.

4. Criterion Four: Networking - Where the Bottleneck Usually Is

The best drives in the world do not help if the link is narrow. Realistic ceilings by link speed look like this.

LinkTheoretical maxPractical throughputTime to move 1 TBVerdict
1GbE125 MB/sabout 110-115 MB/sabout 2.5 hoursSlower than one large drive. The floor today
2.5GbE312 MB/sabout 270-290 MB/sabout 1 hourBest perceived gain per dollar
10GbE1,250 MB/sabout 1.1 GB/sabout 15 minutesUnfillable without an SSD pool or a wide array

One number anchors all of this: a modern high-capacity 3.5-inch drive sustains roughly 150 to 250 MB per second sequentially. Gigabit ethernet cannot even deliver the performance of a single drive. At the other end, 10GbE leaves bandwidth on the table unless you stripe several drives or add an SSD cache.

Also remember that 10GbE is a three-piece set. The NAS port, the switch and the client network card all have to support it, and the cabling has to match. Controller heat and extra power draw come along for the ride. For these reasons the common conclusion is that 2.5GbE is the sensible balance unless large sequential work such as video editing is part of your daily routine. And if you will only ever reach the NAS over wireless, no amount of port upgrading helps - your ceiling is the router, so improving your mesh Wi-Fi setup comes first.

5. Criterion Five: Noise, Power and Placement

5.1 Electricity Cost Comes Down to One Formula

A NAS runs continuously, so its draw is a fixed cost. The math is simple.

annual energy (kWh) = average draw (W) × 8.76

The 8.76 is 8,760 hours per year divided by 1,000. Typical NAS draw sits in the 20 to 60 watt band, and a four-bay unit populated with hard drives is commonly reported to idle around 30 to 40 watts. As rough per-drive figures, a 3.5-inch hard drive draws 3 to 5 watts idle, 5 to 9 watts active, and 0.5 to 1 watt spun down, while an SSD sits under 1 watt idle.

Worked example. Averaging 35 watts around the clock is 306 kWh per year. At 0.15 US dollars per kWh that is roughly 46 dollars annually; at 0.30 dollars per kWh it is closer to 92. Adding two more drives typically pushes that up by another 10 to 20 dollars a year. Because many residential tariffs are tiered, an always-on load is best costed at your marginal rate rather than an average one, so take the per-kWh figure from your own bill.

The main lever for savings is drive spin-down. Parking drives during hours with no access, such as overnight, saves several watts each. Set the idle timer too aggressively, though, and you multiply start-stop cycles, so avoid very short thresholds.

5.2 Noise: The Bedroom Question

NAS noise has two sources - drive seek noise and fan noise. Datasheets for NAS-class 3.5-inch drives typically list idle acoustics in the low 20 decibel range and seek acoustics around 30 decibels. The catch is that this accumulates with drive count, and vibration at particular frequencies is far more irritating than the raw number suggests. Decide like this.

  • Bedroom or studio apartment: minimise drive count or consider an SSD build. Larger fans tend to be quieter because they move the same air at lower rpm
  • Next to the living room TV: check for fan control profiles (a quiet mode) and anti-vibration drive mounts
  • Utility room or closet: noise stops mattering, but temperature and dust become the new variables. Summer heat in an enclosed space is hard on drive life

7. Recommendations by Use Case

7.1 Photo and Document Backup Only (One or Two People)

If automatic phone photo backup and document storage is the whole job, there is no reason to overspend.

  • Bays: two, mirrored
  • Drives: two CMR drives of 4 to 8 TB (about 3 to 6 TiB usable)
  • CPU/RAM: entry tier, 4 GB minimum, 8 GB if available
  • Network: 1GbE is workable, though 2.5GbE makes the initial bulk upload much less painful
  • Caution: a mirror is not a backup. Add a second copy in the cloud or on an external drive

7.2 Family 4K Media Server

With several people streaming at once and remote access in the mix, the CPU tier is decisive.

  • Bays: four, RAID 5 with drives under 8 TB, or RAID 6
  • Drives: four CMR drives of 6 to 12 TB
  • CPU/RAM: x86 with an integrated hardware encoder, 8 to 16 GB RAM
  • Network: 2.5GbE recommended, though streaming bandwidth itself is modest - the real bottleneck is concurrent transcodes
  • Key tip: aligning playback devices so they support the source codec directly (direct play) drops the required CPU tier by one step

7.3 Homelab (Containers, VMs, Development)

  • Bays: four to six or more, RAID 6, or RAID 10 if random I/O dominates
  • CPU/RAM: multi-core x86, 32 GB or more, and check ECC support if you can
  • Storage tiers: separating NVMe as a cache or a fast pool markedly improves VM responsiveness
  • Network: the one place 10GbE genuinely pays, provided you budget for the switch and clients too
  • Expansion: the presence of a PCIe slot largely determines how you feel about the machine in five years

8. The Most Important Part: RAID Is Not a Backup

Having read this far and built a RAID 6 array, you may feel safe. But RAID guards against essentially one hazard: drive failure. In all of the following, RAID does nothing at all.

  • You delete a folder by mistake - it disappears from every drive simultaneously
  • Ransomware encrypts your files - the encrypted state is faithfully written to parity
  • A sync tool overwrites in the wrong direction - the array treats it as a legitimate update
  • Fire, flood, lightning, theft - every drive in that one box goes together
  • The enclosure or controller fails - the drives may be fine but inaccessible

Hence the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, on two different media types, with one copy physically offsite. It is safest to count the NAS and its mirrored drives as a single copy in that arithmetic, because they fail together in the same incident. A modern extension is often added on top: keep one copy immutable or offline, and verify restores until errors are zero.

A workable home combination looks roughly like this. First, the NAS itself as the working copy. Second, periodic copies to an external drive that stays physically disconnected between runs - decisive against ransomware. Third, a remote copy in the cloud or on a NAS in another building. If you are comfortable at the command line our rsync guide is a good starting point, and for comparing cloud options see the cloud backup service comparison and home server backup strategy.

One last thing. A backup you have never restored from is not a backup. Build in a routine, twice a year, of actually recovering a few files.

9. Pre-Purchase Checklist

  1. Did you compute five-year capacity as (current + annual growth × 5) ÷ 0.8?
  2. Did you carry your chosen RAID level all the way through × 0.909 × 0.8?
  3. Is there a spare bay left? Keeping at least one free makes expansion far easier
  4. Are the drives CMR, and did you check the annual workload rating and warranty?
  5. If you need transcoding, is the tier hardware accelerated?
  6. Is the RAM replaceable and upgradable?
  7. Is the network port 2.5GbE or better, or is there an expansion slot?
  8. Can your router and switch actually carry that speed?
  9. Did you run the annual cost as W × 8.76 × marginal rate?
  10. Have you accounted for noise tolerance and summer temperature at the install location?
  11. Do you have an offsite copy plan under the 3-2-1 rule?
  12. If you need remote access, do you have a plan for admin accounts, two-factor authentication, and minimising open ports?
  13. Can you live with vendor OS lock-in, or do you need portability to a standard filesystem?

10. Conclusion: The Decision, Condensed

Choosing a NAS reduces to four questions. How much will you store, what will you run, where will it sit, and can you afford to lose the data? Condensed into conditions:

  • Photo and document backup only → two bays mirrored, entry CPU, one cloud copy. Anything more is overspending
  • The family watches 4K together → four bays, x86 with a hardware encoder, 8 to 16 GB RAM, 2.5GbE. Aligning direct play lets you drop a tier
  • You run containers and VMs → four to six bays or more, multi-core x86, 32 GB RAM, an NVMe tier, and 10GbE if warranted
  • It has to live in a bedroom → cut drive count or consider SSDs, and check for fan control profiles
  • Your drives are 8 TB or larger → make dual parity (RAID 6 class) the default
  • You do not want to spend time administering it → turnkey. You want to choose the filesystem yourself → mini PC or DIY

To close, once more: RAID is an availability mechanism, not a backup. Spending half the time you devote to bay counts and RAID levels on a 3-2-1 backup plan protects your data far more effectively. A good NAS is not the one with the highest specs. It is the configuration that still has free space in five years, whose electricity bill you do not resent, and whose data exists in at least two places.

Note: The power draw, throughput, and acoustic figures in this article are general ranges drawn from manufacturer datasheets and published material; actual values vary with product generation, drive model, configuration, firmware, and environment. The electricity cost calculations are simplified estimates, so apply the per-kWh rate from your own utility bill for an accurate figure. This article recommends no specific brand or model, and you should confirm supported features and expansion limits in the official specification documents for any product before purchase. No configuration eliminates the risk of data loss, so important data must always be kept in a separate backup copy.
