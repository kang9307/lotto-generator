Aortic Aneurysm: Having No Symptoms Is the Whole Story

Who has to go looking, and what must not be missed once one is found

2026-07-29 Health & Wellness

Introduction: the absence of symptoms decides everything

In most conditions, symptoms act as the guide. Something hurts, you go to a clinic, and the clinic looks for the cause. There are conditions in which that sequence never starts. An aortic aneurysm is one of them.

The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes aortic aneurysms as balloon-like bulges in the aorta and states that they can develop and grow before causing any symptoms. Korea's national health information portal notes that small aneurysms are mostly found incidentally on a chest radiograph, without clear symptoms. The UK National Health Service compresses the point into one sentence: most abdominal aortic aneurysms do not have any symptoms, so screening is the best way to find one.

Lay those three statements on top of each other and the subject of this article appears. If there are no symptoms, then a strategy of waiting for symptoms does not exist. Only two actions remain. The first is deciding who goes looking, which is screening. The second is what happens after one is found by accident, which is surveillance.

So this article deliberately avoids becoming an encyclopaedia entry. Instead of cataloguing aortic anatomy or surgical technique, it stays on the points that can be acted on. The order is as follows: the diameter criteria, why there are no symptoms, the screening recommendation and its differences by sex, the situation in Korea, the relative size of the risk factors, growth rate and surveillance intervals, the thresholds for discussing surgery, the two repair approaches in concept, what is and is not evidence-based, emergency signs, inherited aortic disease, and a decision table.

Figures were taken from institutional and society documents wherever possible, and anything that could not be verified was left out. This is not written to frighten and not written to reassure. It puts down numbers and conditions only.

Medical disclaimer
This article is general health information and is not medical advice. It does not replace diagnosis or treatment, and nothing here should be used to judge your own condition or to decide alone whether a test or a treatment is needed. Whether to be screened, how often to be followed up, and when to treat must be decided in consultation with a doctor. 1. What an aortic aneurysm is - two criteria used together

The aorta is the largest artery, carrying blood from the heart to the rest of the body. When part of its wall stretches and bulges locally, that is an aortic aneurysm. The question is where bulging begins, and two different criteria are used side by side.

The first is a relative criterion. Korea's national health information portal explains that a segment grown beyond 1.5 times the normal aortic wall is regarded as an aneurysm. Medical reference texts likewise define an aneurysm as a permanent localized dilatation enlarging by at least 150% compared with a relatively normal diameter. This criterion exists because the aorta's normal caliber differs by segment and by person. A tall person and a short person do not start from the same number.

The second is an absolute criterion. The US Preventive Services Task Force states that an abdominal aortic aneurysm is typically defined as aortic enlargement with a diameter of 3.0 cm or larger. The 2024 European Society for Vascular Surgery guideline on abdominal aorto-iliac aneurysms uses the same line and classifies the 25 to 29 mm band separately as a subaneurysmal aorta that is not yet an aneurysm.

The distribution is worth knowing too. Korean national health information puts roughly 75% in the abdomen and 25% in the thorax. Those in the abdomen are abdominal aortic aneurysms; those in the chest are thoracic aortic aneurysms.

A common misreading starts here. Crossing 3.0 cm does not mean treatment starts. Three centimetres is the line at which observation begins, and the line at which treatment is discussed sits far above it. That gap is the subject of sections 6 and 7.

2. Why there are no symptoms - and how aneurysms are found

The aorta is wide, and the abdominal aorta lies deep, in front of the spine. When a vessel in that position stretches slowly, nothing signals until it is large enough to press on neighbouring structures. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute states plainly that an aortic aneurysm may not cause any symptoms, and where symptoms do occur it lists a pulsating or throbbing feeling in the stomach area and pain in the neck, jaw, back, chest, stomach area, or shoulder. For thoracic aneurysms it notes difficulty breathing if the trachea is pushed on and difficult or painful swallowing if the oesophagus is pushed on.

What matters is that these symptoms are either too late or too common to serve as a starting point for diagnosis. Enormous numbers of people have back pain or abdominal discomfort, and only a tiny fraction have an aneurysm. Conversely, most people who do have an aneurysm feel none of this. Filtering for this disease by symptoms does not work.

In practice the route to discovery is often accidental. The Korean portal's note about incidental discovery on a chest radiograph is one example. Another common route is an abdominal ultrasound or CT ordered for an unrelated reason that comes back with a comment about aortic diameter. That is where the second theme of this article begins: the real problem starts after the accidental finding.

People who are found incidentally usually go one of two ways. Some are told it is small, so it is fine and hear that as this is over. Others receive a follow-up schedule and postpone the next scan because nothing hurts. Both routes end the same way: years later the number is different. That is why the surveillance interval table is treated here as the practical core of the article.

One clarification. Although the word aneurysm is shared, a brain aneurysm is a separate condition with different rupture symptoms and a different screening discussion. That subject is handled separately in the guide to brain aneurysm warning signs. This article covers the aorta only.

3. The screening recommendation - who does the USPSTF advise?

If symptoms cannot act as a guide, the remaining action is to go looking first. The most widely referenced public recommendation in this area is the US Preventive Services Task Force statement on abdominal aortic aneurysm screening with ultrasonography. It carries a date of 10 December 2019 and, as of July 2026, is posted as a final recommendation with no update flagged in progress.

The key point is that this is not one recommendation but four. And the recommendation for women is clearly different from the one for men.

PopulationGradeWhat the recommendation says
Men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smokedBRecommends one-time screening with ultrasonography
Men aged 65 to 75 who have never smokedCSelectively offer screening rather than routinely screening all men in this group
Women who never smoked and have no family historyDRecommends against routine screening
Women aged 65 to 75 who ever smoked or have a family historyICurrent evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms

It matters not to misread the letters. A grade of D does not mean the test is harmful but that routine use is not advised, and a grade of I does not mean do not test but that there is not enough evidence to judge. If you fall into the I group, whether to test is a question settled by individual discussion.

3.1 Why the recommendation differs for women

The recommendation statement gives three main reasons. First, prevalence itself is lower. Population-based studies in men older than 60 found abdominal aortic aneurysm prevalence ranging from 1.2% to 3.3%, and prevalence in women is reported to be lower than in men. In one trial the figures were 1.3% in women against 7.6% in men.

Second, the harms of surgery are greater. The statement notes higher 30-day mortality rates in women along with higher rates of other harms such as major surgical complications. Screening does not end at detection but leads to treatment, so heavier treatment harms shrink the net benefit of screening.

Third, the same document makes clear that the picture for women is not simply benign. It states that in women, small aneurysms have an increased risk of rupture, and rupture occurs at an older age than in men. In other words, grades D and I for women are not a declaration that there is no risk but the output of an evidence and net-benefit calculation. That distinction should not be blurred.

One more point. The 2024 European Society for Vascular Surgery guideline took a slightly different direction. Screening, formerly advocated for all men aged 65, is now recommended only for high-risk populations, with the definition of high risk left to country-specific circumstances. Screening policy, in other words, moves with a population's smoking rate and prevalence rather than being fixed.

4. South Korea has no national screening item for this

This deserves to be stated plainly. The South Korean national health examination contains no test aimed at finding an aortic aneurysm.

The common items of the general health examination described by the National Health Insurance Service are as follows.

  • Consultation and physical examination
  • Body measurements including height, weight, waist circumference, and body mass index
  • Vision and hearing tests
  • Blood pressure measurement
  • Chest radiography
  • Blood tests covering haemoglobin, fasting glucose, liver enzymes, renal function, creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate
  • Urinalysis
  • Dental examination

Age-based additional items sit on top of that: dyslipidaemia (men from 24, women from 40, every four years), hepatitis B (age 40), hepatitis C (age 56), pulmonary function (ages 56 and 66), bone density (women at 54, 60, and 66), cognitive impairment (from 66, every two years), mental health (from 20), and physical function testing for older adults (ages 66, 70, and 80).

Nowhere in that list is there an abdominal ultrasound or a measurement of aortic diameter. The national cancer screening programmes cover stomach, colorectal, liver, breast, cervical, and lung cancer, and the aorta is not among them. Chest radiography is on the list, but it is not a test designed to screen for aortic aneurysm. As noted above, it merely happens to reveal one occasionally.

For someone living in Korea, the practical conclusions run as follows.

  1. Having attended every national health examination is not evidence that you do not have an aortic aneurysm.
  2. If checking is warranted, it has to be approached outside the national programme, through a clinical consultation or a self-funded add-on test.
  3. Whether it is warranted is not a decision to make alone; it should be weighed with a doctor against age, sex, smoking history, and family history.
  4. If a first-degree relative had an aortic aneurysm, saying so at the appointment is the single most practical step available.

How to read the other numbers on a national examination report is covered separately in the guide to interpreting health checkup results.

5. Risk factors - smoking dominates

Lists of risk factors are easy to find, but what is actually needed is how large each one is. For this condition the answer is comparatively clear.

The USPSTF statement describes smoking as the strongest predictor of abdominal aortic aneurysm prevalence, growth, and rupture rates, with a dose-response relationship. The Society for Vascular Surgery puts it more bluntly: more than 90% of patients with an abdominal aortic aneurysm have smoked cigarettes at some point in their lifetime, and the condition is second only to lung cancer in its epidemiologic association with cigarette smoking.

The numbers make the scale clearer. In a large community cohort of 15,703 people followed for a median of 22.5 years, the lifetime risk from age 45 was 5.6% overall, 8.2% in men and 3.2% in women. Split by smoking status the gap widens: 10.5% in current smokers, 6.3% in former smokers, and 2.0% in never smokers. In the same study the hazard ratio for current smoking was 7.59 (95% confidence interval 5.78 to 10.0) against never smoking.

Set beside the other risk factors, the contrast is visible at a glance.

Two things in that table need comment. First, the hazard ratio for age comes out larger than the one for smoking. But age cannot be changed. The point is that among modifiable factors, smoking dominates. Second, the inverse association reported for diabetes is an unusual finding. It certainly does not mean diabetes is beneficial; it is an epidemiological association observed for this particular condition. Diabetes raises the risk of numerous other vascular complications.

On family history, the wording of the USPSTF statement is useful. It says that a family history of aortic aneurysm in a first-degree relative doubles the risk of developing one, and adds that the size depends on the relative's sex. The odds ratio was 4.32 with a female first-degree relative, stronger than the 1.61 with a male first-degree relative. That is why it is worth naming exactly who in the family was affected.

Hypertension carries a modest hazard ratio, but it matters separately because it is modifiable. Lifestyle strategies for lowering blood pressure are set out in the evidence-based guide to lowering blood pressure, so they are not repeated here.

6. How fast does it grow, and how often should it be checked?

Aortic aneurysms usually enlarge slowly, over years. Medical reference sources put growth at 0.2 to 0.3 cm per year for small abdominal aneurysms of 3 to 5 cm, and 0.3 to 0.5 cm per year once the diameter exceeds 5 cm. In other words, the larger it is, the faster it grows.

That rate is what sets the surveillance interval. When it is small there is room before the next scan; as it grows the interval shortens. The intervals proposed by the major guidelines are below. They differ somewhat between bodies, and the interval that applies to an individual is set by the treating clinician.

The striking part of the table is that the widest disagreement sits in the 4.5 to 5.4 cm band. The English screening programme rescans it every 3 months, while the American and European guidelines propose every 6 months. This difference does not mean one side is wrong; it reflects that running a population screening programme and managing an individual patient are designed differently. The interval that applies to you is the single one your clinician set.

On imaging modality, the Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines propose ultrasound, when feasible, as the preferred imaging modality for aneurysm screening and surveillance. Ultrasound involves no radiation and is easy to repeat. CT is used when an operation is being planned or more anatomical detail is needed.

7. When surgery is discussed - diameter and growth rate

Elective repair is discussed along two axes, diameter and growth rate. And the thresholds differ between men and women.

The 2022 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology aortic disease guideline recommends repair for abdominal aortic aneurysm at 5.5 cm or more in men and 5.0 cm or more in women. The 2024 European Society for Vascular Surgery guideline holds the same line, keeping the repair threshold at 55 mm in men and 50 mm in women and adding an explicit negative recommendation against repairing fusiform degenerative aneurysms below 55 mm in men and below 50 mm in women. In other words, operating early on a small aneurysm is also not recommended.

The USPSTF statement summarises standard practice the same way: surgical repair is standard practice for men with an aneurysm of 5.5 cm or larger, or an aneurysm larger than 4.0 cm that has rapidly increased in size, while the standard of care for stable smaller aneurysms is to maintain ultrasound surveillance.

The second axis, rapid growth, can trigger a surgical discussion even when the diameter is below threshold. The widely cited criteria are more than 1.0 cm in a year or more than 0.5 cm in six months. Korean national health information likewise gives 5.5 cm or more, or an increase of 1 cm or more per year as the operative criterion for descending and thoracoabdominal aneurysms.

One caveat has to be added. These numbers do not decide surgery automatically. The European guideline states the thresholds alongside an instruction to take account of fitness for repair, aneurysm anatomy, and patient preferences. Two people at 5.5 cm can be judged differently according to age, comorbidity, and vessel shape. The table is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.

8. EVAR and open repair - the difference in concept

There are broadly two repair approaches. This article does not declare one superior. Debate about long-term results continues, and the choice is made case by case according to anatomy and patient condition.

  • Endovascular aneurysm repair, or EVAR - a stent graft is advanced through the vessels in the groin and lines the aneurysm from the inside. The abdomen is not opened widely.
  • Open repair - the abdomen is opened and the aneurysmal segment is replaced with a graft.

Confining this to verified statements: the Society for Vascular Surgery notes that EVAR is better tolerated than open surgical repair, particularly when performed under local anaesthesia. A summary of the 2024 European guideline reports that EVAR shows a significant short-term survival benefit with similar long-term outcomes up to 15 years, and that EVAR remains the preferred treatment modality.

EVAR has one characteristic that matters here: imaging follow-up continues after the operation. The European guideline recommends imaging at 30 days followed by delayed imaging until five years after repair in patients at low risk of complications, and computed tomography angiography of the entire aorta and iliac system at five years for long-term follow-up. In other words, EVAR does not end surveillance; it changes its form. Stopping follow-up after the operation, unaware of this, can undo part of the benefit.

To repeat: which approach suits a given person is outside what this article can judge and belongs to a vascular surgery consultation.

9. Stopping smoking - the one intervention with evidence behind it

The question people most often ask after a diagnosis is what can I actually do. There is one answer with clear evidence behind it: stop smoking.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute states that cigarette smoking is one of the main factors that increases risk for an aortic aneurysm, especially an abdominal aortic aneurysm, and that in a current smoker an abdominal aortic aneurysm may grow more quickly and be more likely to burst. The USPSTF description of smoking as the strongest predictor of prevalence, growth, and rupture points the same way.

The size of the effect of quitting is measurable too. In a meta-analysis pooling 23 prospective studies and more than 4.7 million participants, the relative risk against never smokers was 4.87 for current smokers (95% confidence interval 3.93 to 6.02) and 2.10 for former smokers (1.76 to 2.50). The risk in those who had quit was less than half that of those still smoking. A dose-response was confirmed as well, at 1.87 per 10 cigarettes a day and 1.78 per 10 pack-years. Risk fell with time since quitting, at a relative risk of 0.45 per 10 years of cessation, approaching that of never smokers at around 25 years.

The long-term cohort described earlier points the same way. People who had stopped smoking 3 to 8 years before follow-up had a lifetime risk of 6.6%, 29% lower than those who continued. No other intervention in this condition has been shown to produce an effect of that size.

The practical methods of quitting - nicotine replacement, medication, behavioural support, relapse prevention - are covered separately in the evidence-based guide to stopping smoking, and are not repeated here.

9.1 What has no evidence should be rejected explicitly

The other side deserves to be stated as clearly. No food or supplement has been shown to slow the growth of an aneurysm that already exists. Claims of the following kinds should be met with a demand for evidence.

  • That some food good for the aorta can shrink or reverse an aneurysm
  • That a particular supplement or health functional food regenerates the vessel wall and removes an aneurysm
  • That vessel cleansing or detox can substitute for treating an aneurysm
  • That an aneurysm at the repair threshold can be managed without surgery
  • That some self-measurement device or app can replace the scheduled surveillance imaging

This is not a dismissal of habits that support vascular health generally, such as not smoking and controlling blood pressure and lipids. An approach like managing LDL cholesterol is meaningful across cardiovascular disease. It simply has to be distinguished from claims that sell such measures as a way to reverse an aneurysm that already exists. The interventions currently supported are stopping smoking, controlling blood pressure, keeping the scheduled surveillance, and discussing surgery once a threshold is reached.

If rupture is suspected - this is an emergency
Rupture of an aortic aneurysm is an emergency that cannot wait. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists sudden, severe pain in the stomach area, chest, or back, light-headedness, and rapid heart rate as signs, and states that quick treatment may save your life. In the situations below, do not try to work out the cause yourself and do not spend time choosing transport. Emergency numbers differ from country to country. Call your local emergency number immediately.
· Sudden, severe abdominal or back pain that begins with a tearing quality
· A pulsating mass felt in the abdomen accompanied by pain
· Fainting or clouded consciousness together with cold sweat, pallor, and a rapid pulse
· New pain in a person already under surveillance for an aortic aneurysm
Do not take a painkiller and wait, and do not drive yourself. The emergency service comes first. If you know you have an aortic aneurysm, tell the ambulance crew and the emergency department that fact and the last diameter you were given. When in doubt, choose to call.

10. Rupture and dissection - two different emergencies

The severity of rupture is documented numerically. The USPSTF statement notes that the associated risk for death with rupture is as high as 81%. Medical reference sources record that more than 50% of patients with a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm die before reaching the emergency room. These figures are not quoted to frighten. They are quoted because they explain why detection and surveillance are the only points at which action is possible.

Rupture risk rises with diameter. Korean national health information states that aortic rupture is the most common cause of death in aortic aneurysm and that the risk is large once the diameter reaches 6 cm or more.

A concept that is easily confused with this is aortic dissection. The simple distinction is as follows.

  • An aneurysm is a vessel wall that has bulged outward. It usually develops slowly and silently.
  • A dissection is a tear in the inner layer of the wall through which blood forces its way between the layers. It usually begins suddenly and painfully.
  • The two are linked. Korean national health information lists aortic dissection among the risk factors for aortic aneurysm.
  • A member of the public does not need to tell them apart. The response is the same. Sudden, severe chest, back, or abdominal pain is an emergency either way.

This is precisely why this article avoids phrasing of the form these symptoms mean an aortic aneurysm. The signs above are not exclusive to aortic aneurysm and may indicate another lethal cause. The judgement required is not naming the disease but getting to care.

11. Inherited aortic disease - the thresholds change

Most of the figures so far assume the sporadic case, without a specific genetic condition. When inherited aortic disease is present, the thresholds themselves change.

The genetic conditions the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists as raising risk are Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Turner syndrome, familial thoracic aortic aneurysm, and bicuspid aortic valve. Korean national health information likewise names genetic factors such as Marfan syndrome as a risk factor and describes the characteristic degeneration of the middle layer of the vessel wall.

How the surgical thresholds shift is set out in the 2022 American guideline.

  • Marfan syndrome - surgery recommended at an aortic root diameter of 5.0 cm or more, and considered reasonable at 4.5 cm or more with high-risk features.
  • Nonsyndromic heritable thoracic aortic disease - repair recommended at 5.0 cm or more without high-risk features, and at 4.5 cm or more when high-risk features are present.
  • Bicuspid aortic valve - surgery recommended at 5.5 cm or more, and reasonable at 5.0 to 5.4 cm with a risk factor for dissection.
  • The high-risk features referred to include a family history of dissection at a diameter below 5.0 cm, unexplained sudden death before age 50, and rapid aortic growth.
  • For first-degree relatives of a person with bicuspid aortic valve, screening with transthoracic echocardiography is recommended.

This area sits within specialist judgement. If inherited aortic disease is suspected or diagnosed, the general screening recommendations should not be applied to oneself; an individual plan belongs in specialist care for that condition. If there has been aortic disease at a young age or unexplained sudden death in the family, saying so at an appointment matters.

Frequently asked questions

At what diameter does an aortic aneurysm begin?

Two criteria are in use. Korean national health information treats growth beyond 1.5 times the normal aortic wall as an aneurysm, while the USPSTF defines abdominal aortic aneurysm as enlargement to 3.0 cm or larger. The 2024 European guideline uses 30 mm or more and sets 25 to 29 mm aside as subaneurysmal. Three centimetres is the line where observation begins, not where treatment begins.

Should I be screened for an abdominal aortic aneurysm?

It depends on age, sex, and smoking history. The USPSTF recommends one-time ultrasound screening for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked at grade B, and advises selective offering at grade C for men of the same age who have never smoked. For women who never smoked and have no family history the grade is D, meaning routine screening is not recommended, and for women aged 65 to 75 with a smoking history or family history it is I, meaning the evidence is insufficient. Because this is written for a US population, whether it applies to you is a decision for your own clinician.

Does the South Korean national health check include an aortic aneurysm test?

It does not. The general examination covers consultation, body measurements, vision and hearing, blood pressure, chest radiography, blood tests, urinalysis, and dental examination, and the age-based additions contain no abdominal ultrasound and no measurement of aortic diameter. The six national cancer screening programmes do not cover the aorta either. Anyone who needs it checked has to go through a clinical route.

I was told it is 4 cm. Can I just leave it alone?

Leaving it alone and watching it on a schedule are different things. A 4 cm aneurysm is a surveillance target, not a surgical one. The 2022 American guideline sets annual imaging at 4.0 to 4.9 cm; the English screening programme uses annual at 3.0 to 4.4 cm and every 3 months at 4.5 to 5.4 cm. The danger is not the size but missing the interval. If you have been given a diameter, confirm and write down the date of the next scan on the spot.

Is there a food or supplement that is good for the aorta?

No food or supplement has been shown to slow the growth of an existing aneurysm. The evidence on smoking is clear by contrast. In meta-analysis the relative risk for current smokers was 4.87, falling to 0.45 per 10 years of cessation and approaching never-smoker levels at around 25 years. The interventions with evidence are stopping smoking, controlling blood pressure, and keeping the scheduled surveillance.

Conclusion: going to look, and not losing track

Five lines capture what this article kept returning to.

  1. Symptoms cannot serve as a guide. Most aortic aneurysms signal nothing until they are large, and are found incidentally on other tests.
  2. That leaves screening and surveillance as the only points of action. The USPSTF recommendation splits into four grades, B, C, D, and I, by age, sex, and smoking history.
  3. The South Korean national programme has no such item. Having attended every examination is not evidence that the aorta has been checked.
  4. The real hazard after discovery is missing the interval. Depending on diameter it runs at 5 years, 3 years, 1 year, 6 months, or 3 months.
  5. Among modifiable factors, the evidence for stopping smoking is strongest. Foods and supplements said to be good for the aorta have no matching evidence.

One final observation. In this condition, the most common failure is not finding it late but finding it and then losing track of it. The most dangerous moment is walking out of a consulting room having heard a number and never booking the next appointment. So the practical conclusion of this article shrinks to a single sentence: if you were given a diameter, write down the date of the next scan alongside it.

And this article can only draw a map; it cannot look at your aorta. If any of the criteria on age, sex, smoking history, or family history apply to you, checking at an appointment is faster than reaching a conclusion on your own.
