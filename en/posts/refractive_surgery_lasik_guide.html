Refractive Surgery - Whether You Are a Candidate Is Decided Before Which Procedure Is Better

How LASIK, PRK, SMILE and phakic lens implants are built, what rules people out, and what cannot be undone

2026-09-01 Health & Wellness

Start looking into refractive surgery and almost every piece of information sits on top of the same question. LASIK, PRK or SMILE. Yet in the examination room the order runs the other way. Candidacy is settled before the procedure is chosen. If the cornea is not thick enough, or if topography shows an abnormal pattern, the question of which procedure is better never arises at all.

So this article does not rank the procedures. Its weight sits on the exclusion criteria. Every figure quoted here comes from a source that could actually be opened: the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency national health information portal, the American Academy of Ophthalmology, a tertiary hospital medical information service, and the original papers.

This article is general health information, not a basis for diagnosis or treatment. Eye condition and surgical suitability differ from person to person according to test results, so the actual decision is made with an ophthalmologist. Nothing written here is intended to encourage or discourage any particular operation.

The order is usually taught backwards

There is a reason information about refractive surgery clusters around procedure comparison. A procedure has a name, a name can be chosen, and what can be chosen can be sold. The information that you might not be a candidate, by contrast, does not sell. So most of what search turns up opens with a table of pros and cons for the three procedures, and the exclusion criteria get pushed to a line or two at the bottom.

The real order does not match that layout. Only after refraction, corneal topography and pachymetry are done does it become clear how many options are left. For some people all three remain, for some only one, and for some nothing remains. That last case is not a failure; it is the screening doing its job.

Two branches - reshaping the cornea, or adding a lens

Myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism are states in which light entering the eye does not focus precisely on the retina, and together they are called refractive error. In principle there are only two ways surgery can change that. One is changing the shape of the cornea, the surface where light bends first, and the other is putting an additional lens inside the eye.

The first branch is PRK, LASEK, LASIK and SMILE. The methods differ in detail, but all of them use a laser to remove corneal stromal tissue. Removed tissue is never made again. That single sentence generates every constraint in this branch. The second branch, phakic intraocular lens implantation, leaves the eye's own crystalline lens in place and adds a separate lens inside the eye. It is free of the thickness constraint and leaves room to remove the lens later, but in exchange it carries a different kind of risk: it is intraocular surgery.

  • The corneal branch — tissue is removed, so it cannot be undone. In exchange the inside of the eye is not opened
  • The lens branch — removal remains possible. In exchange the inside of the eye is opened
  • These are not better and worse but options with different constraints. With a thin cornea the first branch was never open in the first place
  • Either way the prescription has to be stable. A prescription that keeps moving cannot be caught by any method

LASIK, PRK and SMILE - how the cornea is opened

The difference among the three reduces to one sentence. How does the laser reach the corneal stroma it has to treat? The goal is identical and the route differs, and pain, recovery speed and flap-related risk all follow from that route.

PRK and LASEK - the epithelium is removed

The outermost layer of the cornea, the epithelium, is removed and the laser is applied to the stroma beneath. The Korean agency's material distinguishes the version that discards the epithelial sheet entirely as surface ablation and the version that lays the lifted sheet back in place as LASEK. Because no flap is created, flap-related complications do not exist. In exchange it hurts until the epithelium closes over again. The same source lists 4 to 5 days of postoperative pain and a long recovery as the drawbacks.

LASIK - a flap is created

A microkeratome or a femtosecond laser creates a thin lid, the corneal flap, which is lifted so the exposed stroma can be ablated before the flap is laid back down. The epithelium is not damaged, so there is little pain and recovery is fast. Seoul National University Hospital's medical information notes that uncorrected acuity reaches roughly 0.5 to 0.9 the day after LASIK and stabilizes over two to three months. In exchange, the flap is not sutured; it adheres on its own. Because of that structure the flap may be cut imperfectly, may be displaced by later trauma, or may allow epithelial cells to grow in underneath it.

SMILE - a lenticule is removed through a small incision

No excimer laser is used. A femtosecond laser alone cuts a disc of tissue called a lenticule inside the corneal stroma, which is then extracted through an incision of about 2 millimeters. With no large flap, recovery is fast and the cornea is comparatively more resistant to external impact, according to the Korean agency's material. But a newer method is not better on every measure. In a meta-analysis of 12 studies and 1,076 eyes comparing SMILE with femtosecond LASIK, there was no statistically significant difference between the groups in the proportion reaching 20/20 uncorrected, in refractive predictability, or in the proportion losing lines of corrected acuity. Where they did differ was dry eye measures, and the authors rated their own evidence as low to very low quality.

Where lens implantation sits

Lens implantation is generally considered in two situations. When the cornea is too thin to have anything to spare, and when the prescription is too high for corneal ablation to handle. It escapes the ectasia axis of risk, but a different axis enters. Because a foreign body now resides inside the eye, the endothelial cells on the inner corneal surface, the crystalline lens, aqueous flow and intraocular pressure are all in play. That is why specular microscopy of the endothelium and ocular biometry are added to the preoperative workup, and why the preoperative endothelial cell count, which does not regenerate, sets the margin available afterwards.

There are verified long-term figures as well. A Swiss retrospective analysis presented in the American Academy of Ophthalmology's EyeNet followed 133 posterior chamber phakic lens implantations performed between 1998 and 2004 for ten years. At ten years lens opacities were present in 54.8 percent of eyes, and cataract significant enough to require surgery had developed in 18.3 percent. The same figure had been 4.9 percent at five years. Elevated intraocular pressure requiring medication had not occurred once by five years, yet was present in 12.9 percent at ten. Still, these numbers should not be transferred to today's lenses. This was a single-centre retrospective series on a model used in the early 2000s, and lens design has changed since. What survives is the structure. The risk of this procedure appears over years, not right after surgery.

  • Not cutting the cornea means avoiding the risk of ectasia, not that there is no risk
  • Rising pressure, lens opacity and endothelial loss are invisible right after surgery. That is why follow-up is part of the procedure
  • Being able to remove or exchange the lens is a genuine advantage, but removal is intraocular surgery too
  • Whether it goes in the anterior or posterior chamber, and whether a size exists that fits the eye, decide feasibility itself

Exclusion criteria - you may not be a candidate

This is the centre of the article. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency portal lists explicitly who cannot be a candidate for myopia correction surgery, and the American Academy of Ophthalmology's patient guidance names largely the same list. Items that two bodies point to independently are closer to evidence than to custom. The table below merges the two lists and adds why each one matters.

The line most often overlooked is the last one. The surgery takes a day, the management takes months. A long posting abroad or extended travel is by itself a reason to postpone.

Why keratoconus and ectasia are decisive

The cornea is a thin membrane under constant pressure from inside. Keratoconus is the disease in which that membrane weakens and bulges forward into a cone; ectasia is the same thing happening after surgery has thinned it. The result is severe myopia and irregular astigmatism, and it is difficult to correct. The problem is that early keratoconus is quiet. Acuity can still be good and the slit lamp can look normal, so a subtle asymmetry on topography is sometimes the only clue. In the EyeNet debate one clinician stated that preoperative topography is typically the critical test that best identifies patients at significantly higher risk.

The thickness threshold is still unsettled. In the same debate one side offered a residual stromal bed of 250 micrometers as probably safe, and the other replied that numerous patients have developed ectasia with a stromal bed thicker than 250 micrometers, and that residual bed thickness is a continuously variable risk factor. A separate literature review cites a recommendation to avoid LASIK when central corneal thickness is under 450 micrometers or the residual bed would be under 150 micrometers, while stating plainly that no universal consensus exists.

The timing is easy to misread too. In that same review the mean interval from LASIK to a diagnosis of ectasia was 36.0 months (standard deviation 16.9, range 12 to 60 months), while another report it cites gave a mean of 13 months (range 6 to 20). A few uneventful months after surgery do not mean safety. It is also worth remembering that in the same series 21.4 percent had no identifiable preoperative risk factor at all. Screening reduces risk; it does not remove it.

What the preoperative exams actually decide

The preoperative workup is not a procedure for measuring the prescription but a procedure for ruling feasibility in or out. Attaching to each item on the Korean agency's list what it decides makes it clear what to ask when the results are read out.

Preparation has conditions attached as well. Contact lenses press on the cornea and reshape it, so imaging topography while they are still being worn can show an abnormality that is not there or hide one that is. The Korean agency's material says to be out of soft lenses for at least one week and hard lenses for at least three weeks before testing, while Seoul National University Hospital gives two to three weeks for hard lenses. That the intervals differ is best read as meaning the more generous interval is the safer one.

Corneal thickness is a budget

Corneal ablation is like sculpture: what is taken away cannot be put back. So corneal thickness behaves like a budget that shrinks once spent. A higher prescription spends more of it, and spending a lot in the first operation leaves less for a second chance. The Korean agency explains that myopic regression appears mainly within the first year after surgery as the cornea heals, and whether it can be treated by ablating again is not a matter of willingness but of whether the remaining thickness allows it.

  • The planned ablation depth and expected residual bed are numbers worth writing down. They become the reference when another clinic assesses you later
  • A thinner cornea makes pressure read lower than it is. Knowing your preoperative value keeps you from being disadvantaged in a later glaucoma assessment
  • Lens implantation escapes this budget but spends a different consumable, the endothelium
  • Irreversible does not mean the result is bad. It means whatever the result, that is where you start from

The first two rows matter most. The PROWL investigators concluded that systematically administered questionnaires detected substantially higher symptom rates than clinician records did. Some symptoms simply do not surface unless someone asks. Conversely, dissatisfaction in the same studies stayed between 1 and 4 percent. Having symptoms and regretting the surgery are different values, and both are true.

The most common item is dry eye. The Korean agency explains that surgery damages corneal nerves, and that reduced nerve stimulation triggers a neuroinflammatory response, with most cases improving by around six months. Seoul National University Hospital likewise says most patients return to their preoperative level in about six months but that in some it persists.

Red flags — if any of the following appear after surgery, that is, pain that suddenly worsens, vision that drops noticeably within a day, redness that keeps increasing, yellow discharge, or glare that abruptly intensifies, then contact the operating clinic or an ophthalmologist with emergency capacity immediately rather than waiting for the next appointment. The same applies if your field of vision changes after a hard blow to the eye.

Red flags - go in without waiting

Recovery involves discomfort by design. Grittiness, watering, a few days of blur and sensitivity to light belong to the expected course. The problem is that this normal range and an emergency look similar early on. The distinguishing criterion is less the type of symptom than its direction. When something that was improving suddenly worsens, that is a different story.

  • Pain that had been easing gets worse again — this can signal infection or an epithelial problem
  • Vision drops noticeably within a day — the direction is the opposite of ordinary fluctuation during recovery
  • Redness keeps increasing and the lid swells — inflammation may be progressing
  • Yellow or sticky discharge appears — this is a finding that should raise suspicion of infection
  • Glare suddenly intensifies — this is distinct from the gradual decline of the usual course
  • Vision changed after a blow to the eye — a LASIK flap is not sutured, so a check is warranted even years later
  • The eye will not open enough to instil the prescribed drops — uncontrolled pain is itself a reason to be seen

The common mistake here is waiting for the next scheduled visit. Corneal infection can progress in hours, and the difference between early and delayed treatment is large. Confirming how to reach someone at night or on a weekend, before the surgery, is the preparation this section actually asks for.

This surgery does not solve presbyopia

The most common misunderstanding sits here. Myopia correction and presbyopia are problems on different levels. Myopia is a refractive problem, light focusing in front of the retina; presbyopia is the accommodating function, in which the crystalline lens changes thickness for near vision, declining with age. Reshaping the cornea changes refraction and leaves accommodation exactly as it was.

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency portal explains that presbyopic symptoms begin in the early forties and progress into the sixties, and that after about 60 accommodation is nearly exhausted so the condition stabilizes rather than worsening further. It also states that there is no way to prevent it. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's patient guidance likewise specifies that LASIK does not correct presbyopia and that most people need reading glasses at around age 40.

There is also a circumstance specific to myopes. The same source explains that people with myopia tend to notice presbyopia later, because taking the glasses off brings near objects into focus. Once the cornea is corrected and being glasses-free is the default, that buffer disappears too, so near-vision difficulty can be felt earlier at the same age. The surgery did not accelerate presbyopia; the cushion that had been hiding it is gone. Monovision, the alternative usually mentioned, sets one eye for distance and the other for near, and both bodies list it as an option. But it is not a solution so much as a trade, and adaptation varies widely between individuals.

What a success rate counts, and what a quote leaves out

When the phrase success rate appears in advertising, what to interrogate is not the value but the denominator and the definition. Placing two numbers from the table above side by side shows why. According to the American Academy of Ophthalmology, about 90 percent of people who have LASIK end up with vision between 20/20 and 20/40 without glasses or contacts. Yet the proportion reporting visual symptoms at three months that they did not have before surgery was 43 to 46 percent. The two numbers do not contradict each other. One counted a line on an eye chart, the other counted symptom experience.

  • Definition — reaching a target acuity, being symptom-free, avoiding retreatment, or being satisfied on a questionnaire
  • Timepoint — three months, one year and five years are different values. Regression appears mainly in the first year
  • Denominator — where did the people lost to follow-up go? Were retreated eyes counted as successes?
  • Measurement — chart review or questionnaire. The PROWL investigators noted that this difference changes the result substantially

Cost has the same structure. This article gives no figures, because they differ by clinic and change over time. What does hold is the items a bundled price tends to leave out: the detailed screening exam, whether retreatment is included and on what conditions, postoperative drops and artificial tears, how many follow-up visits over what period, treatment costs if a complication occurs, and whether care can continue if you move to another region. A promotional price usually shows only the first line.

Recovery and getting back to normal

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency portal advises resting with the eyes closed for at least two hours after surgery, instilling the prescribed drops and artificial tears, and avoiding visually demanding work for one week. Washing the face and a simple shower are placed at three days, public baths and saunas at one week, light exercise at one week and vigorous exercise at one to two months, with alcohol and smoking to be avoided for one month.

Visual recovery diverges by procedure. After LASIK, uncorrected acuity reaches roughly 0.5 to 0.9 the next day and stabilizes over two to three months. Surface procedures require a bandage contact lens for three to five days until the epithelium closes, are painful during that time, and reach a comparable final acuity more slowly. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's PRK guidance likewise says vision improves over three to five days and that reaching best vision may take a month or longer.

  • Driving — early night glare is common. Night driving is safer after it is cleared at a visit rather than when it merely feels fine
  • Screen work — concentration reduces blinking and worsens dryness. A rule about breaks does more than the return date does
  • Water — washing and showering differ from baths and swimming because the route of infection differs
  • Exercise — discuss impact sports separately. With LASIK this matters more because of the flap
  • Follow-up — go even without symptoms. Late-appearing problems are caught by testing before symptoms

The option of not operating

Not operating is also an option, and the comparison is only honest if the risk attached to that option is placed alongside. Contact lenses are not a zero-risk alternative. A literature review compiling the incidence of contact lens related microbial keratitis gives the following table.

What to read here is the pattern rather than the absolute values. Whatever the material, the incidence rises clearly in schedules where lenses are slept in. The review notes that rates were not appreciably different with contemporary materials and wear modalities either. Which means changing the wearing habit reduces risk more than changing the material does.

These figures should not, however, be added to or subtracted from surgical risk directly. The lens side is an annual incidence and the surgical side is the probability of a one-off event, so the units differ, and so do the populations. The table is still useful because it shows that the thing being compared against is not safety itself. Glasses remain the most conservative option in that they touch nothing and can be undone at any moment, and refractive surgery is not a surgery you have to have, but an elective one that changes how you live.

What to ask at the consultation

If you name a procedure first, the consultation drifts into an explanation of that procedure's advantages. Better to reverse the order. Ask for the numbers from your own eye first. The questions below are not for judging on the spot; they are for holding a basis of comparison when you get a second look elsewhere.

  • What is my corneal thickness, the planned ablation depth, and the residual bed? Three numbers, in writing
  • Does my topography show asymmetry or inferior steepening? A negative answer is worth recording too
  • Why this procedure, and why not the others? If the reason is thickness or topography, it has a basis
  • Is there a record of my prescription over recent years? Change within one year bears directly on the exclusion criteria
  • What is my intraocular pressure before surgery? Once the cornea is thinner this value cannot be obtained again
  • Is retreatment possible, and how much thickness would remain then? Ask for a number, not a possibility
  • How wide does my pupil open in the dark? A large pupil is a risk factor for glare
  • Where are complications treated, and how do I reach you at night or on a weekend?
  • How long and how many follow-up visits? The management period is the real length of this surgery

One more thing. Taking the same test data to a second ophthalmologist is standard procedure in front of something irreversible. It matters more if the first consultation used phrases like borderline or possible under conditions. Those phrases mean the assessment is a place where judgments can diverge.

Again, this article is general information and not a personal diagnostic or treatment instruction. The rates and thresholds quoted come from studies with different populations and timepoints, so they do not transfer directly to an individual case. Whether to operate, which procedure to choose, and how to read symptoms afterwards are decided together with a clinician who has seen your test results. If symptoms suddenly worsen, do not reread this article; be seen.

What I could not verify

  • The US Food and Drug Administration's LASIK guidance pages could not be opened. Figures said to be presented there are not quoted here, and for the same reason the approved dioptre range for SMILE is not given as a number
  • Official statistics on the annual number of procedures performed in Korea and on the retreatment rate could not be verified
  • A randomized controlled trial directly comparing the three procedures over ten years or more could not be found. The meta-analysis cited reports mostly one-year outcomes, and its authors rated the evidence as low to very low quality
  • Ten-year or longer Korean follow-up data on current lens implant models could not be verified
  • The true prevalence of ectasia is not settled even in the literature. The 0.02 to 0.6 percent quoted is only the reported range
  • The Korean Ophthalmological Society's official patient information sheet could not be found in an accessible form, so Korean evidence was drawn from the national agency and tertiary hospital material instead
  • Prices are deliberately omitted. They vary so much that a specific figure would create a false reference point
