Why Controllers Do Not Belong on the Internet - Design Principles for Water Utility OT Security

Where IT common sense inverts in control systems, and the boundaries the standards ask for

Aug 10, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: convenience always builds a path

To run the night shift with one operator, someone has to be able to see plant status from home. If the maintenance contractor is two hours away, dialing in beats driving out. If the intake sits up a mountain road, a cellular modem costs a fraction of a patrol route.

Taken one at a time, every one of those decisions is reasonable. Stack a few years of them and one day a device that physically moves equipment is answering on the public internet. Nobody ever decided to do that, and it happened anyway.

Three objections show up at this point. We set a password. Access only comes through the VPN. Who would bother with a utility our size. All three carry some weight in an ordinary information system. In a control system they do not hold, and not because the controls are weak. The assumptions underneath are different.

What this covers. How control systems (ICS and OT) differ from ordinary IT, what boundary design the international standards actually require, and in what order a plant with no budget and no security staff should start. What it does not cover is equally clear: no product vulnerabilities, no techniques for locating exposed devices, no attack reproduction steps. Defending the system does not require any of those. No individual facility or operator is named.

1. Control systems start from different assumptions

The most common mistake people make coming into this field is to carry a familiar information security checklist across unchanged. The individual items are not wrong. What differs is the objective those items are optimizing for.

In an information system, an incident usually means data left or a service stopped. Restore from backup and the system is back where it was. In a control system, an incident means a pump runs, a valve opens, a chemical dose shifts. No backup returns water that already flowed. So the ordering inverts.

AxisInformation technology (IT)Operational technology (OT)
Top objectiveConfidentiality, then integrity, then availabilitySafety, then availability, then integrity, then confidentiality
Shape of failureData disclosure, service outagePhysical state change, equipment damage, safety events
RecoverabilityBackup and rollback restore the prior stateA physical event that already happened cannot be undone
Equipment lifetime3 to 7 years15 to 30 years, with discontinued parts still in service
When you can stop itNights and weekends, routinelyOne or two planned outage windows a year
PatchingAs fast as possibleAfter vendor validation, in the maintenance window only
Authentication baselineNearly every service assumes itMany field protocols were designed with no concept of it
Account lockoutStandard controlOn an operator console it can block incident response
Network scanningRoutine hygieneCan knock over older controllers, so handled with care

The most consequential row is authentication baseline. Many long-lived industrial protocols at the field layer were designed on the premise that they would only ever run inside a trusted closed network. Under that premise there was no reason to sign a command. The consequence is that a well-formed command is indistinguishable from a legitimate operator command, whoever sent it. That is not a bug; it was a design choice at the time, which is exactly why no patch removes it.

This is what turns do not put it on the internet from a slogan into a design principle. It puts a protocol that assumes no authentication onto a network where authentication cannot be assumed. No amount of added control changes that combination.

2. Nobody decided to connect it to the internet

Ask at a plant and most people will say they are segregated. They are rarely lying. But that answer is usually a belief rather than a verified fact. The ways segregation quietly fails are well known, and they share one trait: every one of them was an operations decision, not a security decision.

  • The communications box the vendor installed for maintenance. The contract says remote support in four words, and nobody has opened the cabinet since commissioning day
  • The engineering laptop. It reads email on the business network in the morning and plugs into the control network in the afternoon. The laptop itself is a mobile bridge between two zones
  • Remote support software that dials outward. Inbound firewall rules never see it, because the connection starts on the inside
  • A cloud monitoring service. Adopting it is reasonable, but the moment it is live a new path exists between the control zone and the outside. If that path is not registered as an asset, it is managed as if it did not exist
  • Leftovers from a circuit contract. A public address issued for a temporary need sometimes stays bound to equipment, and when staff turn over the knowledge of it disappears
  • Wireless telemetry and metering units. They are believed to be isolated from upstream networks, and the physical wiring sometimes says otherwise

So the way to confirm exposure is not to go look from outside. It is to reconcile lists from inside. Put procurement and construction records, maintenance contracts, the circuit register and the as-built wiring side by side, and hunt for entries that do not explain each other. The work is unglamorous, and it comes before everything else. Just as with trust boundaries on the web, a boundary exists only for the people who drew it.

3. What the standards ask for first: draw the boundary, then name it

The international standard for industrial automation and control system security is the ISA/IEC 62443 family. People opening it for the first time usually expect a shopping list and come away puzzled, because 62443 is not a product list. It is a scheme that splits what each role must require and must demonstrate.

PartPublishedAudience and content
62443-1-12007Terminology, concepts and models
62443-2-12024Security program requirements for IACS asset owners
62443-2-22025IACS security protection scheme
62443-2-32015Patch management in the IACS environment
62443-2-42018Security program requirements for IACS service providers
62443-3-22020Security risk assessment for system design
62443-3-32013System security requirements and security levels
62443-4-12018Secure product development lifecycle requirements
62443-4-22018Technical security requirements for IACS components

Zones and conduits: not connecting is a conclusion, not a rule

The first step in the 62443 design procedure is to divide the system under consideration into zones and to define the communication paths between zones as conduits. Only then do you assess risk against each zone and conduit. The order matters. Before choosing any control, you commit to paper what is attached to what.

Go through that exercise and the sentence do not put a controller on the internet changes status. It stops being an external prohibition and becomes a conclusion that falls out of the drawing. The public internet is the outermost region to which no trust level can be assigned, and any conduit joining it directly to a control zone has to justify its own existence. Failing that justification, the conduit gets erased.

Security levels: not how safe, but whom you assume

The security level in the title of 62443-3-3 is not, despite the common reading, a product quality grade. It is closer to a statement of what class of adversary the design assumes. A segment that only needs to survive accidental contact and a segment that must assume a motivated party spending real resources call for different controls. The value earns its keep when it is used to agree on how far to assume for this zone, not to score the plant.

The parts you can put in a contract

An asset owner usually cannot modify controller firmware. That is why the most practically valuable parts of 62443 are often 4-1, 4-2 and 2-4: product development process, component technical requirements and service provider requirements. All three can be lifted directly into procurement documents and maintenance contracts. Moving security out of the operations team's homework pile and into purchase conditions is the biggest lever a thinly staffed organization has.

4. Where NIST SP 800-82 writes differently for OT

NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security (September 2023) is the most widely referenced free document in this space. The title of the third revision is a signal in itself: not industrial control systems but operational technology. Its scope covers industrial control systems along with building automation systems, transportation systems and physical access control systems. That expansion matters even if you only care about a treatment plant, because how access control and HVAC on the same site interlace with the control network is part of the attack surface.

The core claim compresses to one sentence: do not transplant controls proven in IT, reselect them against the performance, reliability and safety constraints of operational technology. Concretely, these are the points of divergence.

  • Account lockout. A default in IT, but an operator locked out of a console during an upset is itself an incident. In some segments the right answer is stronger monitoring and recording instead of lockout
  • Active vulnerability scanning. An older controller can stop on a single unexpected packet. In live segments prefer passive observation and defer active testing to maintenance windows and test rigs
  • Automatic updates. An update that has not been through vendor validation can void warranty and safety certification. Patching becomes not fast but validated and scheduled
  • Blanket encryption. In real-time control segments latency and processing load are control quality. You have to choose where it goes
  • Multi-factor authentication. Strongly required on remote paths, but demanding the same ceremony at a local operator panel can obstruct emergency response

The important thing is that these exceptions mean achieve the same objective another way, never skip it. If you cannot lock accounts, record attempts and route an alert to a human. If you cannot auto-patch, subscribe to vulnerability information, assess the risk and apply compensating controls. Writing that substitution down is what an OT security program actually consists of.

5. Asset inventory: you cannot defend what is not on the list

Everything above rests on a single premise: that you know what is there. In practice this is the premise that collapses most often. And internet exposure appears, almost without exception, on a device that was never on the list.

Discovering assets in a control environment uses different methods than in an office. Sweeping the network to solicit responses can stop older equipment, so it is hard to justify on live segments. Two tracks run together instead.

  • Passive observation. Listen to traffic through a span port or a network tap. Nothing is sent to any device, so it is safe during operation
  • Document reconciliation. Lay out as-built drawings, purchase records, maintenance contracts, the circuit register and vendor commissioning reports. A device seen in traffic but absent from the documents and a device present in documents but silent on the wire are both investigation targets

The inventory cannot stop at what exists. To manage exposure it has to record paths and people as well.

  • Device identity, physical location and owning department
  • Firmware and software versions with the date of last change
  • Every communication interface it has: wired, wireless, serial, cellular
  • Which circuit it uses to reach the outside — the only field that turns exposure into a list entry
  • The named people and companies who can reach it remotely
  • Its intended peers, meaning which devices and servers it was designed to talk to
  • Safety interlocks, meaning what happens to what if this device stops
  • End-of-life status and the vendor support end date

The last two read more like an operations register than a security one. But they are precisely the basis for deciding what may safely be disconnected first during an incident. Without knowing the interlocks, the response itself becomes dangerous.

6. Levels and the brokered zone: one boundary is not enough

There is a long-standing reference model for layering control environments: split everything from field devices up to enterprise systems into levels, and define what may cross between them. Names and level numbers vary a little between organizations, but the substance is the same.

LevelWhat lives hereWhat may go outWhat may come in
Field devicesSensors, actuators, instrumentsMeasurementsSignals from the controller above only
ControlControllers, remote terminal unitsState and alarmsValidated commands from supervision only
SupervisionOperator screens, supervisory serversOperating dataQueries from the operations level
Operations managementHistorians, engineering toolsAggregated and reporting dataOnly what passed the brokered zone
Brokered zone (DMZ)Data replicas, jump hosts, update file storesCopies bound for the enterpriseApproved remote sessions
Enterprise ITBusiness network, corporate systemsThe internetNo direct connection into control levels

The row that carries the weight is the brokered zone. The rule is plain: the control levels and the enterprise network never see each other directly. The inside publishes data into the brokered zone and the outside collects it from there. The goal is a structure in which no upstream system ever queries a device inside the control zone.

The reason a single boundary is insufficient is organizational more than technical. Firewall rule sets only ever grow. An exception is opened for an urgent job, the job ends, the rule stays. Years later the list carries entries nobody can account for. Splitting into levels shortens the list each boundary has to permit, and a short list actually gets reviewed. A rule set that is never reviewed is functionally absent.

7. Unidirectional gateways: separate what leaves from what enters

Plenty of data genuinely needs to leave a control zone: monitoring views, daily reports, regulatory submissions, statistics and billing. Far less needs to come in. That asymmetry is where the design room is.

A unidirectional gateway, or data diode, is built to carry signal in one direction only. The point is that the return path is absent structurally, not by configuration. A firewall blocks by rule and a wrong rule opens it; a one-way transport does not acquire a reverse direction no matter how badly the rules are written.

What you gain and lose is explicit.

  • Gain — data for remote monitoring, reporting and regulatory submission still leaves, so you do not surrender the operational benefit of unattended running and remote visibility
  • Gain — no manipulation from outside can physically reach in. Even a compromised remote account cannot change plant state through this path
  • Lose — no remote control and no remote updates. More jobs require someone to drive to site
  • Lose — ordinary request-and-response protocols do not survive the crossing, so the usual answer is to keep a state replica outside the gateway and let upstream systems talk only to the replica
  • Lose — hardware cost and operational overhead exceed a firewall, so it is not an option for every link

The realistic sequence follows from that. Convert the paths that only need to export first: monitoring data, historian feeds, reporting aggregates. Leave the genuinely bidirectional paths in place, but keep that list short and attach the conditions in section 8.

One common misconception. Installing a unidirectional gateway does not mean no other path exists. The shadow paths from section 2 live on regardless of the gateway. That is why the inventory comes first: a boundary device is not a substitute for a list.

8. Remote access: if you cannot remove it, attach conditions

Proposals to eliminate remote access entirely rarely survive contact with operations. Unattended night running, distant maintenance contractors and vendor support are real operating conditions. So the working goal is not removal but conditioning: access exists, but not at any time, by anyone, to anywhere.

MethodWhat it lets you controlResidual risk
Remote access straight to the controllerEssentially only the credentialA compromised credential reaches physical control immediately
VPN into the control networkEncryption, authentication of the partyBroad reach once inside; a standing tunnel is always open
Via a jump host in the brokered zoneReach, session recording, approval workflowThe jump host becomes the target; it needs patching and monitoring
Opened on demand onlyThe exposure window itselfHabits form that route around the opening procedure
With physical concurrence on siteNothing opens unless a person on site agreesHard to apply at unattended sites and overnight
Vendor remote support toolingThe tool's own settingsStarts outbound, so inbound blocking never catches it

The last row deserves emphasis. Many organizations believe blocking inbound connections is enough. But most remote support tools initiate outbound and then use that channel in both directions. No inbound rule ever sees them. Egress has to be restricted by destination as well, and doing that sends you back to needing the list.

The conditions to attach to remote access come out as follows. You do not need all of them at once; work down the list.

  • Retire standing tunnels. Open on a work request, close when the work ends
  • Force everything through a broker. Leave no path that lands directly on control zone equipment
  • Give every person their own account. A shared contractor login makes it permanently impossible to say who did what
  • Require multi-factor authentication. A remote path must not open on one credential
  • Approve per job. Replace this vendor may connect at any time with this job, in this window
  • Bound it in time. Approvals need an expiry, and the expiry has to act on its own
  • Record the session. If you cannot review what changed, post-incident analysis is impossible
  • Confirm closure. That it closed is as much a fact to verify as that it opened, and this should not depend on anyone's memory
  • Write it into the contract. None of the above holds unless the contractor agreed to it, which makes renewal the best opportunity

9. Why total disconnection is not the answer

Read this far and it is tempting to conclude that you should simply pull every cable. Physical isolation is a strong control. It is not a free one, and two side effects follow with regularity.

  • Data and software move anyway. Cut the circuit and removable drives and maintenance laptops take over the job. Portable media are harder to control than a cable and usually leave no record. A large share of incidents on isolated networks are explained by exactly this path
  • Losing visibility delays detection. At an unattended site, learning about an anomaly hours late is a safety risk in its own right. Trading safety for security is not a good trade

So the practical goal is not zero connectivity but connectivity whose direction and conditions are controlled. Export what only needs to leave through a one-way path, and cut the paths that can carry commands inward down to a short list with conditions. Three questions are enough to decide which paths stay.

  1. What becomes impossible without this path? If the answer is only that things get less convenient, delete it
  2. Can this path change physical state inside? If it can, convert it to one-way or attach every condition in section 8
  3. Who checks whether this path is open right now? If you cannot name the person, that path is unmanaged

10. What water sector authorities actually ask for

The water sector has more operators and a wider spread of scale than most infrastructure sectors. Large utilities have dedicated teams; small ones frequently have nobody assigned to security at all. That is why authority guidance in this sector arrives as short action lists rather than specialist specifications.

United States: free, published action lists

  • Top Cyber Actions for Securing Water Systems — a joint fact sheet from CISA, EPA and the FBI (February 2024) laying out the actions water and wastewater operators should take first, together with free services and tools that support them
  • Internet-Exposed HMIs Pose Cybersecurity Risks to Water and Wastewater Systems — a joint CISA and EPA fact sheet (December 2024) that takes on operator screens reachable from the internet head-on, with recommendations for limiting exposure and hardening what remains
  • Guidance on Improving Cybersecurity at Drinking Water and Wastewater Systems — US Environmental Protection Agency, document number EPA 817-B-23-001 (March 2023, revised August 2024)
  • The water and wastewater cybersecurity resource set maintained jointly by CISA and EPA, aimed at helping operators assess their own weaknesses and build a response plan

What these documents share is how basic the items are. Reduce operator screens exposed directly to the internet. Change factory default credentials. Put multi-factor authentication on remote paths. Build an asset inventory. Hold an incident response plan in writing. And confirm the plant can be run manually without the control network. The diagnosis is that the gap is in executing fundamentals, not in exotic technology.

The coverage gap for small systems

The harder problem in the water sector is structural. Federal and state attention concentrates on larger systems, while the long tail of very small operators runs comparable equipment with no dedicated staff, no security budget and often no vendor relationship beyond an annual service call. Guidance reaches them as a document to read rather than a program to join. Given the sector profile just described, it is reasonable to assume a meaningful share of real risk sits in that tail.

That is precisely why criteria a utility can apply on its own initiative matter, and why 62443 and SP 800-82 are useful outside any compliance obligation. Neither carries legal force for most operators. Both work as design language, which is the part that survives when there is no auditor coming.

11. A ninety-day order for utilities with no budget

Doing all of the above requires an organization and a budget. Far more places have neither. So the ordering is what matters. Put low cost and high risk reduction first and it comes out like this.

  1. Days 1 to 30 · Build the list. Equipment, circuits, everyone with remote access, everything vendors installed. It does not have to be complete; the aim is to surface entries that nothing explains. The only cost is staff time
  2. Immediately · Clean up credentials. Factory defaults, shared contractor logins, accounts of people who left. These three are the most common and the cheapest holes to close
  3. Days 30 to 60 · Cut direct exposure. Move any path leaving the control zone straight for a public network behind the brokered zone. For paths you cannot move, write down why. That note becomes the basis of the next budget request
  4. Days 30 to 60 · Condition remote access. Retire standing tunnels, then start with per-person accounts and multi-factor authentication
  5. Day 60 · Verify backups and manual operation. Keep configuration and control logic on offline media, and actually rehearse running the plant with the control network gone. That single drill does more to bound incident damage than anything else on this list
  6. Days 60 to 90 · Build records and alerting. Create a route by which the fact that a remote path opened reaches a human. A log nobody reads is post-incident material, not a control
  7. At the next renewal · Move conditions into contracts. Access conditions, an obligation to declare installed equipment, an obligation to notify incidents. The 62443 service provider requirements give you reference wording

The logic of the order is simple. Items one and two cost almost nothing and remove the most risk. Conversely, buying equipment is impossible to place correctly without the list. Tools always come after the inventory.

What is not settled here

These are the items whose evidence chain is not completed in this article. They are kept out of the body, and anyone who needs them is better served checking directly.

  • Statutory citations for US water sector cybersecurity obligations. The body describes the structure of federal guidance and does not cite section numbers of any act or rule. Requirements vary by system size, state primacy and current rulemaking status
  • How many water systems fall under any given federal or state program. No count was verified, and no individual facility or operator is identified anywhere in this article
  • Attribution of specific incidents. Who was behind which intrusion is out of scope. Attribution does not change the defensive design
  • Product-level exposure status. Whether a given vendor or model is affected, and what has been remediated, is not covered. That belongs to vendor security advisories and national response bodies

Frequently asked questions

How is control system security different from ordinary IT security?

The priorities and the shape of failure differ. In IT an incident means data left or a service stopped, and restoring ends it. In control systems it means a pump runs and a valve opens, and water that already flowed does not roll back. So where IT leads with confidentiality, control systems lead with safety and availability. The time scale differs as well: equipment lives past twenty years and there may be only one or two windows a year in which it can be stopped.

If we use a VPN and changed the passwords, can the system be online?

Both raise the threshold; neither replaces a boundary. A design where a stolen credential reaches a controller directly and one where it stops in a brokered zone are different systems with identical passwords. Look at whether the tunnel stands open, how far a session reaches once inside, and whether a named person confirms daily that the path is open.

Is a full disconnection from the internet the safest option?

Isolation is strong but carries side effects. Cut the circuit and removable drives and maintenance laptops take over, which is harder to control. Losing remote visibility delays detection and raises safety risk. The workable goal is not zero connectivity but connectivity whose direction and conditions are controlled.

How is a unidirectional gateway different from a firewall?

A firewall blocks by rule; a unidirectional gateway blocks by structure. Rules open when written wrong, but a return path that is physically absent cannot be opened by a configuration mistake. In exchange you give up remote control and remote updates, so the usual pattern is a state replica outside the gateway that upstream systems talk to instead.

What should a small utility with no budget and no staff do first?

The asset inventory. Equipment not on a list is under no control, and exposure almost always appears on a device that was never listed. After that come credentials, removing direct exposure, and offline backups with a manual operation rehearsal. Buying equipment comes after those.

Conclusion: draw the boundary before counting controls

The recurring mistake in control system security is to start by filling in a control list. Turn on multi-factor authentication, install endpoint protection, collect logs. All of it is necessary, and none of it tells you what is being protected while you do not know what is attached to what.

The order has to run the other way. Build the list, draw the boundaries, name what crosses each one. Do that and do not put a controller on the internet stops being a rule to memorize and becomes a conclusion that falls out of a drawing you made yourself. An organization that reached the conclusion that way also knows what it is accepting whenever it grants an exception. That is the whole difference.
