Forced Heirship in Korea: Complete Guide — Rights, Ratios, Claims & Statute of Limitations

What Korea's Civil Act guarantees heirs even against a will — general information as of June 2026

1. What Is Forced Heirship (Yurubun)?

In Korea, a person generally has the freedom to dispose of their assets during their lifetime through gifts, or by will after death. However, if this freedom were unlimited, close family members could be left with nothing at all. To prevent this, Korea's Civil Act establishes a system called yurubun (유류분) — commonly translated as "forced heirship" or "forced share."

Forced heirship is the minimum portion of a decedent's estate that the law guarantees to certain heirs, regardless of the decedent's will or lifetime gifts. It represents a balance between the decedent's freedom to distribute property and the protection of close family members. The rules are set out in Civil Act Articles 1112 through 1118.

The forced heirship system underwent significant changes in 2024 following a landmark Constitutional Court ruling, and the Civil Act was subsequently amended in 2026. This guide focuses on the current framework as of June 2026.

2. Who Is Entitled to Forced Heirship?

Not every heir can claim a forced share. Civil Act Article 1112 limits forced heirship to specific classes of relatives.

2.1 Current Entitled Heirs (Post-2026 Amendment)

  • Lineal descendants: children, grandchildren, and other downward-line relatives
  • Spouse: the legally married spouse (common-law/de facto spouses are not included)
  • Lineal ascendants: parents, grandparents, and other upward-line relatives

Lineal ascendants only become heirs — and therefore forced heirs — when there are no lineal descendants or surviving spouse. In practice, forced heirship claims are most often brought by children and spouses.

2.2 Siblings: Removed After 2024 Constitutional Court Ruling

Prior to April 2024, Civil Act Article 1112(4) granted siblings a forced share of one-third of their statutory share. The Constitutional Court struck down this provision on April 25, 2024 with a unanimous unconstitutional ruling. The Court held that siblings contribute little to forming the estate and have minimal grounds to expect an inheritance, making the provision unjustifiable. Because the ruling was a direct unconstitutionality finding (rather than merely incompatible with the Constitution), the provision lost effect immediately. The Civil Act was formally amended in September 2024 to delete the provision.

3. Forced Share Ratios — How Much Is Guaranteed?

The forced share is calculated as a fraction of each heir's statutory share under the Civil Act. The ratio depends on the type of heir.

Entitled HeirForced Share Ratio
Lineal descendants1/2 of statutory share
Spouse1/2 of statutory share
Lineal ascendants1/3 of statutory share

Example: Suppose the decedent leaves a spouse and two children. Under Civil Act Article 1009, the statutory shares are: spouse 3/7, each child 2/7. The forced share of the spouse is 3/14 (half of 3/7); the forced share of each child is 1/7 (half of 2/7). Even if the decedent willed the entire estate to a third party, these family members may claim back their forced share.

4. Calculating the Forced Share

Before computing the forced share, it is necessary to determine the base estate for the calculation. Civil Act Article 1113 provides the formula.

The forced share is calculated by adding the value of gifted property to the value of property held by the decedent at the time of commencement of succession, and deducting the total amount of debts.

4.1 The Formula

Base Estate = (Property held at death) + (Gifts made during lifetime) – (Debts)

Gifts to third parties are generally included only if made within one year before death, or if both parties knew the gift would harm forced heirs. However, gifts to co-heirs (other heirs) are included regardless of when they were made.

4.2 Computing the Shortfall

The amount actually recoverable is the forced share shortfall: the forced share minus what the heir has already received.

Shortfall = Forced Share Amount – Special Benefits Already Received (prior gifts/bequests to the heir) – Net Inheritance Received

This calculation can be complex in practice. Professional legal assistance is strongly recommended.

4.3 Contribution Shares

A contribution share (Civil Act Article 1008-2) may be credited to an heir who made special contributions to caring for the decedent or building the estate. Previously, contribution shares were not reflected in forced heirship calculations, creating injustice when caregiving heirs had to return gifts received as compensation for their efforts. The 2024 Constitutional Court ruling and the 2026 amendment addressed this by allowing contribution to be factored in.

5. How to File a Forced Heirship Claim

When a forced heir's share has been infringed, they may assert their right to claim return under Civil Act Article 1115.

5.1 Who to Sue

The respondent is whoever received property that infringes the forced heir's share:

  • Legatee: a person who received property under the decedent's will
  • Donee: a person who received a lifetime gift from the decedent

Claims must be directed first at legatees; claims against donees are only possible if the shortfall cannot be fully satisfied from legacies. Where there are multiple obligors, each bears responsibility in proportion to the value received.

5.2 How to File

  • Out of court: A written demand (e.g., certified mail / registered letter) or oral notice is sufficient to exercise the right. Certified mail is recommended for evidence and to toll the statute of limitations.
  • Civil lawsuit: If the respondent refuses to cooperate, the heir may file a civil action in the competent court.

Return is made in kind (the actual property) where possible, but monetary compensation is allowed where return in kind is impossible or significantly difficult.

6. Statute of Limitations

The right to claim a forced share does not last forever. Civil Act Article 1117 sets the limitations period.

6.1 Short Period: 1 Year from Knowledge

The claim expires one year from the date the heir becomes aware of both the commencement of succession and the existence of a gift or bequest that infringes the forced share. Courts have interpreted "awareness" as knowledge of both facts simultaneously — merely knowing a gift was made is not enough without also knowing it infringes the forced share.

6.2 Long-Stop: 10 Years from Succession

Regardless of whether the heir is aware, the claim expires ten years from the date succession commences (i.e., the date of the decedent's death). This is an absolute deadline; once ten years pass, the claim cannot be filed even if the infringement was only recently discovered.

6.3 Practical Notes

Whichever period expires first extinguishes the claim. For example, if the heir learns of the infringement nine years after the decedent's death, the ten-year period may expire before the one-year period runs. Prompt action is essential. The statute of limitations can be interrupted by filing a lawsuit, obtaining a provisional attachment, or obtaining an acknowledgment of the debt.

7. The 2024 Constitutional Court Ruling and 2026 Reforms

April 25, 2024 was a watershed moment for Korea's forced heirship system. The Constitutional Court issued three types of rulings in a single decision.

7.1 Siblings' Forced Share — Unconstitutional (Immediate Effect)

The Court unanimously struck down Article 1112(4) granting siblings a forced share. The provision lost legal force on the day of the ruling. The Civil Act was formally amended to delete it in September 2024.

7.2 Forfeiture Rules — Constitutionally Incompatible (Legislative Deadline: Dec 31, 2025)

The Court found it unconstitutional that no provision existed to strip forced heirship from heirs who committed serious abuse (prolonged neglect, physical or emotional abuse) against the decedent. The legislature was ordered to enact corrective legislation by December 31, 2025.

7.3 Contribution Not Reflected — Constitutionally Incompatible (Legislative Deadline: Dec 31, 2025)

It was also found unconstitutional that a caregiving heir's contribution-based gifts could be clawed back through forced heirship without any offset for their contribution.

7.4 The 2026 Civil Act Amendment

The National Assembly passed a Civil Act amendment in February 2026, effective March 17, 2026. Key changes include: formal deletion of siblings' forced share; new statutory grounds for forfeiture of forced heirship (serious breach of duty to support, serious criminal acts against the decedent or their family, prolonged abandonment or seriously unreasonable treatment); and integration of contribution share considerations into the forced heirship framework.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a will override forced heirship?

Not entirely. Even a clearly expressed will cannot eliminate the forced share of entitled heirs. However, if an heir qualifies for forfeiture of forced heirship under the new statutory grounds (as of March 2026), the family court may declare that heir's forced share forfeited upon application.

Does renouncing inheritance also waive the forced share?

Yes. An heir who formally renounces their inheritance loses their status as an heir and therefore cannot claim a forced share. In the case of a qualified acceptance (limited liability), the situation is more nuanced and requires professional advice.

Does a gift made 20 years ago count toward the forced share calculation?

Gifts to third parties are generally included only if made within one year before death (or made with knowledge of harm to forced heirs). However, gifts to co-heirs are included regardless of how long ago they were made. This is a frequent source of disputes in multi-decade estate planning scenarios.

Do I need to go to court to make a forced heirship claim?

No. You may make the claim out of court — for example, by sending a formal written demand. However, if the other party refuses to cooperate, litigation will be necessary. Bear in mind the statute of limitations: 1 year from knowledge, 10 years from death.
