Account Hacked? Complete Recovery Guide

Immediate Actions, Service-by-Service Recovery, Secondary Damage Prevention, and Long-Term Protection — A Practical Guide for Everyday Users

2026-06-30 Tech & IT

Introduction: Account Compromise Can Happen to Anyone

You receive a login alert from a city you have never visited. Emails you never wrote appear in your Sent folder. Your password suddenly stops working. These events are not abstract threats — credential stuffing attacks (where stolen username-password pairs from one breach are automatically tried on hundreds of other sites) and phishing campaigns run at massive scale in 2026. What matters most is responding quickly and in the right order.

This guide is for everyday users who suspect — or have confirmed — that an account has been compromised. It does not instruct or assist in accessing accounts that belong to others. All content is based on official service documentation and publicly available security guidance. Current as of June 2026.

1. Warning Signs — Recognizing a Compromised Account

Attackers often work quietly. A compromised account can go unnoticed for days while information is harvested or access is sold. Watch for these indicators.

1.1 Login and Access Indicators

  • A password-change confirmation email you never requested
  • Login alerts from unrecognized devices or locations — especially foreign IPs
  • Unable to log in with a password you know is correct (attacker may have changed it)
  • Recovery email address or phone number changed without your action

1.2 Account Activity Indicators

  • Emails, DMs, or posts sent from your account that you did not write
  • Contacts, followers, or subscriptions added or removed without your action
  • Account settings changed — name, profile photo, 2FA configuration — without your input
  • Friends or colleagues reporting strange messages supposedly from you

1.3 Financial Indicators

  • Unauthorized charges, transfers, or point redemptions
  • Fraud alerts from your bank or card provider
  • Sign-up confirmation emails from services you never joined (attacker using your account to register elsewhere)
Quick check: Google — myaccount.google.com → "Recent security activity." Microsoft — account.microsoft.com → "Recent activity." Most major platforms offer a similar login history view. Look for unfamiliar IP addresses or device types.

2. Immediate Response — Eight Steps in Order

The sequence matters. Changing your password before scanning for malware is counterproductive — a keylogger will capture the new password within minutes.

Step 1 — Scan Your Device First
Before doing anything else, update your antivirus software and run a full scan. If malware is present, remove it and restart from a clean state. If you are uncertain about your current device, switch to a trusted device (a smartphone or a different computer) for the steps below.
Step 2 — Sign Out of All Other Sessions
While you still have access, go to the security settings of the compromised service and select "Sign out of all devices" or "End other sessions." This immediately revokes any active sessions the attacker may hold.
Step 3 — Change Your Password Immediately
Set a new password of at least 16 characters, combining uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols. It must be unique to this service — never reuse a password from anywhere else. Do not revert to any previously used password.
Step 4 — Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)
Set up 2FA at the same time as your password change. Prefer an authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, Authy) over SMS. SMS codes are vulnerable to SIM-swapping attacks; app-based one-time passwords are not.
Step 5 — Verify Recovery Information
Check that the recovery email address and phone number on file are yours. If an attacker has replaced them with their own, changing your password will not prevent them from triggering another reset. Replace any unfamiliar recovery contacts immediately.
Step 6 — Audit Connected Apps and Permissions
In security settings, review all third-party apps with access to your account. Revoke access for anything you do not recognize. On Google: myaccount.google.com → Security → "Third-party apps with account access."
Step 7 — Act on Financial Accounts Immediately
If payment methods, bank accounts, or cards are linked to the compromised account, contact those financial institutions immediately. Request a transaction freeze or fraud monitoring. File a police report if unauthorized transactions have occurred.
Step 8 — Notify Your Contacts
The attacker may have already sent phishing or scam messages to your contacts using your account. Let them know that your account was compromised and that they should not click any recent links they received from you.

3. Service-by-Service Account Recovery

If you are completely locked out and cannot change your password, use each service's official recovery flow.

3.1 Google (Gmail) Account Recovery

Go to accounts.google.com/signin/recovery. Google will prompt you for your recovery phone number, recovery email address, or a previous password. Attempting recovery from a device and network you normally use increases your chances — Google's system analyzes past login behavior to verify your identity. Once access is restored, enable 2FA immediately and refresh all recovery contacts.

3.2 Microsoft (Outlook) Account Recovery

Submit the account recovery form at account.live.com/acsr. Provide as much accurate detail as possible: previous passwords, devices typically used, recent contacts emailed, and geographic location. According to Microsoft's official support documentation, consumer account recovery is handled by an automated system — the more historical detail you supply, the more likely it is to succeed. Expect a response within 24 hours.

3.3 Instagram Account Recovery

On the login screen, tap "Get more help" then "I think my account was hacked." Instagram will guide you through a video selfie verification process that compares your face against your profile photos. This is the primary recovery path when an attacker has changed your email and phone number.

3.4 Facebook (Meta) Account Recovery

Visit facebook.com/hacked. Meta offers recovery via email, phone number, or by having three to five trusted friends send you a recovery code. If none of those options are available, an identity document review can be requested through the official help center.

Warning: Avoid third-party "account recovery services" advertised on social media. These are almost always scams that will charge money and take no effective action — or worse, steal additional credentials from you.

4. Stopping Secondary Damage

Recovering one account is not enough. If you reuse passwords across services, the attacker likely has a list and is already trying them elsewhere.

4.1 Audit All Accounts with the Same Password

List every service where you have used the same password (or a variation of it) as the compromised account. Change all of them — prioritizing email, banking, shopping, and payment accounts. Check haveibeenpwned.com to see if your email address appears in known data breaches; this helps you identify where attackers may have obtained your credentials.

4.2 Watch for Follow-on Phishing

After a compromise, attackers often launch targeted phishing attacks using personal details they found in the hacked account. Be suspicious of any "account recovery" or "urgent security alert" emails in the days following the incident. Always navigate to official websites by typing the URL directly into your browser — never through links in emails or texts.

4.3 Check Email Forwarding Rules

A common attacker technique is to set up silent forwarding rules so they continue receiving copies of your email even after you change your password. Open your email settings, find the forwarding or filters section, and delete any rules you did not create.

4.4 Assess What Personal Information Was Exposed

Review what was stored in the compromised account. If government ID numbers, financial account details, or sensitive documents were accessible, you face a higher risk of identity theft. Contact relevant agencies or financial institutions proactively to request additional monitoring.

5. Prevention — Never Getting Hacked Again

5.1 Use a Password Manager

The only practical way to use a unique password for every service is to let a password manager generate and store them. You remember one strong master password; the manager handles the rest. Bitwarden (free, open-source) and 1Password (paid, polished) are well-regarded choices as of 2026. A password manager does not just save convenience — it eliminates the entire attack surface of password reuse.

5.2 Enable 2FA on Every Service That Offers It

Even if your password is stolen, 2FA means the attacker cannot log in without the second factor. Prefer authenticator apps over SMS. For the highest-value accounts (primary email, banking, cloud storage), consider a hardware security key (such as YubiKey) — it is phishing-resistant by design.

5.3 Switch to Passkeys Where Available

Passkeys eliminate the password entirely. A cryptographic private key stored on your device is unlocked by your biometric (fingerprint or face recognition). No password is ever transmitted, making phishing and credential theft structurally impossible. As of June 2026, Google, Apple, Microsoft, GitHub, Amazon, and PayPal all support passkeys. Enabling them is strongly recommended wherever the option is available.

5.4 Keep Software Updated and Stay Vigilant Against Phishing

Attackers frequently exploit known vulnerabilities in operating systems, browsers, and apps. Keep everything updated. Before clicking any link in an email or text message, verify the sender and inspect the URL carefully. When in doubt, navigate directly to the official website.

5.5 Conduct Regular Security Reviews

Set a reminder every six months to review login history, connected apps, and recovery information for your key accounts. Sign up for breach notifications at haveibeenpwned.com so you are alerted automatically if your credentials appear in a new data dump.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. I changed my password but got hacked again — why?

The most common reasons are: malware still present on the device capturing the new password, the same password reused elsewhere, or recovery information (email, phone) that still belongs to the attacker. Follow all eight steps in order, and do not skip the device scan or recovery-info verification. If compromise recurs, contact your national cybersecurity agency for assistance.

Q. I lost my 2FA authenticator app. How do I get back in?

Most services provide one-time backup codes when you first set up 2FA — store these somewhere safe offline (printed on paper or in a secure offline location). If you have them, use one to log in and reconfigure 2FA with a new device. If you have no backup codes, use the service's account recovery process, which typically involves identity verification.

Q. My account sent scam messages to my contacts. Am I legally responsible?

In most jurisdictions, you are not legally liable for actions an attacker took using your compromised account, as you are the victim. However, documenting the incident (screenshots, timestamps) and filing a report promptly strengthens your position. Notify affected contacts so they do not fall victim to the scam, and advise them they can report the incident independently if they suffered losses.

Conclusion

Account compromise is alarming, but the damage can be contained with a rapid, ordered response. The core sequence: scan for malware → sign out all sessions → change password → enable 2FA → verify recovery info. For long-term security, adopt a password manager, use app-based 2FA on every account, and switch to passkeys wherever supported. Security is not a one-time event — build it as a habit, and act before a breach rather than after.
