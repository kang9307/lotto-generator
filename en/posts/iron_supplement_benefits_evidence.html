Iron Supplements and the Evidence - Where Confirmed Deficiency and Everything Else Part Ways

An evidence table that separates what is established from what is only advertised, the factors that genuinely move absorption, how high the alternate-day dosing argument actually stands, and the harm that falls on people who never needed iron - the groundwork for reading your own results

Aug 18, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before anything else: this article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Whether to take iron, and at what dose, should be discussed with a clinician. Every figure below is a value recorded in a study or a guideline, not an intake recommendation for any individual. In particular, iron is not a supplement to start without testing - fatigue has many causes, and someone whose iron is not low gets no benefit while keeping all of the risk. If you are already taking a prescribed medicine, any decision to adjust it belongs to the clinician who prescribed it.

Introduction: a supplement that sits in a different place from the rest

Take supplements apart one by one and the conclusions start to rhyme. The mechanism is plausible but human results do not separate from placebo; early small studies looked good and the effect collapsed toward zero as trials got larger. Iron does not belong on that list.

Iron has a clear indication, and within that indication the effect is established. Put iron into someone whose anaemia comes from a lack of iron and haemoglobin rises and stores refill. That is not a contested question. And yet the same compound offers no benefit and only risk to someone who does not need it. The body has no active route for getting rid of iron, so regulation happens entirely on the way in. Override that regulation and iron accumulates.

So the axis of this article is not good or bad but asymmetry. Where the established story ends, where the advertising copy begins, and what this compound does to a person who is not standing on the established side. If you want the disease itself - causes, diagnosis, management - the complete guide to iron deficiency anaemia is the right page, and if breathlessness came first, see anaemia and breathlessness. Other compounds handled the same way include creatine and the evidence, multivitamins and the evidence and coenzyme Q10 and the evidence.

1. What iron does in the body - and why regulation sits only at the entrance

Iron's job is to grab oxygen and let it go again. Haemoglobin in red cells and myoglobin in muscle do that work, and the proteins that shuttle electrons in mitochondria carry iron too. That is why a shortage does not show up as anaemia alone: it spreads first as a general inability to produce force.

  • Oxygen transport - the core component of haemoglobin and myoglobin
  • Energy metabolism - a cofactor in the electron transport proteins of mitochondria
  • Site and route of absorption - iron enters in the duodenum and proximal jejunum. Ferrous iron crosses into the enterocyte through DMT1, and the only exit from the cell is ferroportin. If gastric surgery or acid suppression changes conditions in that stretch, absorption changes with it

Here is the mechanism that runs through the whole article. Hepcidin, a hormone made by the liver, binds ferroportin and causes it to be internalised and degraded. With the exit gone, the iron an intestinal cell has taken up stays trapped as ferritin and is shed when the cell is shed. When body iron is high, hepcidin rises and absorption is locked down.

That structure explains two things at once. The first is that what you swallow and what you absorb are not proportional. Reviews of absorption physiology use roughly 10 mg ingested to 1 mg absorbed as a working figure. The second is that you cannot regulate by excretion. There is no device for removing an excess, so all of the machinery for preventing one sits at the entrance. The alternate-day dosing discussion later, and the reason excess is dangerous, both descend from this paragraph.

2. Where the effect is established - confirmed iron deficiency

Start with the solid side. If this part wobbles, every later judgement wobbles with it.

A Cochrane systematic review of menstruating adult women and adolescent girls pooled 67 trials with 8,506 participants. In groups taking 30 to 60 mg of elemental iron daily for at least five days a week, the review found the following.

  • The prevalence of anaemia and iron deficiency fell
  • Haemoglobin and iron stores rose
  • Exercise performance improved and symptomatic fatigue was reduced
  • In exchange, constipation and abdominal pain increased - the authors wrote plainly that iron supplementation clearly increases the risk of gastrointestinal side effects

The clinical course points the same way. Seoul National University Hospital's medical information page describes oral iron treatment as producing a reduction in fatigue starting within two to three days, increased red cell production by five to six days, and normalised haemoglobin by around two months. And here is the part that matters most: it states that treatment continues for about six more months after haemoglobin has normalised, in order to refill stores. The moment the number looks normal and the moment the warehouse is full are not the same moment. These are figures from clinical practice, not intake advice.

Draw the boundary of the established claim precisely. Everything above describes correcting a state of shortage. Iron reduces fatigue and correcting a shortage of iron reduces fatigue look like the same sentence and are not, and advertising copy almost always uses the first one.

It is only fair to note that the evidence sits lower once you move to screening and blanket supplementation. In 2024 the US Preventive Services Task Force issued an I statement for iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia in pregnancy, meaning the evidence is insufficient to weigh the benefits and harms of screening and supplementation. The supporting systematic review found across 17 trials and 24,023 participants that supplementation lowered the risk of maternal iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia at term, but there were no studies on the benefits or harms of screening itself. Correcting a deficiency and running a programme that filters and supplements people without symptoms are different questions.

4. Claims that are unestablished or come with conditions

4.1 Fatigue - the conditional clause gets deleted

The conditional clause is the first thing to disappear from iron advertising. What the Cochrane review established is that symptomatic fatigue fell in a population that was short of iron, and that population was losing iron through menstruation to begin with. No higher-level evidence produces the same result in people who are not deficient.

What about the band where deficiency exists but has not yet become anaemia? A review built on Cochrane material pooled 21 trials and 3,514 participants looking at intravenous iron, and fatigue improved overall while quality of life showed no overall difference. Three things have to be read together here. First, this is intravenous iron, not an oral supplement, so the finding cannot simply be transplanted onto a tablet. Second, the people whose fatigue improved had confirmed iron deficiency, so the conditional clause survives here too. Third, the fact that quality of life did not separate in the same study is a signal that the conclusion moves depending on which outcome you choose.

4.2 Exercise performance - visible only when the deficit is deep

The exercise literature is genuinely mixed. A systematic review of iron-deficient but non-anaemic athletes examined 12 studies with 283 participants and found performance improvements in about half of them, with one striking regularity: every study reporting improvement had used a treatment threshold of ferritin at or below 20 µg/L. A separate review restricted to non-anaemic iron-deficient women found no improvement in performance measures such as time to exhaustion or time trials.

The direction converges. The deeper the deficit, the more visible the effect; the shallower it is, the more the effect disappears. Put another way, there is no basis for using iron as a performance enhancer in someone who is not deficient. Training hard does not automatically make you short of iron either, and that is confirmed by testing rather than by feel.

4.3 Hair - separate association from effect

Several studies report an association between telogen effluvium and low ferritin. But an association existing and topping it up helping are statements at different levels, and the evidence for the second is thin. There are reports that where ferritin was not severely low, supplementation raised the ferritin number without producing an observable change in hair, and the same body of literature contains the observation that prescribing iron on the assumption that low stores cause hair loss is not itself evidence-based practice. If hair loss is the concern, identifying the cause comes first, and that is dermatology's territory.

5. Food and absorption - heme, non-heme, and what moves the number

5.1 The two kinds of iron do not come through the same door

Iron in meat, liver, fish and oysters is heme iron; iron in grains, legumes and vegetables is non-heme iron. Reviews of absorption physiology put heme iron absorption at 15 to 35% and note that despite being a small share of the diet it contributes 10% or more of total absorbed iron. Non-heme iron is absorbed less well and, more importantly, its absorption swings sharply with whatever it is eaten alongside. Heme iron swings far less.

The food list given by Seoul National University Hospital's medical information page overlaps with that distinction. Beef, beef liver, chicken, fish and oysters appear alongside seaweeds such as gim, miyeok and dasima, and beans, red beans, pine nuts and sesame; coffee and black and green tea are listed as things to limit because they lower absorption.

5.3 Vitamin C - the clearest case of absorption and outcome parting ways

The advice to take vitamin C with iron is widespread, and on absorption physiology alone it has a basis. But the trial that asked directly whether people recover faster gave a different answer. In a 2020 randomised trial in adults newly diagnosed with iron deficiency anaemia, the change in haemoglobin at two weeks was 2.00 g/dL with vitamin C versus 1.84 g/dL without, meeting the equivalence criterion, and the change in ferritin at eight weeks was 35.75 ng/mL versus 34.48 ng/mL, with no difference.

The lesson is not that vitamin C is useless but the habit of separating levels. An effect confirmed in a test tube or an absorption measurement frequently fails to carry through to a clinical outcome, and marketing copy is almost always manufactured at the earlier level. And as the next section shows, for someone with an iron overload condition this combination points entirely the other way.

6. Alternate-day dosing - how far the current argument has got

This is the most discussed iron topic of recent years. The evidence splits into two layers, and skipping which layer you mean is what makes the claim balloon.

6.1 The absorption layer - fairly solid

An open-label randomised trial published in The Lancet Haematology in 2017 measured absorption directly using stable isotope labelled iron. In iron-depleted women, fractional absorption was 21.8% with alternate-day dosing and 16.3% with daily dosing, and total absorption was higher on the alternate-day schedule too. The mechanism is the last row of the table in 5.2: oral iron raises serum hepcidin and that rise persists for 24 hours, cutting absorption of a dose taken later the same day or the following day. The conclusion was that alternate-day, single-dose administration optimises absorption.

6.2 The outcome layer - still knee-high

Absorption is not the outcome. What people want to know is when haemoglobin recovers, and the answer at that level is much flatter.

  • A 2024 systematic review (7 studies, 544 participants, 5 of them randomised) found that between-group differences in haemoglobin improvement were generally nonsignificant, with one exception in which daily dosing showed the greater gain at three to six weeks. Ferritin improvements were comparable
  • A 2025 meta-analysis (11 trials, 1,014 participants) reported haemoglobin of 12.84 g/dL with daily dosing and 12.52 g/dL with alternate-day dosing, essentially the same, and other iron indices did not differ significantly either. The overall certainty of the evidence was rated very low because of heterogeneity. What did separate was tolerability - gastrointestinal effects such as nausea, metallic taste and constipation were less frequent on the alternate-day schedule, and the risk ratio for metallic taste was 1.53 in favour of the alternate-day arm
  • Guidelines have been moving in this direction. The 2024 American Gastroenterological Association clinical practice update advises giving oral iron no more than once a day and notes that every-other-day dosing may be better tolerated in some patients with similar or equal absorption. Guidance from the American College of Gastroenterology and the European Hematology Association the same year, and a 2025 expert consensus, likewise cite alternate-day administration as a strategy for improving tolerability and adherence

To put it plainly: alternate-day dosing has not been established as a way to recover faster, and is supported as a way to feel less awful. Given how much adherence drives recovery, that is not a small thing - but whether to actually change your interval is a decision to make with the clinician who prescribed the iron.

7. Common adverse effects and what to do about them

What makes people stop taking iron is usually not a lack of effect but an unhappy stomach. The Cochrane review did not soften this: it stated that iron supplementation clearly increases the risk of gastrointestinal side effects. What rose were constipation and abdominal pain.

  • Constipation, abdominal pain, nausea and metallic taste - the most common. This is the cost reported alongside the benefit in the Cochrane review, and for metallic taste the 2025 meta-analysis put the risk ratio at 1.53 for daily dosing
  • Black stools - caused by unabsorbed iron. But attributing every black stool to the supplement can hide a gastrointestinal bleed. If it is new, or comes with abdominal pain or dizziness, medical assessment comes first
  • The direction of the response - what guidelines mention is not exceeding once-daily dosing, and trying an alternate-day interval. Both amount to adjusting a prescription, so neither is yours alone to decide
  • Other medicines you take - acid-reducing drugs substantially lower iron absorption, and iron in turn lowers the absorption of certain drugs. The specific intervals are set by the product labelling and by your pharmacist

8. When iron should not be taken, and the hazards of excess

This is where the compound parts company with other supplements. Something with weak evidence usually does nothing at all when you take it. Iron is not like that.

8.1 When the body already has too much

Hereditary haemochromatosis is a condition in which iron is absorbed beyond need and accumulates in the liver, heart and endocrine organs. The C282Y and H63D variants of the HFE gene are described as the main causes, with a reported C282Y homozygosity rate of 0.4% and heterozygosity of 9.2% in European populations and 0.5% and 9% in North America. On the diagnostic side, ferritin above 300 ng/mL in men and above 150 to 200 ng/mL in menstruating women raises suspicion of overload, and fasting transferrin saturation above 45% strengthens diagnostic confidence. Patient education in this setting is unambiguous: strictly avoid iron supplements and iron-containing multivitamins, and also vitamin C, which increases absorption.

Inherited disease is not the only route. Repeated transfusion therapy for chronic haematologic disorders including thalassaemia major or intermedia, myelodysplastic syndromes and aplastic anaemia, along with chronic haemolysis, sideroblastic anaemia, alcohol-associated liver disease and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, are all described as backgrounds to secondary iron overload.

8.2 Acute iron poisoning in children - reported as a real cause of death

Keep iron supplements out of reach of children. The US Food and Drug Administration labelling regulation requires iron-containing products to carry the warning that accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6, that the product must be kept out of reach of children, and that a doctor or poison control centre should be called immediately in case of accidental overdose. If a child may have swallowed iron, seek emergency care immediately even in the absence of symptoms.

The toxicity bands and clinical course described in the literature follow. The figures are elemental iron, and they appear here not as guidance for any action but to show why storage matters.

  • Below 20 mg per kg of body weight is generally nontoxic; 20 to 60 mg per kg may produce moderate symptoms; above 60 mg per kg is associated with severe and potentially fatal toxicity
  • The course is described in five stages - gastrointestinal symptoms at 0.5 to 6 hours, then a 6 to 24 hour period in which the child appears to have improved, then shock and organ injury at 6 to 72 hours, progressive hepatic failure at 12 to 96 hours, and late complications such as strictures at 2 to 8 weeks. The second stage is the trap - there is a window in which the child looks fine, and that window is not grounds for reassurance
  • The 2015 annual report of the American Association of Poison Control Centers recorded 4,072 single exposures to iron or iron salts, of which 2,036 occurred in children aged 5 years or younger
  • Iron is described as accounting for the majority of fatal paediatric poisonings involving vitamins and supplements in the United States

Getting a feel for how much sits in a single adult tablet makes the danger concrete. The elemental iron contents given in the literature are roughly 65 mg (about 20%) in a 325 mg ferrous sulfate tablet, about 36 mg (about 12%) in a 300 mg ferrous gluconate tablet, and about 33 mg (about 33%) in a 100 mg ferrous fumarate tablet. Prenatal vitamins are listed at 60 to 90 mg per tablet and children's multivitamins at 5 to 19 mg. For a child weighing in the low tens of kilograms, that means a handful of adult tablets lands straight in the dangerous band.

8.3 Starting iron without looking for the cause

The third hazard is not the compound but the sequence. In adults, iron deficiency is usually a consequence rather than a cause. The British Society of Gastroenterology guideline on iron deficiency anaemia in adults states that a diagnostic trial of iron treatment should not be used in men and postmenopausal women, and that gastrointestinal sources of bleeding need to be excluded. Conversely, it does not recommend routine bidirectional endoscopy in premenopausal women without gastrointestinal symptoms, a strong family history of colorectal cancer, or age above 50. The same guideline notes that age, sex, haemoglobin concentration and mean cell volume are independent predictors of gastrointestinal cancer risk in this setting.

Translated to the consumer's position, those sentences say this. Start iron on your own and you may paper over a signal that testing was supposed to reveal, leaving the numbers slightly improved. The reason I am tired so I will take iron is dangerous is not that it does nothing, but that it does just enough to delay the answer.

9. Confirming it by testing - ferritin and the inflammation trap

The usual marker for iron status is serum ferritin. The problem is that different bodies attach different thresholds to the same marker, and that gap creates most of the confusion.

The 2020 World Health Organization guideline sets it out as follows. Below 12 µg/L from birth to 59 months and below 15 µg/L from age 5 through adulthood indicate iron deficiency, but in the presence of infection or inflammation the thresholds become below 30 µg/L and below 70 µg/L respectively. On the overload side, above 150 µg/L in healthy women and 200 µg/L in healthy men, and above 500 µg/L in non-healthy populations, indicate a need for further evaluation. The guideline explicitly states that markers of inflammation should be assessed alongside ferritin and ferritin adjusted as necessary.

The 2020 American Gastroenterological Association guideline uses a different threshold. For diagnosing iron deficiency in patients who have anaemia it makes a strong recommendation, with high quality evidence, to use 45 ng/mL rather than 15 ng/mL. In a synthesis of 55 studies, ferritin below 45 ng/mL had a sensitivity of 85% (95% CI 82 to 87) and a specificity of 92% (91 to 94), whereas below 15 ng/mL the sensitivity was only 59% (55 to 62). The choice weights avoiding missed diagnoses.

  • The two thresholds are not a contradiction but different purposes. A standard for measuring a population's status and a standard for not missing a cause in an anaemic patient have no reason to coincide
  • Ferritin is an acute phase protein. With inflammation, infection or liver disease the number can look normal while stores are empty
  • That is why in chronic inflammation or chronic kidney disease, markers such as C-reactive protein, transferrin saturation and soluble transferrin receptor may be needed alongside it. Clinical descriptions likewise read haemoglobin below 13 g/dL in men and 12 g/dL in women, ferritin usually below 15 ng/mL and serum iron at or below 50 µg/dL together rather than one at a time

The conclusion is simple. This is not a marker you can self-assess by looking up a single ferritin number. Which threshold applies, whether inflammation adjustment is needed, and what to examine next are all matters for a clinician to judge.

Conclusion: judgement turns on a single point

Iron is not a compound whose problem is weak evidence. It is a compound whose evidence rests on a condition, and the condition gets deleted in the advertising. Give iron to someone short of iron and haemoglobin rises, stores fill and fatigue falls. That is not in dispute. What that same compound does for someone who is not short of it has no higher-level evidence behind the expectation.

And the asymmetry does not stop at a benefit converging on zero. That the body has no device for shedding iron, that overload conditions list it as something to avoid, that a few adult tablets are a different quantity to a child, that adult iron deficiency is usually a consequence rather than a cause - these all stack in the same direction. For someone who does not need it, an iron supplement is not a harmless choice.

  • Testing comes first. Fatigue has many causes, and taking iron without knowing your iron status masks the real one. Nor can you self-assess from a single ferritin number - thresholds differ between bodies, and inflammation pushes the number up
  • If deficiency is confirmed, look for the cause at the same time. In men and postmenopausal women especially, self-medication covers the signal
  • When copy targets fatigue, performance or hair, check whether the conditional clause has been deleted. The evidence came from populations with confirmed deficiency
  • Alternate-day dosing is supported as a way to feel less awful. It is not yet evidence of faster recovery
  • If there are children in the house, where you store it is the most practical safety measure you have. If ingestion is suspected, seek emergency care immediately even without symptoms

Reduced to one sentence: iron is a compound where you have to ask who it is for before you ask what the evidence says, and whether you are that person is answered by a blood test rather than by how you feel.
