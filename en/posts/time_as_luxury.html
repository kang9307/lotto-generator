If Time Is a Luxury, What Is Actually Scarce - Measuring Discretion and Unbroken Blocks

The day is the same length for everyone. What differs is how much of it you get to decide

2026-09-08 Trends & Culture

The sentence 「the real luxury is time」 is almost never contradicted. That is precisely why it survives so well and does so little. Once you have nodded, there is nothing left to do with it.

Making the sentence useful requires filtering one word. A luxury is something that some people have a great deal of and others have very little of. The day, however, is 24 hours long for everybody. It is 24 hours for a billionaire and 24 hours for someone on a rotating night shift. In terms of raw quantity, time is the most evenly distributed resource in human history, and no amount of money moves the number.

So if 「time is a luxury」 is pointing at something real, the thing it is pointing at is not time. This piece takes one axis and stays on it. When we call time a luxury, what is genuinely scarce?

Every figure below comes from a published statistic or a published paper, and each one is given with its collecting body, reference period, sample and conditions. Korean statistics and non-Korean studies were gathered under different designs, so they are not blended into a single conclusion. Items whose wording could not be pinned down are gathered in a later section together with the reason.

What the essay that prompted this actually says

On 5 September 2026 a short piece titled The Real Luxuries In Life appeared on feld.com. It is a personal essay written by Brad Feld as he approached his sixty-first birthday. By his own account the list in it is not his own composition: Amy sent it to him as a text message, he reproduced it, and he added his reflections on the seasonal turn from summer into autumn.

The list has twelve items. Time, health, a quiet mind, slow mornings, the ability to travel, rest without guilt, a good night of sleep, calm and boring days, meaningful conversations, home-cooked meals, people you love, and people who love you back. In the order given, time comes first.

What is absent is worth recording too. There are no statistics. There are no citations to research. Not a single number appears. The only outbound link is to a post the author himself wrote in 2015. The piece is not built to advance a claim and support it with evidence; it is built to enumerate what one person finds valuable, and reading it that way is the accurate reading. Twelve items are a list, not twelve propositions.

Even so, the list has structure. Some of the twelve are close to purchasable. Some arrive only through relationships. And some only exist if the shape of the day permits them. Slow mornings, rest without guilt and calm boring days are of that kind. Those three depend not on what a person owns but on how their day has been cut up. From that point onward the subject becomes countable, and countable is where this article goes.

The scarce thing is discretion, not time

Start by separating the words. Three different things are stacked underneath the single noun 「time」, and mixing them is what makes the conversation go nowhere.

  • Quantity - 24 hours in a day, 168 in a week. Identical for everyone. It cannot be increased and it cannot be reduced, which is exactly why it cannot be a luxury.
  • Discretion - the share of those hours whose content and timing you set. This varies enormously between people. Two workers on the same eight-hour day hold different resources if one chooses the start hour and the other is told it.
  • Blocks - the size of the stretches that run without interruption. Equal totals arranged differently permit entirely different work. Six thirty-minute pieces and one three-hour stretch are not the same three hours.

The sentence 「time is a luxury」 is reaching for the second and third of these. The first cannot be a luxury at all. For the word to apply, there has to be a division between the people who have it and the people who do not, and the length of the day does not divide.

ResourceSame for everyone?How it is measuredWhere it can be changed
Quantity of timeYes. 24 hours a dayNo measurement needed. It is a constantNowhere

SpeedVariesTime taken to finish the same piece of workTools, skill, delegation

The fourth row is there deliberately. Conversations about time almost always slide into the fourth row: finish faster and time will be left over. But if speed rises while discretion stays flat, whatever is freed gets absorbed immediately by something else. The pattern of adding tools and finding the workload unchanged has already been taken apart from another angle, and that axis is not reopened here.

Two hours in fragments is a different resource

Discretion and blocks travel together but they are not the same thing. Discretion can exist without blocks: nobody is summoning you, yet something has to be checked every half hour. Blocks can exist without discretion: eight uninterrupted hours during which you must remain at a fixed post.

The practical hinge that separates them is notice. If you learn in the morning that a meeting starts in three hours, those three hours remain a whole block. If the same meeting is announced ten minutes beforehand, everything from morning to that moment converts into standby. The time consumed on paper is one hour of meeting. What was actually lost is a three-hour block, and nothing in any calendar records that loss.

This distinction matters because the usual arithmetic gets it wrong. If you calculate slack by subtracting meeting hours from the day, an ambush meeting and a scheduled meeting score identically. Yet the remaining time supports completely different work. Slack is a question of arrangement, not subtraction. What a calendar records is the interval a commitment occupied, never the effect that interval had on its surroundings. So part of what looks empty on the calendar was never usable in the first place.

  • A commitment announced in advance leaves the time before and after it intact as blocks.
  • A commitment announced without notice consumes time that never happened. Because its arrival is unknown, the whole surrounding stretch gets filled with short tasks only.
  • A commitment with no stated end time takes the whole afternoon behind it hostage. If it might run two hours or four, nothing can be placed after it.
  • When times set by other people are scattered across the day, every gap between them becomes a fragment. It is the distribution, not the count, that determines what blocks survive.

Blocks are usually lost not inside the work but at the seams around it. The same shape appears when tracing where organisational gains leak away: the loss is at the boundary rather than in the task.

What time poverty means in the literature

Everything so far has been definitional. Now to the measured part. The research literature has a name for this territory: time poverty.

The reference treatment is a paper by Laura M. Giurge, Ashley V. Whillans and Colin West published in Nature Human Behaviour, volume 4, pages 993 to 1003, in 2020. It frames time poverty as the persistent sense of having too many things to do and not enough time in which to do them. The abstract opens from a specific observation: over the past two decades global wealth has risen, and material affluence has not converted into time affluence.

The detail worth noticing in that definition is that the measured object is a report rather than a clock. Time poverty is not defined by a threshold such as fewer than so many leisure hours per day. It is defined by whether the person says they feel that way. That choice carries an advantage and a cost together.

  • The advantage - it separates two people whose clocks agree but whose situations do not. Three continuous hours of leisure and six half-hour scraps read identically on a stopwatch and differently on a questionnaire, so the block distinction from the previous section partially enters the measure.
  • The cost - self-reports move with expectations, reference groups and question wording. The same person can answer differently in the same week, and cross-national comparisons absorb differences in reporting culture on top of that.
  • The genre - this article reports no new experiment. It is a perspective piece that reviews existing work and argues for a direction. Quoting a sentence from it as though it were an experimental finding puts the claim on the wrong layer.

One contrast recurs throughout the paper. Billions of dollars are spent every year to relieve material poverty, while time poverty is overlooked or actively worsened. That contrast is the form of a policy argument, not a measured value, and reading it that way keeps it from blending into the numbers that follow.

What happens when you buy time with money

The empirical study cited most often in this area is a different one. Ashley V. Whillans, Elizabeth W. Dunn, Paul Smeets, Rene Bekkers and Michael I. Norton published it in PNAS, volume 114, issue 32, pages 8523 to 8527, in 2017. The title argues that buying time promotes happiness, and the structure has two layers: surveys and a field experiment.

The survey layer totals 6,271 respondents across four countries. The composition is as follows.

Two survey figures get quoted repeatedly. One is that 28.2 percent of respondents said they spent money to buy themselves time each month. The other is that in the preregistered survey, using a broader definition of what counts, the same figure rose to 50 percent. The same behaviour nearly doubled in prevalence when the wording changed, which is itself a useful warning about how subjective indicators behave.

The field experiment is designed like this. Sixty working adults in Vancouver received 40 dollars on each of two consecutive weekends. On one weekend the money went to a time-saving purchase and on the other to a material purchase, with the order counterbalanced across participants. At five in the afternoon they were phoned and asked about positive affect, negative affect and time stress for that day.

  • Positive affect was higher on the time-saving day (effect size 0.33, p=0.007).
  • Negative affect on the same day was lower (effect size 0.33, p=0.009).
  • Time stress was lower (effect size 0.36, p=0.004).
  • The effect was reported as not moderated by income or wealth. The authors take this to mean the benefit is not confined to the rich.

That last item invites misreading, so the condition belongs next to it. Income failing to moderate the effect means it failed to moderate within this sample, and the authors state in the paper that their samples include relatively few people at the lowest rungs of the income spectrum. A finding that income differences did not change the effect among people who could spend 40 dollars is not a finding about people who could not.

How far that result can be pushed

Psychology and happiness research have spent the past decade working through a replication problem. The values above are therefore better handled as 「reported under these conditions」 than as 「established」. The table below gathers those conditions in one place.

If one row is worth carrying away it is the third. A value carried by the conditions sixty people, forty dollars, one phone call per day circulates in public as the sentence 「buying time makes you happier」, and in that translation every condition falls off. Just as the twelve items in the prompting essay were a list rather than a set of propositions, this number is a conditional observation rather than a law.

There is also a boundary on how much of the problem this axis covers at all. What money buys is mostly work that can be handed to somebody else. Unannounced messages, commitments with open ends, and other people's fixed times scattered through the day are not purchasable, and those are the main places where blocks are destroyed.

Where the Korean day actually goes

From here the figures are Korean public statistics. They were collected under different designs and on different populations from the studies above, so the values are not mixed. Readers elsewhere should treat these as a worked example of the method rather than as figures for their own country, since working hours, commuting patterns and household composition differ substantially between countries.

Where twenty-four hours went

The 2024 Time Use Survey, released by Statistics Korea on 28 July 2025, measures how people aged 10 and over allocate the day. The result splits into three blocks.

  • Essential activity, 11 hours 32 minutes (48.1 percent) - sleep, meals, personal maintenance
  • Obligatory activity, 7 hours 20 minutes (30.6 percent) - work, study, housework, travel
  • Leisure activity, 5 hours 8 minutes (21.4 percent) - including 2 hours 43 minutes of media use and 1 hour of socialising and participation

Five hours and eight minutes of leisure sounds generous until the block is opened. More than half of it, 2 hours 43 minutes, is media use. Against 2019, five years earlier, work fell by 6 minutes, housework and travel by 4 minutes each, sleep by 8 minutes and study by 5 minutes, while media use rose by 17 minutes. The increase in media exceeds the sum of the reductions in obligatory time.

That arrangement connects back to the earlier distinction. Media use is an activity that fits into small fragments. Not much leisure fits into a fifteen-minute gap, and a screen is one of the few things that does. Leisure can grow in total and still arrive in pieces, and when it arrives in pieces the screen absorbs it. Causation cannot be read off a time-use table, but the divergence between total and composition is sitting in the table itself.

The same release carries two further items. Sleep fell for the first time since the survey began, and the share reporting difficulty falling asleep rose by 4.6 percentage points. In households with children under 18, compared with five years earlier, husbands' housework rose by 13 minutes while wives' fell by 17 minutes. Against 1999, a quarter of a century earlier, study fell by 47 minutes, work by 36 minutes and socialising by 12 minutes, while personal maintenance rose by 36 minutes, eating and snacks by 21 minutes, and sleep and sport by 17 minutes each.

The time spent getting there and back

Commuting is time with something close to zero discretion. Neither the start hour nor the duration is usually yours. Statistics Korea published the 2024 analysis of commuter travel characteristics on 20 December 2024. It is an experimental statistic built by pseudonymised linkage of the agency's population and household data with location and movement data from the three mobile carriers, referenced to June 2024.

  • Average departure time 08:10, average return time 18:18
  • Average round-trip commute 73.9 minutes (36.5 minutes out, 37.4 minutes back)
  • Average distance 17.3 kilometres
  • Average time present at the workplace 9.1 hours, rising to 9.3 hours in the capital region

Seventy-four minutes is a little over five percent of the day. The weight changes once you look at where in the day it falls. One piece sits in front of the morning block and one in front of the evening block. That is why 「slow mornings」 earns its own line in the prompting essay. The issue is not the length of the morning but where its leading edge has been nailed down.

Hours worked are themselves falling

Working hours are tracked by the Ministry of Employment and Labour through its establishment labour force survey, which covers establishments with one or more regular employees. The release of 26 February 2026 carried the annual figures for 2025.

Monthly hours per worker in 2025 came to 153.8, down 1.1 hours on the previous year, which annualises to 1,846 hours, down 13.2. The direction is downward, and it agrees with the Time Use Survey finding that work time fell by 6 minutes against five years earlier.

Read the conditions before the number

In that same establishment survey, hours per worker in December 2025 came to 163.2, up 5.4 hours on the same month a year earlier. The annual figure fell; the December figure rose. The release stated the reason plainly: the calendar contained one more working day.

That single line contains most of what there is to know about reading time statistics. Monthly hours rise and fall with how many weekdays a month happens to hold. Nobody's day got longer; there was one more of them. Strip the condition and report that working hours spiked in December, and the sentence has become false.

  • Who is counted - the Time Use Survey covers the whole population, the establishment survey covers employees of establishments. Setting the two side by side as comparable is a mistake.
  • How it was collected - the Time Use Survey is a diary the respondent fills in, while the commuting analysis is an experimental statistic built from linked carrier location data. The first absorbs perception, the second admits only people whose location data was captured.
  • What the average conceals - 73.9 minutes is a national mean. The distribution is wide across regions and occupations, and that width lives in other tables of the same release.
  • Which year applies - travel in June 2024 and annual hours in 2025 are values from different years. Putting them in one sentence requires attaching a date to each.

Attaching conditions makes sentences longer and harder to quote, which is a large part of why the conditions fall away. But the moment they come off, the number becomes an impression that nobody can check. What has to travel with a figure is not the figure itself but three things: the collecting body, the reference period and the population covered. A number carried without those three leaves no route back to the source when somebody later wants to verify it.

Five places where discretion gets cut

The measuring part ends here and the day-to-day part begins. What follows is not a prescription but a set of observations: not what to do, but what to count in order to see the shape of your own day.

Who set the time

Lay out the day's commitments and write beside each one who fixed its start: you, another person, or an institution. The commute is institutional, meetings are usually another person, a walk is you. What matters is not the ratio but the arrangement. A day with one externally-set time in the morning and one in the afternoon and a day with three of them clustered before noon have the same count and completely different remainders.

How much notice arrived

The same commitment behaves differently depending on whether you learned of it a day before, an hour before, or as it began. Counting the items that arrived with no notice gives you the size of the time spent on standby, which is a quantity that never appears in a calendar and never appears in a total.

Whether the end is fixed

A commitment with no end time holds the hours behind it as collateral. If you do not know whether a meeting is one hour or two, nothing can be placed after it, and that stays true even when it finishes in one. Time held in reserve against an unfinished thing has already been spent.

The fourth row needs care. Judging that something need not be you does not lead automatically to handing it over. Some tasks have nobody to hand to, some cost money to hand over, and some damage a relationship if handed over. The value of this column is not an invitation to delegate but a way of separating which items remain because of something only you can supply and which remain out of momentum.

What the worksheet does is not diagnosis. It is separating things that a total had lumped together. Saying there are two spare hours in the day and saying those two hours are six twenty-minute pieces describe entirely different situations, and most ways of recording a day write them down identically.

A few days of records usually produce one of three pictures: the total itself is short; the total exists but arrives as fragments; or both the total and the blocks exist, yet externally-set times are driven through the middle so the blocks cannot be used. The three have different causes and therefore different places to act. Much of the sensation that effort is not converting into results comes from the difference in when signals arrive, and holding both frames at once makes the distinction easier.

Sorting twelve items into three columns

Back to the original list. Dropping the twelve items into the three columns built earlier exposes what kind of list it is.

  • Depends on discretion - slow mornings, rest without guilt, calm and boring days, the ability to travel. All four turn on who sets the times rather than on how much time exists.
  • Depends on blocks - meaningful conversations, home-cooked meals, a good night of sleep. Each has a minimum duration below which it simply does not happen, and none of them fits in a twenty-minute fragment.
  • Sits on another axis - health, a quiet mind, people you love, people who love you back. These do not reduce to the arrangement of a day, and the instrument used here does not measure them.

Eight of the twelve fall into the first two columns. The original does not sort them that way; the original has no sorting at all, only a list. But splitting it like this makes it more specific why the sentence 「time is the real luxury」 lands so widely. What people are describing is not the length of their day. It is the shape of it.

The split is also practical in that it does not exclude people without options. Advice to buy time with money requires money to spend. Moving a notification a day earlier, writing an end time into a meeting invitation, clustering scattered commitments into the morning: none of those cost anything. Whether a person is in a position to ask for them is a separate matter, and that position is itself another name for discretion. What can be said here stops at the observation that discretion and money are not the same axis.

One last thing the word luxury does. Calling something a luxury turns it into something you could do without. Name slow mornings a luxury and they become a reward to be earned, and whoever lacks them looks for the reason in themselves. What the statistics above show is that the leading edge of the morning mostly sits outside individual choice. An 08:10 departure and a 36.5-minute commute are not values an individual selected. Swap the word for discretion and that fact stays inside the sentence.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were left out of the body because the wording could not be pinned down. The reason is given with each.

  • Continuity statistics for leisure - the Time Use Survey publishes totals by activity, but the published release does not appear to break leisure into the number of blocks it arrives in. That is why the section on blocks carries no Korean figure.
  • An official Korean indicator of working-time discretion - no regularly published statistic measuring how far workers set their own start and end times was located. Flexible-work take-up series exist, but their definition is not the same thing.
  • Sample size of the Time Use Survey - the coverage of ages 10 and over and the release date are confirmed, but the exact number of households and individuals surveyed is not.
  • Regional commute figures - the direction is reported consistently, with the capital region longest and Gangwon shortest, but the region-by-region minute values were not confirmed against the table in the release itself, so they are not in the body.
  • International comparison of annual hours - the relevant OECD indicator page could not be reached, so no international ranking is attached. The 1,846 hours in the body is a Ministry of Employment and Labour figure, measured on a different population and by a different method from OECD aggregates.
  • A large direct replication of the time-buying study - no large independent replication rerunning the 2017 PNAS design on a fresh sample was located, which is why the effect sizes appear here only as reported values.
  • Individual figures from the time-poverty paper - a large United States sample analysis is widely quoted alongside the 2020 perspective article, but that value does not appear in the abstract, so it was not carried into the body.
