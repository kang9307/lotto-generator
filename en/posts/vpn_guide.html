What a VPN Actually Protects and What It Does Not

It does not remove risk, it relocates trust - the precise scope, checked against official documentation and RFCs

2026-07-29 Tech & IT

Introduction: 「Am I safer with a VPN」 is a question that cannot be answered

The question has no answer because three things are missing from it. Safer from what, invisible to whom, and over which segment of the path. If you choose the tool before filling in those blanks, then when the coverage of the tool and the shape of your concern fail to overlap, nothing in your experience will tell you so. The connection still works, the indicator still turns green, and the mismatch stays invisible.

So this article does not answer 「which VPN is best」. It answers exactly one question instead: 「is the thing I am worried about a thing a VPN solves」. Answering that requires knowing what the protocol actually does and does not do, and that information lives in official documentation and standards documents rather than in marketing copy.

One ground rule before starting. Every technical fact in this article was read directly from project documentation, IETF RFCs, and published material from standards and oversight bodies on 29 July 2026, and anything that could not be confirmed was left out entirely rather than softened. This article also does not recommend or rank any commercial VPN service. Open source protocols and their implementations are named as facts, but naming a protocol is not an endorsement of any paid service built on it. Methods for circumventing any country's access restrictions are also out of scope.

1. What a VPN actually does - not removing risk, but relocating trust

Reduced to one sentence, the function is this. It takes the traffic leaving your device, wraps it in an encrypted tunnel to a server the provider operates, unwraps it there, and releases it onto the internet as ordinary traffic. From the destination server's point of view, that VPN server is the visitor. So the source address the destination sees changes, and the destination address an intermediate observer sees changes too.

The central angle of this article follows immediately. A VPN does not eliminate an observer. It replaces one. If you switched it on because you do not trust your access provider, then the seat that provider occupied is now occupied by the VPN operator. The information the access provider used to hold, which destinations you reached and when and how much, does not disappear; it moves. The real choice is therefore not between being observed and not being observed. It is between which party gets to observe you.

There are clearly situations where that swap is a net gain. But conditions apply.

  • When the new observer knows less about you than the old one — an access provider already holds your legal name, billing address, and line identifier. If the new observer holds substantially less, the trade has real value.
  • When the new observer has less motive and less obligation to use what it sees — this depends on which jurisdiction the provider sits in and what retention duties apply there. That is a question of law and contract, not of cryptography.
  • When the old observer is a concrete threat — not knowing who runs the café router is a different situation from dealing with a carrier you have a contract with. The threats have different shapes and different remedies.
  • When the thing you are protecting is the path — as the rest of this article shows, if your concern lives at the endpoints, in your device or your accounts, then a tool that changes the path does nothing whatsoever about it.

Conversely, if none of these conditions hold and you switch a VPN on anyway, the only thing that changed is that you now trust one more party. Where the service is free, the question of how the cost is recovered belongs in the same analysis rather than in a separate one.

Compressed into a single line, the table says 「the tunnel ends at the provider, not at the destination」. Advertising imagery draws the tunnel as a pipe running from the user all the way to the site, but in the real structure the far end of the tunnel is always the provider's server. Past that point the traffic becomes ordinary internet traffic, and protection once again depends entirely on the TLS session you negotiated with the destination.

And the last row is the one most often ignored. What the destination server knows about you is essentially unchanged by a VPN. Your signed-in state, your cookies, and the characteristics your browser volunteers do not shift because the exit address shifted.

3. What HTTPS already does and what a VPN adds on top

Skipping this distinction is the main reason a VPN gets overvalued. RFC 8446, the TLS 1.3 standard, states in its abstract that the protocol is designed to prevent eavesdropping, tampering, and message forgery, and its introduction separates three properties. Authentication, in that the server side of the channel is always authenticated while client authentication is optional. Confidentiality, in that data sent after the channel is established is only visible to the endpoints. And integrity, in that such data cannot be modified by attackers without detection.

The same document is candid about the limits. It states that 「TLS does not hide the length of the data it transmits」, adding that endpoints are able to pad TLS records in order to obscure lengths and improve protection against traffic analysis. Contents are protected, but the size and timing shape of your traffic survives.

That HTTPS has become the default rather than the exception is also a checkable fact. According to material Google published in October 2025, HTTPS accounts for 95 percent of Chrome navigations on Windows, over 99 percent on Mac, and 84 percent on Linux. Excluding navigations to private and local addresses and counting public sites alone raises Windows to 98 percent and Linux to nearly 97 percent, which means the largest remaining contributor to insecure HTTP is traffic to private sites. Google also stated that Chrome would enable 「Always Use Secure Connections」 for Enhanced Safe Browsing users in version 147, releasing in April 2026, and make it the default for everyone in version 154 in October 2026.

Against that background, the list of things a VPN adds on top is shorter than most people expect.

  • It hides the destination IP from observers on the path — every destination such an observer sees collapses into a single VPN server address.
  • It pulls plaintext DNS queries inside the tunnel — but only when the configuration is correct. Section 6 covers the specific ways that condition breaks.
  • It covers whatever traffic is not using HTTPS — older applications, some proprietary protocols, and local service discovery chatter fall into this category.
  • It bypasses filtering and policy on the local network — this is obstruction of visibility rather than protection, and inside an administrator's legitimate scope it can be a policy violation.
  • It creates a remote access path — this was in fact the original purpose of a VPN, and it remains the least ambiguous one.

What is absent from the list matters more than what is on it. Confidentiality of content is already TLS's job. A VPN wraps another layer around it, but for an HTTPS connection the additional threat that extra layer blocks is 「an observer on the path learning the destination」, not 「someone reading your password」.

4. Recalculating the public Wi-Fi story for 2026

「Connect on public Wi-Fi without a VPN and your password gets stolen」 is still a common sentence. It was true when HTTPS was a minority. Today it needs qualification, and the qualification changes the recommendation.

For someone on the same wireless network to read a password out of a login form, they have to break TLS. Because RFC 8446 mandates that the server side is always authenticated, intercepting the session requires presenting a certificate the browser trusts, and failing that the browser raises a warning. In other words, the actual failure point in this scenario is not the cryptography but the person. The habit of clicking through warnings, mistaking a captive portal for a login screen, and older applications that do not validate certificates properly are where the real risk sits.

So what does remain exposed on public Wi-Fi without a VPN? Set out as a list, it is this.

  • The domain name you connect to — even in TLS 1.3 the Server Name Indication in the ClientHello travels in the clear. The IETF Encrypted ClientHello document calls this value 「perhaps the most sensitive information left unencrypted in TLS 1.3」. RFC 9849, standardised in March 2026, specifies encrypting the ClientHello itself under a server public key, but real-world coverage depends on support at both ends.
  • DNS queries — without encrypted DNS, the names you look up are visible as they are. The same IETF document notes that the target domain may also be visible through other channels, such as plaintext client DNS queries.
  • Destination IP, connection time, and duration — this information is required for routing, so no arrangement removes it from the path entirely. All you can change is who is positioned to record it.
  • Traffic length and timing — the property RFC 8446 explicitly declines to hide. Switching on a VPN relocates these signals to the provider rather than erasing them.
  • Residual non-HTTPS traffic — as the figures above show, a large share of what remains on plain HTTP is bound for private and local addresses, and much of that never leaves the local network in the first place.

The summary is that the value of a VPN on public Wi-Fi has migrated from 「protecting passwords」 to 「concealing destinations」. If protecting a password is the goal, the habit of never dismissing a browser warning and the use of two-factor authentication both rank ahead of a VPN. If keeping your destinations away from whoever runs the access point is the goal, then a VPN does precisely that job and there is no close substitute.

5. Protocol design differences - WireGuard, OpenVPN, IKEv2

To keep a protocol comparison from sliding into a product comparison, this section holds to a single axis: 「is the cipher negotiated or fixed」. That one decision propagates outward into code size, auditability, and how a vulnerability gets remediated.

WireGuard sits at the fixed extreme. The official site lists the Noise protocol framework, Curve25519, ChaCha20, Poly1305, BLAKE2, SipHash24, and HKDF among its components, and states that it is 「meant to be easily implemented in very few lines of code, and easily auditable for security」. The protocol document explains that it uses the Noise_IK handshake and that the handshake completes in two messages, one from initiator to responder and one back, after which the initiator may send encrypted session packets. The handshake then recurs every few minutes on a time basis rather than based on the contents of prior packets, rotating keys for forward secrecy.

And the official whitepaper does not hide what that design costs. It states that the protocol 「is cryptographically opinionated」 and 「intentionally lacks cipher and protocol agility」, then names the price immediately: 「if holes are found in the underlying primitives, all endpoints will be required to update」. Removing negotiation buys simplicity at the stated price of making remediation a fleet-wide replacement.

The official 「known limitations」 page is even more direct. Restricting the list to the items most often misunderstood in practice:

  • Obfuscation is explicitly not a goal — the page states that WireGuard does not focus on obfuscation, and that obfuscation should rather happen at a layer above WireGuard.
  • TCP tunnelling is not supported — it says WireGuard explicitly does not support tunnelling over TCP, citing the classically poor network performance of tunnelling TCP over TCP. On a network where UDP is blocked it will not work unaided.
  • It is not post-quantum secure by default — the page notes that pre-shared keys can add post-quantum protection, or that a separate post-quantum handshake can be run on top.
  • Roaming carries a specific risk — an active man-in-the-middle could update endpoint addresses during roaming, though the page is careful to add that encrypted packets remain undecipherable.
  • Identity hiding has a boundary — the handshake encrypts the sender's public key using the static public key of the responder, so compromise of responder keys combined with retained traffic could reveal handshake sources, but not data contents.
  • Hardware acceleration assumptions differ — ChaCha20Poly1305 lacks widespread hardware acceleration, while remaining competitive in software on modern CPUs.

OpenVPN sits on the negotiated side. The official repository describes it as an open source VPN daemon distributed under GPL-2.0. The 2.6 manual documents that --data-ciphers restricts the ciphers allowed to be negotiated and defaults to AES-256-GCM:AES-128-GCM:CHACHA20-POLY1305, and that for servers the first cipher from that list which the client also supports is pushed to clients that support cipher negotiation. It adds the condition that cipher negotiation is enabled in client-server mode only. Transport is selectable through --proto as tcp or udp with IPv4 and IPv6 variants, and the manual distinguishes a control channel that runs a reliability layer even over UDP from a data channel forwarded without that mediation.

IKEv2 and IPsec represent negotiation written into a standard. RFC 7296 defines IKE as a component of IPsec used for performing mutual authentication and establishing and maintaining Security Associations. The negotiation structure lives in section 2.7: each proposal includes one protocol, and each protocol contains one or more transforms, each specifying a cryptographic algorithm, with the responder obliged to select a single accepted suite. Establishment costs four messages. The IKE_SA_INIT pair negotiates cryptographic algorithms, exchanges nonces, and performs a Diffie-Hellman exchange, and the IKE_AUTH pair authenticates the previous messages, exchanges identities and certificates, and establishes the first Child SA. IKE normally listens and sends on UDP port 500, though messages may also be received on UDP port 4500 in a slightly different format, and NAT traversal is covered in section 2.23.

6. Where it actually leaks - DNS, IPv6, and the kill switch

「The VPN is on」 and 「all traffic goes through the tunnel」 are not the same statement. Leaks live in the gap between them. Three cases dominate, and all three have identifiable causes rather than mysterious ones.

IPv6 leakage is old enough that the IETF devoted a document to it. RFC 7359 (August 2014, Informational) states in its abstract that the subtle way IPv6 and IPv4 coexist in typical networks, together with the lack of proper IPv6 support in popular VPN tunnel products, may inadvertently result in VPN tunnel traffic leakages. The mechanism is simple. An IPv4-only VPN client running on a dual-stack host secures IPv4 through the tunnel and ignores IPv6, so 「any packets destined to an IPv6 address will be sent in the clear using the local IPv6 router」. Worse, under the default address selection rules of RFC 6724 many host implementations prefer IPv6 addresses over IPv4, which raises rather than lowers the leak probability whenever the destination is dual-stacked. The recommendation is equally clear: if IPv6 is not supported in the VPN software, disable IPv6 support on all network interfaces, and if it is supported, ensure all IPv6 traffic routes through the tunnel.

DNS leakage comes out of operating system name resolution behaviour. Microsoft's official documentation publishes the Windows DNS client behaviour as a timetable. When two or more DNS servers are configured, the client queries them sequentially at first, but from the four second mark it queries all the servers in the list at the same time, repeating that fan-out at eight seconds. The same document notes that when more than one NIC is active with different DNS servers configured on them, the client resolution behaviour is different again. In other words, if even one resolver path outside the tunnel interface remains reachable, name queries going out that way is not an anomaly. It is documented behaviour.

The obvious rejoinder is to use encrypted DNS. That is partly right. RFC 8484 (October 2018) defines a protocol for sending DNS queries and getting responses over HTTPS, mitigating passive surveillance and query diversion together. But the same document's privacy considerations are unambiguous. The resolver operator still sees the queries. It further notes that HTTP features such as cookies, header fields, and IP addresses let servers correlate requests to user identities. The conclusion is identical to section 1. It is one more relocation of trust, not an elimination of the observer.

The kill switch is not a standardised term. With no specification behind it, behaviour differs by implementation. The underlying idea is singular: a firewall rule that blocks traffic leaving by any path other than the tunnel interface. When a tunnel drops, the operating system quietly falls back to the default route, and from that instant traffic flows as it normally would. Which is why 「we notify you if the connection drops」 and 「we prevent anything from leaving if it drops」 are entirely different products wearing the same label.

7. Is 「no logs」 a verifiable claim

Start by classifying the claim. Saying that no logs exist is a claim of absence. A claim that something exists is settled by producing one instance, but a claim that something does not exist requires exhaustive observation in principle. Since no external auditor can watch every system a provider runs at every moment, this claim is structurally resistant to complete verification.

That does not make audits worthless. It means you have to know precisely what they can say. The formal title of the AICPA guidance covering these engagements is 「reporting on an examination of controls at a service organization relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, or privacy」. The title itself identifies the subject. What is examined is the service organization's controls, not the truthfulness of a marketing sentence.

Which is why an audit report should never be read as a conclusion and should always be read as a scope document. The items to look for are these.

  • Scope — which systems and which services were included. An audit covering part of the infrastructure and an audit covering all of it are simply different documents.
  • Period or point in time — examining the design of controls on one date and examining whether they operated over a defined period support very different statements.
  • Whether the full report is public — if only a summary or a certification badge is published, neither of the two items above can be checked.
  • Everything after the audit date — an audit looks backwards. Code changes, infrastructure changes, and changes in ownership or funding after that date are not covered by that report.
  • Jurisdiction — which country's law the provider answers to, and what retention or assistance obligations follow, is a legal question that no audit resolves.
  • Technical necessity — relaying traffic requires at minimum maintaining connection state. 「Nothing is processed」 and 「nothing is retained」 are different sentences, so check whether the provider distinguishes what is processed from what is stored and for how long.

This article does not judge which providers meet that bar. That judgement belongs to the reader and their own threat model, and what this article supplies is the criteria, not the verdict. One thing can be said flatly, though. If the sentence 「we have been independently audited」 arrives without a scope, a period, and an auditor attached to it, then it is not verification information. It is advertising copy.

8. What a VPN cannot do - the gap between the ad and the coverage

VPN advertising runs on padlocks, shields, and people fading out of view. There is a measurable gap between the coverage those images imply and the coverage that exists. The widest part of the gap is in tracking.

The W3C fingerprinting guidance, published as a Group Note in September 2025, defines browser fingerprinting as 「the capability of a site to identify or re-identify a visiting user, user agent, or device via configuration settings or other observable characteristics」. Then comes the decisive sentence: 「tools such as clearing cookies or using a VPN do not prevent further correlation」. The same document concludes that complete elimination of browser fingerprinting by a determined adversary through solely technical means that are widely deployed is implausible.

The reason is structural. Fingerprinting does not run on your IP address. It runs on the combination of characteristics your browser and device volunteer, and changing the exit address leaves that combination untouched. Account-based tracking is simpler still. Signing in submits your identity directly, at which point which country your packets transited through carries no meaning at all.

Five rows say no and two say largely yes. If that ratio feels exaggerated, the feeling itself is a measure of how much the advertising imagery has shaped expectations. A VPN is a tool that operates on the path, and every 「no」 row above describes something that happens at an endpoint.

9. Enterprise VPNs and consumer VPNs are different products

They may share a protocol and they certainly share a name, but the two differ in purpose and in trust structure. Missing that distinction produces bad inferences of the form 「my employer uses a VPN, so a VPN must make me safe personally」.

  • Ownership of both tunnel ends — in the enterprise case the organisation controls both the client and the gateway. In the consumer subscription case the far end is somebody else's infrastructure.
  • Purpose — the enterprise purpose is access control over internal resources. The consumer purpose is substituting the exit address and concealing the path.
  • Opposite requirements on record keeping — the enterprise case has to record who accessed what and when, or audit is impossible. The consumer case sells the absence of records as the product.
  • Consequences of failure — an enterprise failure collapses access control over internal systems. A consumer failure adds one observer.
  • The nature of the trust — enterprise trust is backed by employment relationships and enforceable policy. Consumer trust is backed by a contract and a reputation, and nothing else.

On the enterprise side the underlying assumption is itself moving. NIST Special Publication 800-207 (August 2020) defines zero trust as 「the term for an evolving set of cybersecurity paradigms that move defenses from static, network-based perimeters to focus on users, assets, and resources」. It states that a zero trust architecture assumes no implicit trust based on physical location or device ownership, and that 「authentication and authorization (both subject and device) are discrete functions performed before a session to an enterprise resource is established」.

The NIST implementation guide SP 1800-35 describes a zero trust architecture as one that 「enables secure authorized access to enterprise resources」 distributed across on-premises and multiple cloud environments, while letting a hybrid workforce and partners reach those resources 「from anywhere, at any time, from any device」 in support of the organisation's mission.

What that migration means in practice is direct. The premise that being inside the tunnel makes you an insider is being discarded. This does not mean VPNs disappear. They remain where they are genuinely needed, but network location stops being equivalent to authorisation. The perspective transfers usefully to individuals as well: a tool that changes your location only affects location-based controls and cannot touch identity-based ones.

10. Law and terms of service - check before you switch it on

Stopping at the technical picture creates problems in real use. The items to check separate into three layers, and none of them are optional.

  • The law where you live and travel — regulation of the use, provision, and sale of VPNs varies by country, and some jurisdictions require licensing or registration or impose restrictions. This article does not cover methods of circumventing any particular country's blocking. Checking the law where you actually are is the only correct procedure.
  • Your organisation's policy — company devices and company networks carry separate rules. Installing a personal VPN on a work device can be a violation in itself, and it can obscure security monitoring in ways that make you the subject of an investigation.
  • Service terms — changing your apparent location to reach region-restricted content may be technically possible while still breaching terms of use, with account suspension or payment reversal as the consequence. Technical possibility and contractual permission are separate things.
  • Signup and payment details — subscribing with an identifiable payment method while expecting anonymity leaves identity information with the provider. This gap goes missing from threat models remarkably often.
  • Other people's traffic — some free services are structured so that user devices act as exit points for other people. Whether such a clause exists is something to read in the terms before signing up, not after.

To put it plainly, a VPN is not a legally neutral zone. Changing the path changes who observes you. It does not grant an exemption from the rules that apply to you.

Conclusion: change the question and the answer appears

「Should I use a VPN」 is a question that produces no answer. Split into three questions, it produces one every time.

  • Which observer am I actually concerned about — the café router operator, the access provider, the destination service, or the advertising network. Different targets call for different tools.
  • Does replacing that observer with a VPN provider improve my position — if the new observer knows less about me and has less reason to act on it, the trade is a gain. If not, the only change is one more party to trust.
  • Does my concern live on the path or at the endpoint — malware, phishing, fingerprinting, and account tracking are all endpoint problems, and a tool that changes the path cannot reach any of them.

A VPN is a good tool. But the reasons it is good are not the reasons the advertising gives. Building an authenticated path across a network you do not trust, and reaching a far end you control safely, are jobs with few substitutes. For anonymity, tracking prevention, and malware defence, on the other hand, tools at other layers do the job far more precisely.

One last note on the posture of this article. There is no reason to frighten anyone and no reason to reassure anyone either. What is needed is an accurate picture of what the tool covers, and that picture is written in official documentation rather than in advertising.
