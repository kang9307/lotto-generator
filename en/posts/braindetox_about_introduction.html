Why the four editions are not translations of one another, and what you take away from here

August 11, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: if a search result brought you here

Most people arrive from a search result. They are about to sign a lease, or they want to check a dose before opening the packet, or a new model shipped and they want to know what actually changed. If you followed that trail all the way to this page, you probably have one question. What is this place.

The short answer: a site that keeps written articles and browser tools in four languages. As of August 2026 there are 1,296 Korean articles and 5,184 across all four editions. There are 66 tools, and none of them ask you to sign up or install anything. The first article went up on 21 January 2025, which makes this day 566.

Article counts are a poor way to describe a site, though. Nobody gets anything out of the number 5,184. The thing worth saying first is that the four editions are not translations of each other. That is where this site parts company with the usual multilingual blog, and the reason it parts company is practical rather than aesthetic.

What this page answers — what you can take away from here, why the language editions differ in substance, how far you can trust the facts in an article, where the values you type into a tool go, and what happens when something turns out to be wrong.

1. The four editions are not translations of each other

Translation fails the moment an article rests on a legal rule. A tenant in Korea and a tenant in Japan facing the same landlord problem are standing on different statutes with different remedies. Render the Korean sentence into flawless English and the grammar survives while the usefulness does not. You end up reading an accurate description of a procedure that does not exist where you live.

So each edition gets a new anchor. The subject stays fixed, the supporting authority is swapped for the one that governs the reader, and the document is rebuilt on top of it. This is not a find and replace on article numbers. What you may demand, what the other side may lawfully refuse, and where you escalate when refused are all jurisdiction specific, which means the outline itself changes.

1.1 What moves and what stays put

  • Stays put — the problem being addressed, the situation the reader is in, the number of tables and lists, the structural density of the piece
  • Moves — the governing statute and article, the order and deadlines of the procedure, the body you complain to when it goes wrong
  • Gets dropped — mechanisms that simply do not exist in that jurisdiction. Cutting them beats forcing a false equivalence
  • Never gets added — figures about a foreign system that were not confirmed. Filling that gap plausibly turns the paragraph into a lie

1.2 Plenty of the catalogue is Korea specific, and that is stated plainly

The roots of this site are in Korea. A large share of the articles cover institutions that exist nowhere else, such as jeonse deposits, the year end tax settlement and national health insurance. In the English edition those articles stay Korean in substance. For someone working, living or signing a contract in Korea, that is the accurate thing to do, and each such article says in the body that the scheme is Korea specific.

Topics that survive a change of jurisdiction get moved instead. Data protection, consumer contracts, employment and tax all have counterparts elsewhere, so those are re-anchored rather than labelled. Which treatment a topic gets depends on the topic, and neither treatment is forced onto the other.

2. One subject, four different answers

That is abstract, so here is a real article. It covers how far you can demand what a service holds about you — access logs, IP addresses, internal identifiers.

All four jurisdictions have a mechanism for this. But having a counterpart is not the same as being the same. So the four editions rest on four different provisions.

EditionLegal anchorWhat the article actually answers
KoreanPersonal Information Protection Act, Article 35How to demand access from a Korean operator, and the grounds for refusal

Two people can walk in with the same question and, depending on where they live, need to walk out with different answers. The point of that table is the right hand column, and no amount of translation produces a right hand column. That is why the editions are written separately.

There is a cost to working this way. It is not four times the work, it is more than four times. The reason for paying it is blunt enough: an article anchored to the wrong jurisdiction is a wrong article, not a short one.

4. How facts are handled

An article is worth something or not depending on whether the numbers in it are right, not on how the sentences read. That goes double for figures where being wrong costs the reader money or health: doses, tax rates, filing deadlines. A few rules follow from that.

ItemCurrent stateWhat it means
Articles with a references section1,063 of 1,296 (82.0%)Sources for figures and provisions are listed below the body
Articles listing what could not be confirmed97 (7.5%)A recent practice, applied going forward
Language editions4, totalling 5,184 articlesRewritten per jurisdiction rather than translated

The 82 per cent is not offered as a boast. The remaining 18 per cent mixes articles with nothing to cite and articles that should have cited something and did not, and how many fall into the second group is a question that has to be counted rather than guessed. The direction, at least, is settled: state a number, show where it came from.

  • Anything that changes carries a date. Rates and thresholds get amended, so an undated figure is half a figure
  • Provisions are cited down to the article number. Naming the statute alone leaves the reader to go looking
  • The grade of the evidence is distinguished. A regulator publication, a news report and someone else's summary do not weigh the same
  • A figure that failed cross checking is left blank rather than filled. A plausible invented number is far worse than a gap
  • Products and services get judging criteria rather than verdicts. What to look at, not what to buy

5. Why the unconfirmed items get their own section

Write enough of these and you learn that some doors never open. A schedule is not published, two agencies give figures that disagree, or the primary text sits behind a paywall. At that point there are three options: fill the gap by estimate, drop it quietly, or write down that it could not be confirmed.

The third one. Estimating is out of the question, and dropping it quietly means the reader never learns the item existed and finishes the article believing it was complete. Where the confirmed territory ends is information the reader is entitled to.

This practice is recent, so it is on 97 articles rather than all of them. Calling it a property of the whole catalogue would be an overstatement. It applies to new writing, and older pieces pick it up when they are next revised.

  • Confirmed and unconfirmed material is gathered into a single section. Scattering it across paragraphs wrecks the reading line
  • Inside a table the cell is left empty with a one line footnote
  • The reason it is unconfirmed gets stated. Nothing published and nothing found are different situations

6. Medicine, law and money do not get verdicts

Those three areas run under a different rule. Being definitive there is dangerous because the reader pays a different price for a mistake. Pick the wrong software and you lose an afternoon. Get a dosing schedule or a filing deadline wrong and what you lose is not an afternoon.

So articles in those areas do not hand down a conclusion. They lay out the material a decision needs: what evidence exists and at what grade, which figures set the baseline, which conditions change the outcome, and at what point this stops being a reading problem and becomes a professional one.

  • No claims of efficacy. The type and strength of the evidence goes in, the judgement stays with the reader
  • No instructions on starting or stopping medication. That call belongs to the treating clinician
  • Nothing written that reads as investment advice. Schemes and conditions get explained and the article stops there
  • Legal pieces pin down provisions and procedure without predicting how a case comes out

7. Sixty six tools, no signup and no install

Reading only gets you so far before something has to be calculated. What actually lands in the account, how many tokens a document comes to, what one unit is in another. If the answer to that moment is install an app or create an account, most people stop there.

The 66 tools exist to remove that moment. Open the address and it is ready, with no account and no checkout. And what you type is not sent to a server. Whether it is a salary figure or a document you opened, the calculation finishes inside your browser.

  • Salary calculator — what actually reaches the account from a gross figure
  • Token counter — how many tokens a piece of text comes to
  • LLM API price calculator — what those tokens cost
  • PDF toolkit — merging and splitting inside the browser
  • HWP viewer — opening Korean word processor files without the software
  • Unit converter — length, weight, temperature, area
  • Full tool index — all 66, sorted by kind

8. Free, and paid for by advertising

Articles and tools are all free. There is no paid tier and no checkout step. The bills are covered by advertising. That sentence belongs in an introduction because knowing an ad supported site is ad supported is part of how a reader weighs what they read.

There are limits set on what the advertising may bend.

  • Body copy is not steered toward the ads. The advertising stays in the advertising slots
  • No tool is gated behind an advert. The tools work as tools
  • Korean-language articles published before July 2026 still carry an affiliate banner, each with a disclosure that a commission is earned. Articles published from 29 July 2026 onward do not carry it. Either way, no product is pushed through the prose itself

If an ad sits somewhere that gets in the way, that is a placement to fix rather than something to ask readers to tolerate.

9. When something turns out to be wrong

At this volume there are errors. And more of them are sentences that were right on the day they were published than sentences that were wrong from the start. Rules get amended, rates move, products are discontinued. Correction is therefore ordinary work rather than an exception path.

The handling looks like this.

  • It gets corrected. Not deleted but replaced with the right value, with a note of when it changed
  • The same error is searched for across the whole catalogue. Fixing one article leaves the rest standing
  • All four editions are checked together. Fixing one leaves three running out of step
  • If the correct value cannot be confirmed, the slot is emptied and marked as empty rather than swapped for a guess

For getting in touch, the notice at the foot of every page is the route. Anything reported and confirmed gets folded in.

10. Where to start

On a first visit the entry point depends on what you came for.

  • You have a question right now — the article list narrows by area, or search straight for it
  • You have something to calculate — open the tool index first. Most of them finish on one screen
  • You deal with Korea — the finance and legal areas name provisions and deadlines rather than gesturing at them
  • You would rather read another language — the language switch on each page moves to the Korean, Japanese or Chinese edition. As above, that is a separate piece written for that jurisdiction, not the same piece translated

Conclusion: reasons to come back

What this site is trying to do compresses into one line. Let someone who arrived from a search result leave with what the decision needed. Hence sources next to figures, an honest note where confirmation failed, and a tool within reach the moment arithmetic starts.

Two reasons to return, if reasons are wanted. One is that the moving values get chased and corrected. Rules and prices keep shifting, and an article that does not shift with them stays in the index while ceasing to be useful. The other is that changing language changes the content. When a question follows you into a different jurisdiction, there is an edition waiting at a parallel address with the article numbers already swapped for that jurisdiction's.

The thin spots are named above. Practical and legal coverage is light, the unconfirmed items section is on 97 articles rather than all of them, and some pieces still carry figures without sources. Those are the places currently being filled in.
