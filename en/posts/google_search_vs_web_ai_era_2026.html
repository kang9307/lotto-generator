Google vs The Open Web 2026 - How AI Overviews Are Replacing Links, and What Content Creators Must Do Now

From Links to Answers - How Google's Web Layer Shift Threatens Creators (And How to Adapt)

May 22, 2026 Tech

Introduction: The First Line of Search Has Disappeared

If you open a Google search results page in English-speaking markets in May 2026, the first thing you see is no longer a list of blue links. The entire above-the-fold area is taken up by a synthesized answer block called AI Overviews, with three or four small source cards underneath that decide the fate of every remaining click. The same change is creeping into Korean, Japanese, and other regional markets for many informational queries, and I have watched it happen on my own site (braindetox.kr) where traditional "definition" and "how-to" pages now show clear, measurable CTR collapse.

This article starts from a discussion on news.hada.io dated May 21, 2026 ("Google vs The Web"), but I do not simply translate or repeat that thread. Instead, I take the larger debate happening across English-speaking publisher circles and recast it for the people who actually need to make decisions right now: solo creators, small media operators, niche site owners, and anyone whose income depends on search traffic. I run a four-language tech blog, and what follows is the playbook I am applying to my own site.

Here is the short version. The claim that "the open web is dying" is overstated. But the more specific claim that "the ad-funded, informational-blog model is structurally finished in its current form" is, in my opinion, essentially correct. The eight strategies in this post are how creators move from that dying business model to something that can outlast AI Overviews.

1. How Google AI Overviews Actually Work

AI Overviews is not a single model. It is a pipeline. Based on Google's I/O 2024-2025 announcements, DeepMind technical posts, and Google Search Central documentation, the rough flow looks like this:

  • Step 1 - Query intent classification: Decide whether the query is informational, navigational, or transactional, and whether AI Overviews should fire at all.
  • Step 2 - Search index recall: Pull dozens of candidate pages from Google's existing search index.
  • Step 3 - LLM reading and synthesis: A Gemini-family model reads the body text of candidate pages and produces a synthesized answer.
  • Step 4 - Source selection: Three to four pages that actually contributed to the answer are surfaced as small citation cards.
  • Step 5 - Safety filtering: YMYL queries (medical, financial, legal) get extra-conservative post-processing.

The most important thing to understand here is Step 3. The model reads body text, not meta tags. The old SEO recipe of stuffing meta descriptions and title tags has lost most of its decisive power. What the body of your page literally says, how clearly it says it, and how citation-friendly it is - that is now the key variable.

2. CTR Data - How Much Traffic Has Actually Been Lost

Combining publicly available industry reports (Ahrefs, SimilarWeb, Search Engine Land, Authoritas) with my own measurements, here is the rough CTR damage by content type between late 2025 and Q1 2026. My personal site measurements fall within these ranges.

The two rows I want to highlight are the last two. Content that AI Overviews cannot cleanly summarize takes almost no damage. That single observation is the foundation of every survival strategy in this post.

3. Which Sites Are Taking the Heaviest Hit

From watching the discussion across English-speaking publisher communities (Hacker News, the SEO subreddits, Indie Hackers, micro-publisher Twitter), the sites that have lost traffic fastest share a clear pattern:

  • Aggregated Q&A sites: Short question, short answer. Trivially absorbed by AI Overviews.
  • Wiki-style reference sites: Objective, encyclopedia-flavored pages.
  • Single-topic definition blogs: "What is X?" pages built around a single keyword.
  • One- to three-step how-to guides: Procedural content with low context.
  • Listicles: "Top 10", "Best 5" pages with thin per-item depth.
  • Numeric answer pages: Prices, exchange rates, conversion results, simple calculations.

Mapping this onto my own categories, even within tech content, short "What is X?" entries have lost the most ground. Long-form series, real operational write-ups, and command-line case studies have been almost untouched. This is consistent with what I am hearing from other publishers.

4. Google's Position vs Publishers' Position

Both sides of this debate have a partial point. Here is how I read it.

4.1 Google's Official Position

  • AI Overviews improve the user experience, and source links still appear, so publishers retain opportunity.
  • Clicks from AI Overviews are "higher-quality clicks" with stronger conversion intent.
  • Synthesized answers are a natural evolution of search, and that is what users actually want.

4.2 Publishers' Position

  • Publishers wrote the source material that the model summarizes. The user takes the value, the publisher takes a citation at best, and often nothing.
  • "Higher-quality clicks" is a phrase that conveniently hides the drop in absolute click volume.
  • Ad-funded sites take direct revenue damage as page views fall.
  • Q&A, tutorial, and definition content effectively becomes free AI training data.

My honest read: both sides are partially right. UX did improve. Publisher revenue did fall. But the market balance has clearly tipped toward users (and toward Google), and the cost of that tilt is the slow erosion of the ad-funded content model.

5. The Legal and Policy Response - EU and US

This is not just publisher grumbling. There is real legal pressure building in parallel.

  • EU Digital Markets Act (DMA): Gatekeeper platforms cannot self-preference their own content. Whether AI Overviews counts as self-preferencing Google's own synthesized answer over publisher links is now an open question in Brussels.
  • EU AI Act: Training-data transparency and copyright protections. Compensation obligations for publishers whose content trained AI models are under active discussion.
  • US antitrust (DOJ v Google): After the search-monopoly ruling, the remedy phase includes some discussion of structural separation of AI Overviews.
  • National regulators in Korea, Japan, Australia, UK: Smaller markets, but the underlying principle of "no self-preferencing" applies across jurisdictions.

My prediction: within the next two to three years, some form of training-data compensation or publisher revenue-sharing obligation will be introduced, probably first in the EU. The US is likely to follow. Korea and Japan will likely be two to five years behind. Plan as if relief from regulators is real but slow, and the practical adaptation has to happen now.

6. Eight Survival Strategies for Content Creators

This is the heart of the article. These are not theoretical. They are the eight changes I have either already applied to my own site or am actively rolling out in 2026.

6.1 Go Deeper - Make Content the Model Cannot Cleanly Summarize

Long-form analysis above 1,500 words tends to lose only a fragment of its value to AI Overviews. Even if the model lifts a paragraph, the full thesis still requires reading the article. Shifting away from short definition pages toward analytical depth is the single highest-leverage move.

6.2 Use First-Person Experience

Phrases like "from running my own site for three years" or "based on metrics I measured personally" are extremely hard for language models to confidently synthesize. They are also strong E-E-A-T Experience signals. First-person voice is both an AI-resistance moat and an SEO booster.

6.3 Build Interactive Content (Tools, Calculators, Generators)

My site runs more than 100 small tools (a lottery picker, tax refund calculator, currency converter, and others). AI Overviews cannot execute a calculator. Every tool you add is a piece of your site that is structurally immune to the AI absorption layer.

6.4 Build Direct-Reach Channels (Email, Social, Discord, RSS)

If you can be reached without going through Google, you are not at Google's mercy. Newsletters, X accounts, Mastodon, Discord servers, RSS subscribers - any platform-independent channel is long-term insurance.

6.5 Multimedia (Video, Podcast)

AI Overviews can summarize text well. It does not play video. Embedding YouTube, Spotify, or self-hosted video creates traffic channels that are not absorbed into the answer block.

6.6 Strengthen Brand Search

Move from "how to use X" to "[your brand] how to use X". When users search your brand directly, AI Overviews has nothing to synthesize - they already chose you. Consistent brand presence in tool names, titles, and metadata is essential.

6.7 Reduce Google Dependency Below 60%

Aim for a traffic mix roughly like Search 50% / Direct 20% / Social 15% / Referral 15%. Anything above 70% search dependency is a single point of failure in the AI Overviews era.

6.8 Diversify Across Search Engines and Platforms

Bing, DuckDuckGo, Brave Search, Kagi, ChatGPT Search, Perplexity, and regional engines (Naver, Yandex, Baidu) all matter again. Multilingual hreflang and proper sitemaps multiply your surface area across these alternatives.

7. The New SEO - Structured Data, FAQ, Author Entity

SEO in the AI search era is less about keyword density and more about accumulating signals that make the model trust your page.

  • JSON-LD structured data: Use BlogPosting, FAQPage, HowTo, ItemList aggressively. This very post uses FAQPage schema.
  • FAQ sections: Three to five Q&A pairs in or near the article body. AI Overviews loves to lift these verbatim.
  • Strong Author Entity: Explicit author name, bio, social profile links, ideally marked up as schema.org Person. Models weight author identity as a trust signal.
  • Clean canonical URLs: Kill duplicate content. AI models use canonical to identify the "original".
  • hreflang across multiple languages: Better international reach plus a small ranking bonus when the model matches user language.
  • Freshness markers: Phrases like "as of May 2026" help the model recognize recency.

If you want the deep version of these tactics, see my earlier post Google AI Search SEO Optimization 2026 - Practical Guide.

8. The Future of the AdSense Model - Risks and Opportunities

I have run AdSense across multiple sites for years. Here is how I see the model evolving.

  • Risk 1: Informational content loses impressions, which means fewer page views and fewer ad slots.
  • Risk 2: Users satisfied by AI answers search less often in total.
  • Risk 3: Advertisers shift budget to AI-search ad inventory, putting downward pressure on traditional display CPM.
  • Opportunity 1: If AdSense embeds ads inside AI answers, publishers could share that revenue.
  • Opportunity 2: "AI-resistant content" becomes scarce, and scarcity pushes CPM up.
  • Opportunity 3: Multimedia and tool-based pages tend to carry higher per-page ad value than thin informational pages.

My conclusion is that AdSense does not vanish, but relying on AdSense alone becomes dangerously fragile. Affiliate, sponsorship, direct ad sales, premium tool tiers, paid newsletters - some combination of these is now a survival requirement.

9. A Small-Site Case Study (braindetox.kr)

This site is a solo-operated, four-language tech blog. To make this concrete, here is how each of the eight strategies above maps onto my actual operations, including the parts I am still failing at.

  • Tools and calculators: Around 100 small utilities as of May 2026. Core AI-resistant asset.
  • Four-language operation: Korean, English, Japanese, Chinese. Reduces dependence on any single market.
  • Shift to long-form analysis: From 2026 onward, fewer short definition posts and more multi-section analytical pieces (this very post is part of that policy).
  • FAQPage and BlogPosting schema: Now applied uniformly to recent posts.
  • Four-language RSS feeds: Added in May 2026, sitting outside the search-engine layer entirely.
  • Still weak: No newsletter yet, no consistent social presence, no video. These are my next priorities and I am openly behind on them.

I share the weak spots intentionally. No publisher is fully prepared, and the realistic plan is to fix one weakness per quarter rather than wait for a perfect strategy.

10. The Korean Market - Naver vs Google AI

For readers outside Korea, this section explains a structural advantage that small Korean publishers enjoy and that creators in fully Google-dominated markets do not.

  • Naver still holds 30-40% of Korean search: Naver has not deployed Google-style AI Overviews as of May 2026.
  • Daum / Kakao Search: Smaller share, but strong in specific verticals.
  • Naver Cue: and HyperCLOVA X: Korean-style AI search is rolling out, but at a slower pace than Google.
  • Korean-language content has low dependence on English-market AI answers: AI Overviews damage is softer than in English markets.

My view is that Korean creators have an extra two to three years of adaptation runway compared with their English-speaking peers. Whether they use that runway intelligently is what will decide who is still standing in 2030. The eight strategies above apply equally well, just on a delayed timeline.

11. The New Web - What 2030 Could Look Like

Here is the four-layer model of the web I expect by 2030.

  • Layer 1 - AI Answer Layer: Google, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude. About 80% of user traffic ends here without ever reaching a website.
  • Layer 2 - Verification and Depth Layer: Trusted publishers, experts, and tool sites. The sources AI Overviews actually cites.
  • Layer 3 - Community and Social Layer: Reddit, X, Discord, regional communities. Engagement, not search.
  • Layer 4 - Closed Paid Layer: Newsletters, Substack, membership sites, premium tools. Outside the AI absorption zone by design.

Today's ad-funded informational blog either evolves into a Layer-2 publisher, mutates into a Layer-3 community hub, moves to a Layer-4 paid model, or disappears. My personal recommendation is the combination of Layer 2 and Layer 3: be a cited, trusted source while also owning a direct audience that does not depend on search.

12. Conclusion and Three Things to Do This Week

If you do not have time for the entire eight-step program, here are the three actions I would prioritize this week.

  1. Audit your short definition and quick how-to pages. Identify the ones that lost the most CTR over the last six months. Rewrite the highest-traffic ones into long-form analysis, or repurpose them as tool pages.
  2. Add FAQPage schema to your top five articles. One hour of work, real ranking and citation upside.
  3. Start one direct-reach channel. Pick the easiest of newsletter, RSS, or X account, and launch it this week. The hard part is starting, not running.

The single sentence summary of running a small site through this transition is this: the less your site depends on Google, the stronger your site becomes. AI Overviews is a threat, but it is also a filter that separates good content from filler. If this post is part of what nudges your site to the better side of that filter, it has done its job.
