Adoption in Korea: A Complete Guide to Requirements, Procedure and Legal Effects

Ordinary adoption, full adoption and protected-child adoption compared, plus family court approval, surname and inheritance, and records access — based on the framework that took effect on 19 July 2025

Jul 28, 2026 Law & Legal
⚠️ Legal information notice — This article describes the adoption system of the Republic of Korea for general information only and is not legal advice. It reflects the framework that took effect on 19 July 2025; subordinate regulations and administrative guidance are still being refined. For a specific case, consult a lawyer or contact the National Center for the Rights of the Child or your local government office. In Korea, free legal counselling is available from the Korea Legal Aid Corporation at 132.

1. In July 2025 Korea moved its adoption baseline

Search for adoption in Korea and you will still find guides that begin with the phrase under the Act on Special Cases Concerning Adoption. That description is no longer accurate. Two statutes passed by the National Assembly in July 2023 took effect together on 19 July 2025, and in a single step they changed both the name of the governing law and the body that runs the procedure.

Three things changed. First, the former Act on Special Cases Concerning Adoption was renamed the Special Act on Domestic Adoption. Second, a separate Act on Intercountry Adoption was enacted to govern adoptions crossing the border in either direction, so domestic and intercountry adoption are now handled under different statutes. Third, adoption of children under public protection moved from a system centred on private agencies to a public system run by the central and local governments.

That phrase sounds abstract, but in practice it is very concrete. Applications from prospective adoptive parents are received by the National Center for the Rights of the Child. The match between a child and prospective parents is decided not by a private agency but by a subcommittee of the Ministry of Health and Welfare adoption policy committee. While a child is under protection, the mayor or the head of the local government acts as guardian and reviews the child's living situation each quarter. Adoption records that private agencies used to hold were transferred to the National Center for the Rights of the Child, and requests for disclosure of adoption information now go through that single channel.

The international dimension moved too. Korea's status as a party to the Hague Convention on Protection of Children and Co-operation in Respect of Intercountry Adoption formally took effect on 1 October 2025. In the same direction, the principle that intercountry adoption may be pursued only where no suitable adoptive parents can be found within Korea is now written into the statute (Article 7 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption).

This article sets out the requirements, procedure and legal effects of adoption in Korea on that new baseline. Adoption is not something to be urged or discouraged; what follows describes only how the system is built and what needs checking at each stage. Anyone actually moving through the process should confirm the current guidance published by the National Center for the Rights of the Child and the competent local government.

2. Korea has three separate adoption systems

Treating adoption as a single procedure guarantees confusion. What Korean law calls adoption is in fact three distinct systems resting on different statutes, and their legal effects differ from one another.

2.1 Ordinary adoption (Civil Act Article 866 and onward)

This is adoption in its broadest sense. An adult may adopt (Civil Act Article 866), and adopting a minor requires the approval of the family court (Article 867). Adopting an adult requires the consent of that person's parents (Article 871). The decisive point is the effect. An ordinary adoptee acquires the status of a child born to the adoptive parents, but the pre-adoption kinship relationship also continues (Article 882-2 (2)). The legal tie with the birth family is not severed, and the surname does not change automatically.

2.2 Full adoption, or chinyangja (Civil Act Article 908-2)

This form reconstitutes the family relationship. As a rule a couple married for at least three years must adopt jointly; where one spouse adopts the other spouse's biological child, one year of marriage suffices and the adoption may be made alone. The child must be a minor — the pre-2012 threshold of under 15 no longer applies. Birth parent consent is required, and a child aged 13 or over accepts the adoption with the consent of their legal representative, while for a child under 13 the legal representative accepts on their behalf.

The effect differs decisively from ordinary adoption. A full adoptee is treated as a child born in the marriage, and the pre-adoption kinship relationship terminates when the full adoption is confirmed (Article 908-3). Where one spouse adopted the other spouse's biological child alone, the relationship with that spouse and their relatives continues. The surname and clan origin follow the adoptive father's.

2.3 Protected-child adoption (Special Act on Domestic Adoption)

This route applies to a child without a guardian, separated from a guardian, or in a situation of abuse or neglect, whom the provincial governor or the head of the local government has determined, under the Child Welfare Act, that adoption best serves. This is generally what people picture when they say they want to apply to adopt. A child adopted under this Act holds the same status as a full adoptee under the Civil Act (Article 25).

What matters is that this third route is a public procedure. There is no place in it for taking in a child by private arrangement or through unlicensed brokerage. The match is reviewed by a subcommittee of the adoption policy committee, and the adoption takes effect only once the family court grants approval. Giving or receiving money or property in exchange for consent to adoption is a criminal offence, punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine of up to 30 million won.

3. What a prospective adoptive parent must satisfy

For protected-child adoption, Article 18 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption sets the eligibility of a person who wishes to become an adoptive parent. All of the following must be met.

3.1 What removing the age ceiling actually means

A Korean national previously had to be 25 or over and within 60 years of the child's age. The subordinate legislation effective 19 July 2025 deleted that ceiling, so a gap of more than 60 years no longer bars an application where parenting capacity is sufficient. This does not mean that an older applicant is automatically eligible. In exchange for the relaxed age rule, scrutiny of the safety of the home environment was tightened, and criminal record checks now extend to people living with the prospective parents at the same standard applied to them.

3.2 What the adoptive parent training covers

The training is not a formality; it is part of the eligibility requirement. The enforcement rules set the following curriculum.

  • Requirements, procedure and effects of adoption
  • Information on support available to adoptive families
  • Information on raising an adopted child
  • Information on the psychology and emotional needs of adopted children
  • Information on post-adoption services
  • Other matters the Minister of Health and Welfare considers necessary

On completion a certificate is issued, and that certificate is later filed with the family court petition for adoption approval. The National Center for the Rights of the Child also runs an online training channel.

4. Step one — application and training, through a single office

Since 19 July 2025 the office that receives applications for domestic adoption of a protected child is the National Center for the Rights of the Child (Article 19 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption). Applications are no longer filed individually with private agencies.

The documents the Center lists for a domestic adoption application are set out below. Agency guidance can change, so the current notice should be checked immediately before filing.

  • Adoption application form, 1 copy
  • Consent to a criminal record check, 1 copy per prospective parent
  • Consent to a search of the national child abuse information system, 1 copy
  • Certificate of completion of the introductory course, 1 copy per prospective parent
  • Certified copy of resident registration, 1 copy
  • Family relationship certificate, 1 copy per prospective parent
  • Certificate of entry and exit records, 1 copy per prospective parent

The filing method also matters. The Center states that domestic adoption applications are accepted by registered post, and that in-person, telephone and email filings are not accepted. Once an application is received, criminal record checks follow and the applicant is guided toward the basic training course.

5. Step two — eligibility screening, the home study, and the match

Once an application is filed, the process moves to screening whether the prospective parents meet the statutory criteria. That screening is carried out by an entrusted body selected by the Ministry of Health and Welfare through open tender. It covers housing, financial circumstances, physical and mental health, and parenting capacity.

5.1 How thorough the home study is

The home study is not a paper exercise. The enforcement rules require the assessor to visit and examine the home, workplace and neighbourhood at least twice, and at least one of those visits must be unannounced. The point is to see the everyday environment rather than a prepared one. This stage often takes longer than applicants expect.

5.2 The state decides the match

This is the heart of the 2025 reform. Review of a prospective parent's eligibility and the match with a child are decided not by a private agency but by a subcommittee of the Ministry of Health and Welfare adoption policy committee, applying the best interests of the child (Article 20). Once a match is made, the prospective parents receive a match confirmation document recording the names and dates of birth of both sides, which is later filed with the family court.

In other words, prospective parents do not select a particular child and bring them home. The state assesses which environment best suits the child and connects the two, a design that runs in the same direction as the best-interests principle of the Hague Convention.

6. Step three — family court approval and interim care

After the match, the prospective parents themselves petition the family court with jurisdiction over the child's address for approval of the adoption (Article 21). This step belongs to the prospective parents personally and cannot be delegated to an agency.

6.1 Documents to file with the family court

The Special Act on Domestic Adoption and the Supreme Court rules require the following attachments.

6.2 What the family court actually examines

Approval is not a formal check that the paperwork is complete. There is one test: the welfare of the child. Civil Act Article 867 (2) provides that in approving the adoption of a minor the court may withhold approval having regard to the circumstances of the child's upbringing, the motive for adoption, the parenting capacity of the adoptive parents and other circumstances, and a corresponding ground for dismissal applies to full adoption (Article 908-2 (3)). In practice the court looks at the following.

  • Whether adoption serves the child's best interests compared with other protective options
  • Whether birth parent consent was freely given and given at a lawful time
  • Whether the views of a child aged 13 or over have been ascertained
  • The economic and emotional parenting capacity, housing and health of the prospective parents
  • Whether the motive for adoption is consistent with the child's welfare
  • The findings of the home study and the content of the matching review
  • Where the child has siblings, how those relationships will be maintained

Absent special circumstances the court is to decide within six months of the petition (Article 21 (3)). That is a statutory target for processing; the time actually taken varies from case to case.

6.3 Interim care — living together before approval

The new framework introduced a mechanism called interim care (Article 22). Where the family court considers it necessary for early attachment and mutual adjustment, it may, on application or of its own motion, order that the child live with the prospective parents before the approval becomes final. The prospective parents then become interim guardians, and the existing parental authority is suspended.

An interim care order can be revoked (Article 23): where there is a problem with the manner of care, where the legal representative withdraws consent or acceptance, where a birth parent withdraws consent, or where other circumstances make continued care difficult. It is important to understand clearly that at this stage the adoption has not yet been established.

7. Birth parent consent and the reflection period

Consent is the most carefully designed part of the procedure, because the law assumes that the period immediately after birth is not one in which a birth parent can make a settled decision.

7.1 The reflection period — seven days from birth

Article 17 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption requires that consent and acceptance be given only after seven days have passed from the child's date of birth. This is commonly called the adoption reflection period. Consent given immediately after birth does not satisfy the requirement.

7.2 How consent and acceptance work

  • Birth parent consent: required as a rule. An exception applies where consent cannot be obtained, for instance where parental authority has been declared forfeited or the parent's whereabouts are unknown (Article 16).
  • The child's acceptance: a child aged 13 or over accepts with the consent of their legal representative; for a child under 13 the legal representative accepts on their behalf (Article 15).
  • Withdrawal: both consent and acceptance may be withdrawn at any time before the family court grants approval (Articles 15 (4) and 16 (3)).
  • Court override: where a birth parent has failed to perform the duty of care for three years or more and has not exercised visitation, or has abused or abandoned the child or seriously harmed the child's welfare, the court may approve the adoption without consent.
  • No consideration: consent must be freely given, and consent conditioned on money, property or other consideration is prohibited. A violation carries imprisonment of up to three years or a fine of up to 30 million won.

Consent, in short, is controlled along three axes: timing, form and the absence of consideration. If any one of them fails, a petition built on that consent is unlikely to succeed.

8. How legal status changes once the adoption is established

The adoption takes effect when the family court's granting adjudication becomes final. The adoptive parents or the adoptee then attach the court's approval document and file a full adoption registration under the Act on the Registration of Family Relationships (Article 26 (1)). Registration is less a condition of validity than the step that records a settled status in the public register.

8.1 Kinship

A child adopted under the Special Act on Domestic Adoption holds the same status as a full adoptee under the Civil Act (Article 25), so Civil Act Article 908-3 applies directly: the child is treated as born in the adoptive parents' marriage, and the pre-adoption kinship relationship terminates when the adoption is confirmed. Under ordinary adoption, by contrast, that relationship continues (Article 882-2 (2)). The same word, adoption, produces opposite results here.

8.2 Surname and clan origin

In full adoption and in adoptions carrying the same status, the child takes the adoptive father's surname and clan origin. Ordinary adoption does not change them automatically, so where a change is needed for the child's welfare it must be sought separately from the court (Civil Act Article 781 (6)).

8.3 Inheritance

Inheritance diverges as well. In full adoption and equivalent adoptions, the tie with the birth family has ended, so inheritance rights run only toward the adoptive parents. An ordinary adoptee keeps the tie with the birth family and may therefore inherit from both families; conversely, if such an adoptee dies without descendants, both the birth parents and the adoptive parents may be heirs. The general structure of statutory heirs and shares is covered separately in our guide to inheritance order and statutory shares.

8.4 Age thresholds that continue to apply

After adoption a child remains subject to statutes that turn on age. Thirteen is the threshold at which a child accepts an adoption in their own name, and the boundaries of minority, childhood and youth differ from statute to statute. That is set out in our guide to age standards for children and youth in Korea.

9. Can an adoption be dissolved? The bar is very high

The most common misconception is that an adoption can simply be undone. The answer depends entirely on which type of adoption is involved.

9.1 Dissolution of ordinary adoption

Ordinary adoption may be dissolved by agreement (Civil Act Article 898) or by judgment (Article 905). However, dissolution by agreement is not available where the adoptee is a minor or an adult under guardianship. The status of a minor cannot be reversed by the parties' agreement alone.

9.2 Dissolution of full adoption and of adoptions under the Special Act

Dissolution of a full adoption is available only by judgment and only on the two grounds set out in Civil Act Article 908-5.

  • Where the adoptive parents abuse or abandon the full adoptee or otherwise seriously harm the adoptee's welfare
  • Where conduct by the full adoptee toward the adoptive parents makes it impossible to maintain the relationship

The grounds for dissolution by agreement (Article 898) and by judgment (Article 905) that apply to ordinary adoption do not apply to full adoption (Article 908-5 (2)). Standing belongs to the adoptive parents, the full adoptee, the birth parents and the prosecutor, and exclusive jurisdiction lies with the family court for the ordinary venue of one of the adoptive parents (Family Litigation Act Article 30). Once dissolution is confirmed, the full adoptive relationship ends and the pre-adoption kinship relationship revives (Civil Act Article 908-7 (1)).

In short, a full-adoption-type relationship cannot be reversed because someone changed their mind or finds parenting hard. That is not a defect in the design but a deliberate protection of the stability of the child's legal status. Difficulty in parenting is answered through post-adoption support services rather than dissolution.

10. Requesting disclosure of adoption information

The right of an adopted person to access records about their birth and the background of their adoption is guaranteed by statute (Article 33 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption). Under the 2025 reform, records previously held by private agencies and child welfare facilities were transferred to the National Center for the Rights of the Child, and requests are now handled through that single channel.

10.1 Who may request

  • The adopted person — able to request in their own right once an adult
  • A person under 19 — the adoptive parents' consent is required

10.2 What may be requested

  • Identifying details of the birth parents (name, date of birth, address, contact)
  • Background of the adoption (their age at the time, area of residence, circumstances of consent, date of adoption)
  • The requester's own birth information (name before adoption, time and place of birth)
  • Information on the institution or facility that cared for the child before adoption

10.3 Birth parent consent and its exceptions

Identifying details of birth parents are disclosed, as a rule, only with the birth parents' consent. If they do not consent, the remaining information is disclosed without those details. Where a birth parent has died or their consent cannot be confirmed, disclosure may still be made without consent if a special ground such as a medical purpose is recognised. That exception is aimed at needs tied directly to the requester's life and health, such as confirming a hereditary condition.

11. Support for adoptive families and post-adoption follow-up

Adoption does not end with the court's decision. Article 31 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption provides for regular counselling and welfare services for one year after the adoption is established, to support mutual adjustment between the adoptive parents and the child. The period may be extended where the parents or the child request it, or where the circumstances of the child or the family are found to need longer.

Financial support rests on Article 32. The state and local governments may provide the following.

  • Childcare allowance — a regular payment toward the cost of raising an adopted child
  • Medical expenses — the patient's share of treatment, counselling, rehabilitation and therapy costs under the medical care and national health insurance schemes
  • Other childcare subsidies — costs prescribed by local government ordinance
  • Provision for children with disabilities or illness — scope and amount may be set differently

Applications go to the metropolitan or local government head, who decides within 15 days of the application. The childcare allowance is paid monthly, and payment may stop where the adoptive parents are not in fact raising the child.

A caution about amounts: the specific amounts and criteria for the childcare allowance and medical expense support vary by year and by local government, so this article does not state figures. Actual amounts must be confirmed with the child welfare division of the competent city, county or district office, or with the National Center for the Rights of the Child. Some local governments provide additional support by ordinance.

12. Eight common misconceptions

  • Applications go to a private agency. Since 19 July 2025 domestic adoption applications for protected children are received by the National Center for the Rights of the Child; the role of private agencies has been sharply reduced.
  • Full adoption requires a child under 15. That is the old rule. Civil Act Article 908-2 now requires only that the child be a minor.
  • Adoption always severs the tie with the birth family. It does under full adoption and adoptions under the Special Act, but not under ordinary adoption. It depends on the route.
  • An ordinary adoptee's surname changes automatically. It does not. Changing the surname and clan origin requires a separate adjudication (Civil Act Article 781 (6)).
  • Older applicants cannot apply. The 60-year age gap ceiling has been deleted. Anyone 25 or over who meets the criteria may apply, though applying and being found eligible are different things.
  • Consent can be given right after childbirth. It cannot. Seven days must pass from the date of birth.
  • An adoption can be cancelled later. Dissolution of a full-adoption-type relationship is available only by judgment and only on two statutory grounds such as abuse or abandonment. It cannot be reversed by agreement.
  • A child can be brought in privately from abroad. Intercountry adoption must follow the Act on Intercountry Adoption and Hague Convention procedures. Private brokerage sits outside that system and is regulated precisely to prevent the sale and abduction of children.

13. Where to direct questions

The reformed system is barely a year old, so field guidance can differ between sources. Checking in the following order tends to be most reliable.

  • Procedure, applications, training — the adoption division of the National Center for the Rights of the Child. Forms and filing methods are updated periodically, so official notices take priority.
  • Child protection and financial support — the child welfare division of the competent city, county or district office, which handles allowance and medical expense applications and any ordinance-based support.
  • Court procedure — the competent family court and the Supreme Court electronic civil petition centre, for petition forms and filing fees.
  • Statutory texts — the Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr) and Easy Law (easylaw.go.kr).
  • Free legal counselling — the Korea Legal Aid Corporation at 132, which can also advise on litigation support subject to income criteria.
  • Child abuse and emergencies — 112 for reports and 129 for the health and welfare call centre.

Where other family law matters are entangled, our guides to enforcing child support and to applying for adult guardianship may also be relevant.

14. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between ordinary adoption, full adoption and protected-child adoption in Korea?

They rest on different statutes and produce different legal effects. Ordinary adoption follows Civil Act Article 866 and onward, and the kinship tie with the birth family continues after adoption (Civil Act Article 882-2 (2)). Full adoption follows Civil Act Article 908-2; once confirmed, the pre-adoption kinship relationship terminates and the child takes the adoptive father's surname and clan origin. Protected-child adoption follows the Special Act on Domestic Adoption, and a child adopted under it holds the same status as a full adoptee under the Civil Act (Article 25 of that Act). Because these are three separate systems, the route determines both the requirements and the outcome.

What changed in Korea on 19 July 2025?

The former Act on Special Cases Concerning Adoption was renamed the Special Act on Domestic Adoption, and a separate Act on Intercountry Adoption took effect on the same day. Responsibility for adopting children under public protection moved from private agencies to a public system run by the central and local governments. The National Center for the Rights of the Child now receives applications and holds the records, while a subcommittee of the Ministry of Health and Welfare adoption policy committee decides the match between a child and prospective parents. Korea's status as a party to the Hague Adoption Convention took effect on 1 October 2025.

Is there an age limit for prospective adoptive parents in Korea?

For protected-child adoption a prospective parent must be at least 25 years old. The former ceiling, which required the age gap with the child to be no more than 60 years, was removed from the subordinate legislation that took effect on 19 July 2025, so a large age gap no longer bars an application where parenting capacity is sufficient. Meeting the age rule does not by itself establish eligibility: economic and emotional stability, criminal record and health are all assessed.

Can a birth parent in Korea consent to adoption immediately after childbirth?

No. Article 17 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption requires that consent and acceptance be given only after seven days have passed from the child's date of birth. This waiting period is often called the adoption reflection period. Consent that has already been given may still be withdrawn at any time before the family court grants approval (Article 16 (3)), and giving or receiving money or property in exchange for consent is a criminal offence.

Can an adopted person in Korea later obtain information about their birth parents?

Article 33 of the Special Act on Domestic Adoption allows an adopted person to request disclosure of adoption information from the National Center for the Rights of the Child. A person under 19 needs the adoptive parents' consent. Identifying details of birth parents are disclosed only with the birth parents' consent as a rule; if they do not consent, the remaining information is released without those details. Where a birth parent has died or their consent cannot be confirmed, disclosure may still be possible if a special ground such as a medical purpose is recognised.
