Korea School Violence Response Guide - Reporting, Committee Review, Sanctions and Appeals 2026

How Korea's school violence system works: from the 117 hotline to the district committee, victim protections, perpetrator measures, and how to appeal decisions

2026-06-17 Law

Important Notice - Legal Disclaimer

This article is for general information and educational purposes only. It is not legal advice. Korea's school violence laws (including the Act on the Prevention of and Countermeasures Against Violence in Schools) and related ministerial guidelines are subject to amendment. Procedures, time limits, and criteria described here may vary depending on the time, jurisdiction, and the specific facts of each case. For any actual school violence situation, please consult your school, district education office (Wee Center), the School Violence Reporting Center (117), the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (132), or a qualified attorney. This article covers Korea's system as of 2026 and does not guarantee any specific outcome.

1. What is School Violence in Korea?

Under Korea's Act on the Prevention of and Countermeasures Against Violence in Schools (학교폭력예방 및 대책에 관한 법률), school violence is broadly defined as acts involving injury, assault, confinement, intimidation, abduction, defamation, insult, extortion, coercion, forced labor, sexual violence, bullying (ostracism), and cyberbullying that cause physical, psychological, or property harm to students, whether on or off school grounds (Article 2, Clause 1).

This is important: school violence under Korean law is not limited to incidents that occur inside the school building. Events after school, on weekends, and in online spaces can all fall under the Act.

1.1 Common Types

  • Physical violence: Hitting, kicking, throwing objects, causing injury
  • Verbal and psychological abuse: Insults, threats, coercion, forced errands
  • Ostracism (group exclusion): Systematically ignoring or isolating a student
  • Cyberbullying: Harassment via SNS, messaging apps, online games; spreading false information or private photos
  • Sexual violence: Unwanted sexual remarks or contact (also covered by separate sexual violence laws)
  • Extortion: Taking money or property through threats or coercion

2. How to Report School Violence

2.1 Who Must Report

The Act requires anyone who witnesses school violence or learns of it to report it immediately to the school or relevant authorities (Article 20). This applies to victims, bystanders, teachers, parents, and any member of the public. Reporters are legally protected: their identity is kept confidential, and retaliation is prohibited. Violations carry penalties of up to one year in prison or a fine of up to 10 million KRW.

2.3 The First 48 Hours

Once a report is filed, the school is required to protect the victim immediately, notify parents of both parties, and report the case to the district education office within 48 hours. A dedicated school task force (led by the school's designated teacher) then conducts an initial fact-finding investigation.

Evidence checklist: Document the incident with dates, times, locations, and descriptions. Screenshot and back up any relevant messages, photos, or videos. If there are physical injuries, obtain a medical certificate from a doctor as soon as possible.

3. School-Level Resolution vs. Committee Referral

Not all cases go to the district committee. Under Article 13-2, the principal may resolve a case without a committee hearing if all of the following conditions are met:

  • The harm is minor, with no significant physical or psychological injury
  • The perpetrating student immediately apologizes and pledges to stop
  • The victim and their guardian both agree to school-level resolution

If the victim or guardian requests a committee hearing, or if the harm is serious, a committee referral is mandatory.

4. The School Violence Response Committee

4.1 Structure

The School Violence Response Committee (학교폭력대책심의위원회) operates at the district education office level. Since March 2020, the old school-level autonomous committees (자치위원회) were abolished and replaced by this centralized district body. The committee includes education officials, legal professionals, medical/psychological experts, teachers, and police representatives.

4.2 Hearing Process

Committee hearings follow an in-person principle. The victim student, perpetrator student, and both sets of guardians may attend and give direct testimony. Exceptions allow for video conferencing, phone hearings, or written submissions for remote areas or special circumstances.

The committee may hear from specialists including pediatric physicians, psychiatrists, and psychologists. When the victim requests expert opinion, it must be solicited. Hearings are closed to the public by default, though meeting minutes (with personal information redacted) may be disclosed upon request.

5. Victim Protection Measures (Article 16)

The committee may recommend the following protective measures for the victim to the district superintendent, who must implement them within 7 days with parental consent:

Absences taken for protective measures are counted as attendance. Treatment costs are primarily borne by the perpetrator's guardian. In urgent cases, the school safety mutual aid association or provincial education office may advance funds and later seek reimbursement.

Support resources: The Blue Tree Foundation (푸른나무재단), formerly the Youth Violence Prevention Foundation, provides counseling and legal support for victims and families. Phone: 0800-177-233. Website: www.btf.or.kr (Korean)

6. Perpetrator Sanctions (Article 17) - Nine Measures

The committee may impose one or more of the following nine measures on the perpetrating student, based on the severity, duration, intent, and the perpetrator's remorse:

  1. Written apology submitted to the victim
  2. Prohibition of contact, intimidation, or retaliation against the victim
  3. School community service within the school
  4. Social community service outside the school
  5. Special education or psychological treatment by specialists
  6. Suspension from school for a specified period
  7. Class transfer to a different class
  8. School transfer to a different school
  9. Expulsion (high school students only; the most severe measure)

Guardians may also be required to complete special counseling or parenting education. Measures 1-3 may be removed from the student's academic record (school life record) upon graduation; measures 4 and above are retained for specified periods under the Ministry of Education's guidelines.

7. Appealing Decisions - Administrative Channels

7.1 No More Separate Re-examination (Jaerim)

Prior to 2020, there was a separate re-examination (재심) mechanism for appealing committee decisions. That system has been abolished. The only formal avenues for challenging a decision are now administrative appeal and administrative lawsuit.

7.2 Administrative Appeal

  • Who can file: Victim, perpetrator, and their guardians
  • Where to file: Administrative appeal committee at the relevant district education office
  • Deadline: Within 90 days of receiving notice of the decision (no later than 180 days from the decision date)
  • How: Submit an administrative appeal form online (via the National Sinmungo portal) or in person at the education office

7.3 Administrative Lawsuit

  • Deadline: Within 90 days of learning of the decision (no later than 1 year from the decision date)
  • Respondent: The relevant district superintendent (교육장)
  • Court: Administrative division of the competent district court

7.4 Stay of Execution

Filing an appeal or lawsuit does not automatically suspend the committee's measures. If there is concern about immediate harm (e.g., a transfer order being carried out before appeal), a separate stay of execution (집행정지) application must be filed. When a perpetrator's measure is stayed, the victim's guardian must be consulted.

Seek legal advice before appealing: Deadlines are strict and procedures are complex. Contact the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (dial 132) or a specialist lawyer for guidance on your specific situation.

9. FAQ

Does reporting guarantee that the perpetrator will be punished?

Not automatically. A report triggers an investigation, but cases may be resolved at the school level if conditions for self-resolution are met. Even when referred to the committee, the type and severity of any measures depend on the circumstances of each case. Reporting is the starting point for officially establishing the facts and protecting the victim, not a guarantee of a particular outcome.

What if the perpetrator is under 14 (a juvenile offender in Korea)?

Children under 14 cannot be criminally prosecuted in Korea. However, school violence committee measures under the School Violence Prevention Act and juvenile protective measures under the Juvenile Act (handled by family courts) are separate proceedings and can both apply. School violence measures and criminal/juvenile justice proceedings run independently.

Can cyberbullying be reported as school violence?

Yes. Since a 2021 amendment, cyberbullying is explicitly listed as a form of school violence under the Act. Harassment via SNS, messaging apps, or online games, including insults, ostracism, spreading false information, and leaking personal data, can all qualify. Preserve evidence (screenshots, backups) before reporting.

Closing Note

Korea's school violence response system is procedure-heavy by design - it aims to balance victim protection with due process for all parties. Whether you are a victim, a parent, or a bystander, the most effective approach is to document facts carefully, follow the official channels step by step, and seek guidance from qualified professionals at every stage.

This guide covers Korea's system as of 2026, based on publicly available legal sources. For your specific situation, please consult your school, district education office Wee Center, or the 117 School Violence Reporting Center.
