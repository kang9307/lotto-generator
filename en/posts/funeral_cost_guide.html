Funeral Cost and Prepaid Plan Complete Guide - Items, Averages, Prep

The funeral process, where cost comes from, average ranges, prepaid plans, and smart preparation

2026-06-13 Life & Practical

Introduction: The Bill That Lands on Top of Grief

Losing a family member arrives without warning. And in the very middle of that grief, the bereaved are forced to make a string of heavy decisions within a few days: where to hold the wake, which supplies to use, cremation or burial, where to place the remains. What makes it harder is that every one of those decisions immediately carries a price tag. In the rush, families are sometimes steered toward expensive bundles, and large sums leave their accounts before anyone has a chance to compare unit prices line by line.

This article has one purpose: to help you understand in advance how a funeral unfolds, where and how cost arises, and roughly what range to expect, so that when the moment comes, you are not also losing money simply because you did not know. It then walks through the structure and traps of prepaid funeral plans, refunds and bankruptcy protection, realistic ways to cut costs, and a preparation checklist. The framing centers on the Korean system, but the underlying logic of process and cost applies broadly.

1. The Funeral Process at a Glance - From Death to Interment

To understand cost, you first need to see the order in which a funeral proceeds, because cost attaches to each stage. A typical Korean three-day funeral roughly follows this flow.

1.1 Death and Placement

When a person dies, a doctor issues a death certificate (or a post-mortem examination report), and the body is placed in the funeral home's mortuary. A death at a hospital often flows into that hospital's funeral hall, but this is not mandatory; the body can be transported elsewhere. This stage incurs mortuary and transport (hearse) costs.

1.2 Setting the Wake and Receiving Visitors (Days 1-2)

The wake hall is prepared and mourners are received. This is when you pay for the hall rental, the altar and floral arrangements, the portrait, mourning-attire rental, and the most variable item of all: catering for visitors. Depending on hall size and the number of mourners, the food bill often dominates the total.

1.3 Encoffining and the Funeral Procession (Days 2-3)

The deceased is dressed and placed in the coffin (encoffining), after which the wake is closed and the procession heads to the resting place. Supplies such as the shroud and coffin, plus procession vehicles (limousine, bus), are billed here.

1.4 Cremation or Burial, Then Interment

At the resting place, cremation (use of a crematorium) or burial takes place. After cremation, the remains are placed in a columbarium or given a natural burial (such as a tree burial); with burial, a grave is prepared. Crematorium fees, columbarium or natural-burial costs, or grave costs arise here.

In one line: cost arises along six branches - (1) the funeral home (facility and food), (2) supplies, (3) vehicles, (4) cremation/burial, (5) columbarium/grave, and (6) optional prepaid staff and services. Keep that structure in mind and reading a quote becomes far easier.

2. Breaking Down the Cost Items

Let us dissect where the big money leaves the quote. The figures below are general ranges cited across funeral-industry sources and differ by provider, region, and time.

2.1 Funeral-Home Facility Fees

The core is the wake-hall and mortuary fee. This varies sharply by operator: public (municipal or provincial) funeral homes tend to be cheapest, followed by private funeral homes, with university-hospital funeral homes generally highest. Within the same funeral home, the rate also changes with the floor area of the hall.

2.2 Catering for Visitors

Food served to mourners is among the most variable items in the entire bill. Because it is per-person cost multiplied by the expected number of visitors, the more mourners, the heavier the burden. How much food, and at what level, you order frequently determines the overall cost.

2.3 Funeral Supplies

This covers the shroud, coffin, encoffining materials, altar flowers, and portrait. Shrouds and coffins vary enormously in price by material and grade. Expensive bundles usually inflate their price by raising the grade of these supplies.

2.4 Vehicles (Transport)

This includes transport from the hospital or home to the funeral home, the hearse to the resting place at the procession, and a bus for the bereaved. Cost depends on distance and vehicle type.

2.5 Cremation, Columbarium, and Grave

Crematorium fees differ greatly depending on whether you are a local resident or from outside the district; locals usually pay far less. A columbarium varies widely by location and tier, with popular metropolitan facilities costing more. Choosing burial adds separate grave-preparation and maintenance costs.

3. Average Ranges and What Drives Them

"So what does it actually cost?" is the most pressing question. Taken together, industry sources put the total for a three-day funeral generally somewhere in the range of roughly 10 to 15 million KRW. But that number swings enormously.

3.1 What Tips the Scale

For the same funeral, the following choices can nearly double the total.

  • Funeral-home tier: public homes tend to charge lower facility fees than private or hospital ones.
  • Hall size and number of mourners: catering scales with headcount, so this is the biggest variable.
  • Type of service: scaling down to a family-only service lowers the total.
  • Supply grade: how far you raise the material of shroud and coffin makes a large difference.
  • Resting place: cremation plus columbarium or natural burial is generally cheaper than burial in a grave.
Key point: rather than being overwhelmed by "10 to 15 million KRW," focus on the variables you can control - funeral-home tier, catering volume, supply grade, and resting place - and the total shifts substantially. The average is just an outcome; your choices create the cost.

3.2 The Trap Hidden in Averages

An advertised "average of X" means something completely different depending on which items it includes and excludes. Some figures count only facility and supplies, leaving out food; others add the grave or columbarium. When comparing quotes, always line them up on the same basis: confirm exactly what is included in each number.

4. What a Prepaid Plan Is - The Structure of Prepayment

Many people think of a prepaid funeral plan as the way to prepare "in advance." A correct understanding of these plans is central to any cost guide.

4.1 How Prepaid Plans Work

A prepaid plan involves paying a set amount monthly in advance; when a death occurs, you receive the contracted funeral staff (such as funeral directors), supplies, vehicles, and services. Legally this falls under "prepaid installment transactions" and is governed by consumer-protection installment law. The company must register with the Fair Trade Commission as a prepaid installment business and is obliged to preserve part of the advance payments outside the company.

4.2 What a Plan Does and Does Not Cover

One common misunderstanding is that "signing up for a plan covers the whole funeral." In reality, a prepaid bundle mostly covers staff, supplies, vehicles, and the running of the service, while the funeral-home facility fee, food, crematorium fee, and columbarium or grave cost are frequently separate. So you should know in advance that meaningful additional money may be spent on site beyond the plan price.

Always confirm: a prepaid quote may be "the part the plan handles," not the "total funeral cost." The first question to ask is whether facility fees, catering, and cremation/columbarium costs are included or separate.

5. Cautions When Signing Up - Refund Rates and Bankruptcy Risk

The two biggest sources of dispute with prepaid plans are: how much you get back if you cancel midway, and what happens if the company fails.

5.1 Refund Rates - You Get Almost Nothing Early On

Refunds on cancellation follow a standard cancellation-refund schedule set by the Fair Trade Commission. The key point is that early refunds are very small. Because acquisition costs such as sales commissions are deducted first, the refund in the early installments (for example the first dozen payments) is frequently close to zero. As payments accumulate, the rate rises gradually, typically reaching around 85 percent near maturity. In other words, expecting to "break even by cancelling after paying a while" is dangerous.

The figures above are general tendencies; your actual refund depends on the specific plan and number of installments paid. Before cancelling, read the cancellation-refund table in the contract and demand an itemized calculation.

5.2 Bankruptcy Risk and Advance-Payment Preservation

If a plan company goes bankrupt, you do not lose everything. The Installment Transactions Act requires prepaid providers to preserve 50 percent of advance payments with an outside institution such as a bank deposit or mutual-aid cooperative. So on shutdown or deregistration, you can claim compensation up to that preserved 50 percent. But mind two things. First, the protection is 50 percent, not 100. Second, claim deadlines differ by institution - cooperative-preserved funds are typically claimable within three years of the triggering event. There are reported cases of people who claimed too late, after poor notice, and missed compensation.

5.3 Carry-Over Programs and Minimizing Harm

Programs that let members of a failed provider carry their payments over and continue an equivalent service with a sound company may be available. The habit of checking, before and after signing, where and how much of your money is preserved and whether the provider is properly registered - via regulator disclosures and consumer channels - reduces harm.

Five checks before signing a prepaid plan. (1) Is it a properly registered prepaid installment business (regulator disclosure)? (2) Where is the 50 percent of advance payments preserved (bank/cooperative)? (3) Is the cancellation-refund table spelled out in the contract? (4) Does the bundle include facility fees, catering, and cremation/columbarium costs, or are they separate? (5) What are the maturity and add-on cost terms? If any of these is unclear, holding off is the safer choice.

6. Direct Preparation vs a Prepaid Plan - Which Fits?

There is no single right answer between "signing up for a plan in advance" and "arranging it directly when the time comes." Weigh the pros and cons and pick what fits your family.

In practice, people often split the difference: sign up for a plan but trim the bundle to only essential items, and compare and choose the facility, food, and resting place directly when the time comes. Either way, the key is to make crystal clear at the contract stage what is included and what is separate. Settling an estate involves not only cost but inheritance and tax issues, so it helps to also review a guide on inheritance renunciation and qualified acceptance and on writing a will.

7. Realistic Ways to Cut Costs

A funeral feels like an expense you cannot trim, but a few choices change the total dramatically.

7.1 Use Public Funeral Homes and Public Facilities

Public (municipal or provincial) funeral homes tend to charge lower facility fees than private or hospital ones. Some public medical-center funeral homes also offer fee reductions. Operation and pricing differ by region, so it pays to ask in advance.

7.2 Rationalize Scale and Catering

Food, the most variable item in the total, scales with hall size and visitor count. Scaling down to a family-only service, or adjusting the per-person price and order volume realistically, yields large savings.

7.3 Choose Cremation, Columbarium, or Natural Burial

Burial (preparing a grave) is generally costly. Cremation followed by placement in a columbarium or a natural burial (such as a tree burial) can substantially reduce resting-place costs. Crematorium fees are cheaper for local residents, so check the facility for your district.

7.4 Government Support - Funeral Allowance

If a death occurs in a household receiving basic livelihood support (livelihood, medical, or housing benefit), it may apply for a funeral allowance - a program that subsidizes a set amount toward examination, transport, cremation or burial. Households receiving only education benefit are excluded, and the claim needs a death certificate, an itemized funeral statement, and the chief mourner's ID and bank account. Eligibility and amounts differ by locality and over time, so confirm in advance with your district office or the welfare portal.

Sample savings priority: (1) cremation + columbarium/natural burial -> (2) a public funeral home -> (3) rationalized food and scale -> (4) sensible supply grade -> (5) check government-support eligibility. Start with the variables you control and you can lower cost without strain.

8. Preparation Checklist

Preparing only after a funeral is upon you invites loss. Jotting down the following items in advance, even loosely, reduces both the decision burden and the cost in the moment.

  • The deceased's wishes: Have you discussed preferences on cremation/burial, resting place, and scale with the person and family?
  • Funeral-home candidates: Do you know the location and rough facility fees of nearby public and private funeral homes?
  • Resting-place plan: Have you decided a direction among columbarium, natural burial, or grave, and checked local crematorium eligibility?
  • Plan review: If signed up, have you verified registration, preservation details, the refund table, and what is included vs separate?
  • Government support: Have you checked whether the household qualifies for a funeral allowance?
  • Documents: Do you know how to obtain the death certificate and which documents (mourner's ID, bank account) claims and support require?
  • Estate matters: Do you grasp the big picture of post-death steps such as inheritance renunciation and a will?
This checklist does not hand you answers; it flags what to think about in advance. If an item makes you pause, that is exactly the point to research ahead of time.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Must a funeral be a full three-day service?

It is not required by law. Depending on family circumstances, you can scale down to a no-wake or family-only service, which sharply cuts food and facility costs. It is sensible to choose what the family can manage over a fixed format.

Q2. If a death occurs at a hospital, must I use that hospital's funeral home?

Not necessarily. You can transport the body to another funeral home. Just weigh the difference in facility fees against the added transport distance and vehicle cost.

Q3. I have paid into a plan for a long time - is cancelling a loss?

The refund rate rises as installments accumulate, but a full refund before maturity is not typical. Check the cancellation-refund table in the contract and judge it together with bankruptcy risk and your satisfaction with the service.

Q4. I heard a plan company failed - where do I check?

Regulator disclosures and consumer channels let you confirm the preservation institution and the compensation process. Cooperative-preserved funds carry a claim deadline (often three years), so it is best to act quickly.

10. Conclusion: In Grief, Information Is the Shield

A funeral forces the largest decisions, all at once, at one of life's most disorienting and sorrowful moments. Precisely because of that, knowing the structure of cost and the range of choices in advance becomes a shield for you and your family. Cost arises along six branches - (1) the funeral home, (2) food, (3) supplies, (4) vehicles, (5) cremation/burial, and (6) columbarium/grave - and many of them are variables you control.

Three closing suggestions. First, ask "what is included and what is separate" before anything else. Whether prepaid or direct, that question stops overspending. Second, with prepaid plans, verify the terms (refund, preservation, registration) before signing. Early cancellation returns almost nothing, and preservation is 50 percent. Third, learn the savings options - public facilities, cremation/columbarium, government support - in advance. To repeat: this article is for information and education only, and average costs, refund rates, and terms vary by time and provider. Before any contract, read the terms and the regulator disclosures directly.
