Why Public Documentation Sites Cannot Simply Block DDoS - The Line Between Legitimate Traffic and Attacks 2026

Separating signals where blocking equals service outage, the toll every defense extracts, and what records must remain when the flood recedes

Sep 12, 2026 Dev & Infra

When millions of HTTP requests slam into origin servers every single minute, conventional web application operators face a relatively straightforward set of architectural choices. They can dramatically dial up firewall sensitivity, enforce mandatory browser JavaScript challenges or CAPTCHAs across all inbound connections, or wholesale drop traffic originating from suspicious geographic zones and autonomous systems. Even if a fraction of prospective human customers abandon their carts or turn away at the doorway, defending existing authenticated sessions and checkout pipelines remains undeniably profitable.

However, for public documentation hosting platforms underpinning open-source software libraries and the broader global engineering ecosystem, this traditional operational playbook falls completely apart. In this unique domain, heavy-handed defensive blocking is functionally indistinguishable from a catastrophic total outage. Continuous integration (CI) build pipelines compiling critical software, developer terminals querying function signatures, language package managers resolving dependency documentation, and legitimate community mirroring tools operate entirely as anonymous, non-browser HTTP clients.

The massive distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) incident endured by Read the Docs across ten harrowing days in mid-to-late June 2026 exposes this engineering dilemma with exceptional clarity. This analysis is not a celebratory retrospective on repelling an attack. Instead, it systematically deconstructs 「why public documentation infrastructure cannot simply block heavy traffic, and why drawing the precise boundary between hostile requests and legitimate automated load is the central engineering challenge」.

The metrics, operational timelines, and architectural findings cited throughout this article derive strictly from the technical post-mortem published on September 8, 2026, by Read the Docs operations engineer David Fischer. This analysis aims to illuminate causality and defensive trade-offs rather than evaluate commercial entities. Physical bandwidth figures (such as total gigabits per second) and layer-4 packet volumes not documented in the original post are explicitly treated as absent from public disclosure.

The trigger - 5.5 million requests per minute, 100 times normal traffic

The offensive campaign initiated in mid-June 2026 and persisted for nearly ten days without substantial pause. At the absolute apex of the flood, Read the Docs witnessed an assault surpassing 5.5 million requests per minute across its globally distributed edge perimeter. To contextualize this scale, normal daily peak traffic across the entire cluster hovers comfortably under 100,000 requests per minute. The offensive flood represented a sustained one-hundred-fold surge, eclipsing any prior incident in the platform's history by more than an order of magnitude.

Observed metrics and the boundaries of the primary record

The technical retrospective published by the operations team provides meticulous observational parameters. It is essential to delineate precisely what was measured and what remains unrecorded.

  • Peak request volume: Surpassed 5.5 million requests per minute, representing layer-7 application-level HTTP/HTTPS requests arriving at edge points of presence worldwide.
  • Baseline multiplication factor: Exceeded 100 times standard operational peak volume, completely saturating upstream reverse proxy tiers.
  • Uncached requests reaching the origin: During peak cache-evasion phases, requests bypassing caching and hitting backend application workers reached 45,000 requests per minute, which set off the team internal alerting.
  • Network and origin dispersion: Coordinated traffic emerged from millions of unique IP addresses scattered across hundreds of Autonomous System Numbers (ASNs), encompassing both enterprise hosting providers and widespread residential ISP blocks.

Crucially, the primary engineering disclosure contains zero figures regarding raw layer-3/layer-4 bandwidth consumption (Gbps) or packet rates (pps). This conspicuous omission reinforces that the assault was meticulously architected not as a brute pipe-saturating flood, but as an application-layer resource exhaustion attack intended to starve web server event loops and application worker pools of CPU and memory.

How this flood diverged from historical scraping and DDoS patterns

Historically, aggressive scrapers and unsophisticated DDoS attempts exhibited pronounced geographical or infrastructural concentration. Traffic typically originated from a narrow cluster of hosting providers, a handful of specific country subnets, or identifiable static User-Agent strings. Such elementary attacks could be swiftly neutralized using basic rate limits, coarse CIDR blocklists, or regional Geo-IP fencing.

In stark contrast, the June 2026 incident manifested simultaneously across every geographic region on Earth. Traditional Geo-blocking was rendered entirely obsolete on arrival. Furthermore, the botnet continuously rotated through vast residential proxy pools, dynamically randomized HTTP header permutations, and systematically scrambled Transport Layer Security (TLS) handshake parameters. These tactics completely dismantled the signature-based JA3 and JA4 fingerprint filters that edge firewalls routinely rely upon to flag automated tools.

The fundamental architectural asymmetry of public documentation

Applying defensive architectures designed for e-commerce, banking, or SaaS portals to public documentation hosting is a catastrophic architectural error. Public technical documentation operates under fundamentally divergent behavioral dynamics and operational imperatives.

The constraint of zero authentication and universal accessibility

Open-source technical documentation exists as a global public good. Software developers, academic researchers, and automated toolchains must be able to read, reference, and query documentation without logging in, creating accounts, or accepting session cookies. Imposing an authentication wall on a public documentation site violates the core tenets of open-source distribution and immediately degrades its utility to zero.

Because there are no user sessions, backend servers possess no cryptographically signed session tokens or persistent user identities. At the HTTP transport layer, every single inbound transaction is entirely anonymous. The infrastructure cannot natively correlate whether a sequence of incoming HTTP GET requests represents a human developer sequentially reading a tutorial or a distributed botnet crawling endpoints across a dozen residential proxy nodes.

Legitimate traffic is intrinsically automated and bot-dominated

Compounding this challenge is the fact that human users navigating via desktop browsers often constitute merely a fraction of total legitimate traffic. The overwhelming bulk of valid daily requests originates from essential programmatic automation:

  • Continuous Integration (CI/CD) pipelines: Cloud runners executing GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, and corporate build servers regularly pull live documentation, download tarballs, and verify API schemas as part of automated unit and integration testing.
  • Integrated Development Environments (IDEs): Modern language servers and editors (such as VS Code, PyCharm, and Neovim) continuously poll external documentation APIs to render contextual tooltips, autocompletion stubs, and type definitions in real time.
  • Search engine and package index indexers: Legitimate web crawlers and ecosystem metadata aggregators index docstrings and changelogs round-the-clock to maintain search discovery.
  • Offline documentation tools: Popular developer utilities like Dash, Zeal, and DevDocs synchronize complete documentation bundles via rapid, high-concurrency mirroring routines.

None of these vital ecosystem participants run a full headless browser. They do not execute arbitrary client-side JavaScript, nor do they solve interactive puzzles. They communicate via raw HTTP libraries such as curl, Python requests, Go net/http, or Rust reqwest, frequently issuing hundreds of rapid-fire queries. In short, legitimate developer automation exhibits traffic signatures virtually indistinguishable from aggressive scraping bots. The simplistic operational axiom that 「excessive volume implies malicious intent」 is fundamentally broken.

Why blocking equals service outage

This operational reality engenders an acute asymmetry. On an enterprise e-commerce storefront, over-filtering suspicious traffic carries minor consequences compared to preventing credit card fraud. But on a public documentation platform, aggressive firewall heuristics that inadvertently drop automated requests instantly break build pipelines and software deployments across the globe.

If automated build scripts receive HTTP 403 Forbidden or 503 Service Unavailable responses, enterprise deployment pipelines halt, developers are locked out of reference materials, and the documentation platform fails in its primary mission. In this environment, poorly calibrated defense directly causes the exact service failure that the attackers sought to provoke. Defense becomes a razor-edge discipline of surgically shedding malicious load while keeping developer toolchains fully operational.

Signals that reliably separate traffic versus signals that fail

Faced with a globally distributed volumetric assault, infrastructure engineers must ruthlessly categorize observable network telemetry into signals that possess genuine discriminant power versus signals that merely generate disastrous false positives. Misplaced trust in fragile indicators invariably leads to self-inflicted operational wounds.

What truly works for traffic discrimination

While isolated packet headers can be trivially fabricated by attackers, the systemic structural strain that an assault exerts upon backend resources produces unmistakable statistical anomalies. The operational defense at Read the Docs demonstrated that high-fidelity discrimination relies on behavioral and outcome distributions rather than static identity strings.

  • Endpoint HTTP response code distribution (200 OK vs anomalous codes): Legitimate human readers and compliant scrapers primarily query valid, existing URLs, resulting almost exclusively in HTTP 200 responses. Conversely, attack traffic probing for cache bypasses concentrates heavily on generated non-existent paths (HTTP 404) or specific un-cached redirects (HTTP 302).
  • Abnormal frequency of computationally expensive endpoints: Disproportionate spikes directed at search query endpoints, dynamic version switchers, or complex backend routing logic, as opposed to static asset paths.
  • Correlation of Bot Management probability scores with request velocity: Blending edge machine-learning heuristic scores with per-IP and per-subnet velocity thresholds. Clients exhibiting abysmal bot scores while concurrently executing high-frequency queries represent near-certain malicious activity.
  • Verification state and cryptographic edge challenge tokens: Determining whether a connecting client has previously solved an edge challenge and maintains a cryptographically verified cookie, distinguishing full browser sessions from ephemeral scripts.

What ruthlessly cuts down legitimate users

Conversely, several legacy firewall criteria frequently touted in generic security tutorials prove completely ineffective against modern botnets, while reliably punishing genuine developers.

  • Static client IP addresses: The ubiquitous availability of commercial residential proxy networks enables attackers to route every request through distinct residential and mobile internet connections. Banning an IP permanently punishes innocent broadband subscribers or corporate NAT pools long after the botnet has migrated.
  • The User-Agent header field: Per RFC 9110, User-Agent is a fully client-controlled metadata string. Attackers trivially rotate realistic Chrome or Safari strings, whereas legitimate CLI utilities, custom internal enterprise tooling, and curl scripts transmit raw, unfamiliar strings. As explored in Is an Empty User-Agent Proof of a Bot?, header inspection alone cannot determine intent.
  • Geographic origin (Geo-IP): Global open-source contributors and distributed developer teams reside across every continent. Geographic blanket bans sever vital international developer communities from essential technical knowledge.
  • Static TLS client fingerprints (JA3/JA4): Although TLS Client Hello parameter hashing successfully identifies static automated libraries, attackers dynamically randomize cipher suites and extension permutations, causing fingerprint hashes to scatter infinitely across the map.
  • Transport protocol versions: Presuming that automated scripts rely on HTTP/1.1 while browsers use modern protocols is thoroughly antiquated; the June 2026 assault operated entirely across native HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 connections.

How attackers deliberately evade caching and how edge absorption works

Sophisticated adversaries orchestrating volumetric layer-7 attacks are acutely aware of Content Delivery Networks. Fetching fully cached static HTML or CSS assets costs the origin server nothing, as CDN edge servers terminate and serve those requests in single-digit milliseconds. To inflict maximal infrastructure harm, attackers deliberately seek out endpoints that force cache misses, funneling millions of unbuffered queries directly into the origin backend.

Targeting un-cached 302 redirects and 404 endpoints

Throughout the Read the Docs offensive, the adversary ignored static documentation pages. Instead, they concentrated intense fire on two specific categories: non-existent URLs generating HTTP 404 responses and temporary HTTP 302 redirects.

This architectural vulnerability stems directly from standard web framework defaults and RFC specifications:

  • Default CDN caching omissions: By default, standard reverse proxy and CDN configurations cache HTTP 200 OK responses aggressively, while passing temporary redirects (302) and client errors (404) directly to the origin to avoid caching transient states.
  • Heavy computational cost of error generation: Rendering a 404 page is rarely a lightweight filesystem miss. Web frameworks typically traverse full routing trees, evaluate regex patterns, render dynamic templates, and execute database queries to suggest alternative paths.
  • Dynamic cache-busting URLs: Attackers appended unique pseudo-random query strings or path suffixes (e.g., /docs/project/nonexistent-uuid-98741) to every request. Each query produced a unique cache key, rendering traditional URL-based edge caching completely ineffective.

Initially, attackers uncovered un-cached temporary redirects on several hosted domains that were resolved dynamically by the backend Python (Django) application rather than the frontend web server. Within minutes, thousands of concurrent Python worker threads locked up under computational saturation, tripping pager alerts for the operations team.

When Nginx regex rewrites buckle under load

The engineering team's initial instinct was to offload the vulnerable redirect rules to the Nginx reverse proxy tier using hardcoded rewrite regular expression directives. Under standard operating conditions, Nginx's asynchronous event-driven architecture handles tens of thousands of regex evaluations per second with negligible latency.

However, under an onslaught scaling past five million requests per minute, even a horizontally scaling cluster of Nginx instances began dropping packets. The rate of incoming TCP handshakes and TLS renegotiations outpaced auto-scaling spin-up times, saturating kernel socket backlogs and network interface buffers. Resolving the crisis within origin infrastructure boundaries proved technically impossible.

Approximately thirty minutes into the alert, the engineering team executed a decisive architectural pivot: they migrated all redirect logic away from origin servers entirely and deployed it directly onto the Cloudflare CDN edge tier via Edge Rules. By terminating the requests at edge nodes situated geographically proximate to the attackers, origin infrastructure was completely decoupled from redirect processing, instantly restoring backend CPU stability.

The immense power of micro-caching abnormal responses

When the adversary pivoted to bombarding the platform with randomized 404 paths, the decisive countermeasure was the enforcement of aggressive micro-caching on non-200 responses.

Historically, system architects hesitated to cache error pages out of fear that temporary hiccups might persist. However, an ephemeral cache window set via explicit Cache-Control headers keeps repeated requests on the same paths from reaching the origin at all. The source states only that caching was applied to 404 and 302 responses; it gives neither the cache lifetime nor how much of the flood this absorbed. The first hostile probe generates a 404 from the origin, but the subsequent thousands of queries targeting that specific path are absorbed entirely in edge memory.

# Example Nginx / CDN upstream configuration enforcing micro-caching for error responses
location / {
    proxy_pass http://backend_upstream;
    
    # Aggressively cache standard 200 responses while enforcing brief TTLs on errors & redirects
    proxy_cache_valid 200 1d;
    proxy_cache_valid 302 5m;
    proxy_cache_valid 404 1m;
    
    # Collapse concurrent edge cache misses into a single origin query to prevent dogpiling
    proxy_cache_use_stale error timeout updating http_500 http_502 http_503 http_504;
    proxy_cache_lock on;
}

Coupled with edge cache locking (which ensures that only a single upstream query is dispatched to origin while concurrent identical cache misses await its response), micro-caching transformed fragile application endpoints into resilient static buffers. Shrinking the un-cached surface area remains the primary prerequisite for surviving volumetric application attacks.

The exact toll extracted by every defensive mechanism

In infrastructure security, no defensive intervention is cost-free. Every defensive layer exacts a quantifiable toll—whether measured in latency overhead, infrastructure expenditures, or developer friction. Enforcing security policies without calculating their exact operational cost inevitably results in self-sabotage.

Why 「Under Attack Mode」 was strictly avoided

Major commercial CDN providers feature emergency toggle switches—such as Cloudflare's well-known 「Under Attack Mode」—that instantly challenge every inbound connection with a mandatory JavaScript execution sandbox. This blunt instrument undeniably extinguishes naive botnets in seconds.

Yet throughout the ten-day ordeal, Read the Docs deliberately refused to activate this global switch. As documented in Who Pays the Invoice for Crawler Traffic?, defensive friction falls squarely upon genuine visitors. Activating indiscriminate challenges would have wrought catastrophic damage:

  • Disruption for hundreds of thousands of human readers: Software engineers seeking immediate documentation syntax in the middle of active debugging would face multi-second interstitial loading screens on every page transition.
  • Annihilation of programmatic developer automation: Command-line utilities (curl, wget), automated build runners, and IDE plugins cannot evaluate arbitrary JavaScript sandboxes. Every automated build worldwide would instantly fail with 403 Forbidden or 503 Service Unavailable errors.
  • Degradation of search indexing: Major search crawlers unable or unwilling to process interactive challenge pages would rapidly drop documentation pages from search indexes.

Targeted rate limiting and penalty box mechanisms

In lieu of global barriers, the operations team constructed a sophisticated defense combining targeted velocity thresholds with automated Penalty Box isolation. Instead of bludgeoning all inbound traffic, this framework surgically isolates pathological behavioral patterns.

The system dynamically correlated Cloudflare's Bot Management probability scores with IP-specific query velocities. Connecting clients displaying high bot probability while exceeding conservative request thresholds faced immediate friction, whereas authentic browser profiles enjoyed generous headroom.

Crucially, clients generating excessive volumes of computationally expensive responses (HTTP 404 or 302) were automatically quarantined into a Penalty Box. Once quarantined, rate limits tightened across the client's associated ASN and TLS fingerprint cluster. This dynamic containment mechanism absorbed rapidly mutating attack vectors without requiring manual intervention.

The non-negotiable principle of the user escape hatch

Central to this defensive philosophy was the unwavering commitment to guaranteeing legitimate users an escape hatch. The infrastructure adamantly avoided issuing permanent, unconditional IP drops (403 Bans). Even the most stringent defensive response escalated only to an interactive JavaScript challenge.

When an authentic developer tripped a sensitive threshold and solved the edge challenge, the CDN issued an authenticated, cryptographically signed verification cookie. The edge was instructed to make subsequent challenges unlikely for that client for roughly the next day. This critical escape hatch empowered the team to aggressively tighten heuristic filtering while ensuring that legitimate human developers remained completely unhindered.

The Yo-Yo attack pattern and the asymmetric cost of auto-scaling

Perhaps the most technically sophisticated dimension of the June 2026 assault was the adversary's adaptive evasion tactics. Modern volumetric threats no longer operate as static bludgeons; they dynamically probe, measure, and exploit the algorithmic feedback loops governing cloud infrastructure.

Probing thresholds and retreating in a Yo-Yo rhythm

Whenever defensive engineers deployed new rate-limiting rules, the attacking botnet rapidly adapted its behavioral parameters. The botnet systematically varied its request cadence to empirically discover the exact velocity thresholds governing the WAF rules. Once an active rate-limiting window triggered, the adversary throttled back traffic, waiting out the cooldown timer before unleashing another massive burst.

This oscillatory wave dynamic is known in distributed systems engineering as a Yo-Yo attack pattern. Rather than hammering a brick wall, the botnet rotated across hundreds of distinct ASNs and residential proxy clusters, keeping individual node signatures quiet while maintaining persistent cluster-level volatility.

The severe financial asymmetry of public infrastructure

The true target of a Yo-Yo attack is not merely service availability, but the economic elasticity of cloud auto-scaling. When traffic spikes a hundred-fold, cloud infrastructure automatically provisions dozens of additional compute instances and database read replicas. As the attack temporarily recedes, auto-scaling cooldown timers prevent immediate scale-down, incurring massive operational overhead:

  • Complete decoupling of traffic from revenue: Commercial SaaS platforms generate revenue proportional to user activity. Public open-source documentation platforms, by contrast, operate as free community utilities. A hundred-fold spike in traffic yields zero incremental revenue.
  • Defense costs represent pure deadweight financial loss: The ballooning bills for cloud virtual machines, CDN egress overages, and WAF evaluation tiers represent permanent financial drain on open-source organizations.
  • Severe engineering opportunity cost: While small infrastructure teams spend ten straight days mitigating shifting attack vectors, planned feature development, security audits, and platform maintenance grind to an absolute standstill.

Adversaries rent compromised botnets and residential proxies for nominal fees, forcing defenders to shoulder thousands of dollars in cloud infrastructure overhead. The only sustainable defense against this economic asymmetry is to guarantee that every malicious request is neutralized entirely at the CDN edge before triggering an auto-scaling event.

What Infrastructure as Code (IaC) provided to the defensive perimeter

Navigating ten days of relentless, shifting volumetric attacks requires rapid, error-free rule deployments. Read the Docs successfully maintained cluster stability throughout the incident primarily because their entire edge defensive posture was managed via Infrastructure as Code (IaC) using Terraform.

Version control and peer validation under extreme pressure

The most dangerous trap in live incident response is making frantic, manual configuration edits inside cloud provider web consoles. Under intense operational stress, an engineer rushing to mitigate an attack can easily introduce a malformed regex pattern or an overly broad CIDR mask, inadvertently knocking down all legitimate global traffic in a self-inflicted outage. Furthermore, concurrent uncoordinated edits by multiple engineers render root-cause tracing impossible.

By enforcing that every single WAF rule, rate-limiting parameter, and edge redirect was codified in Terraform and stored in Git, the team unlocked invaluable operational capabilities:

  • Mandatory peer code review: Even amid high-severity incidents, newly proposed WAF rules and regex patterns underwent immediate pull-request validation by fellow engineers before production merge.
  • Predictive execution plans (Terraform Plan): Engineers previewed the exact blast radius of proposed modifications across edge points of presence prior to execution.
  • Near-instantaneous automated rollbacks: If a newly deployed heuristic inadvertently impacted legitimate CI/CD build runners, reverting the Git commit restored the known-good edge state in less than sixty seconds.

Sustainable lifecycle management for dozens of edge rules

Read the Docs manages thousands of custom domains and hundreds of thousands of active subdomains. When combined with granular rulesets spanning ASN classifications, bot score tiers, and request paths, the defensive configuration quickly expands into dozens of interdependent rules.

Maintaining such operational complexity via manual web dashboards is unworkable. Declarative Terraform configurations allowed the team to abstract rules into reusable modules, deploy uniform security baselines across global edge nodes, and maintain auditability under battle conditions.

What telemetry and baselines must be permanently retained post-incident

When an intense operational storm finally recedes, exhausted engineers understandably yearn to step away. Yet the immediate aftermath of an attack represents the critical golden hour for permanently hardening infrastructure. What data must be preserved, and what should be discarded?

Metric architectures that empower future mitigation

Many organizations futilely archive millions of offensive IP addresses in sprawling databases. This is a total waste of operational effort. Residential proxy IPs are recycled within hours; yesterday's attacker IP is tomorrow's innocent home broadband user.

What must be permanently captured is an accurate model of peacetime traffic baselines and exposed architectural bottlenecks:

  • Peacetime HTTP status code ratios: Operators must document exact baseline distributions for 200, 301/302, and 404 responses during calm periods. Establishing this precise benchmark allows automated anomaly detection systems to trigger defensive penalty boxes the instant error rates deviate by even 0.1%.
  • Comprehensive audit of non-cacheable endpoints: Every single dynamic path that leaked origin requests during the incident (such as search handlers, version selectors, or dynamic redirects) must be mapped and slated for edge caching or serverless isolation.
  • Baseline profiles for major cloud provider ASNs: Documenting standard traffic volumes originating from legitimate developer platforms (AWS, Azure, GCP, GitHub Actions) ensures that future rate-limiting thresholds do not inadvertently strangle developer tooling.
# Post-incident log inspection commands to evaluate cache miss ratios and response distributions
# 1) Aggregate distribution of HTTP response status codes (comparing 200 vs 302 and 404)
awk '{print $9}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# 2) Identify the top 10 request paths triggering HTTP 404 errors during the incident window
awk '($9 ~ /404/) {print $7}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -n 10

# 3) Profile client IP frequency specifically generating abnormal (404 or 302) response codes
awk '($9 ~ /404|302/) {print $1}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -n 10

Iterating operational baselines rather than static blocklists

The paramount post-incident responsibility is safely de-escalating temporary emergency rules. Overly restrictive WAF rules left running in perpetuity silently reject novel developer tools and external integrations months down the road.

Teams must conduct rigorous false-positive reviews against incident logs, identifying which legitimate automation scripts encountered temporary challenges and recalibrating velocity thresholds accordingly. As Read the Docs observed in their retrospective, residual background noise from compromised subnets continues indefinitely. With AI-assisted tools and proxy networks lowering operational costs for attackers, large-scale distributed attacks are no longer rare black-swan events. They are the permanent ambient noise of maintaining public internet infrastructure. Defensive perimeters do not conclude; they evolve alongside the baseline reality of legitimate traffic.

What could not be verified

Due to the specific scope of the published engineering retrospective and the operational boundaries of disclosed infrastructure telemetry, the following items could not be independently confirmed:

  • Physical layer-3/layer-4 bandwidth volume (Gbps) and packet rates (pps): The primary disclosure explicitly specified layer-7 HTTP request rates (5.5 million requests per minute) but omitted raw layer-4 throughput metrics, which were presumably absorbed upstream by CDN infrastructure.
  • Attacker identity, geopolitical origin, and underlying motives: The retrospective made zero claims regarding threat actor attribution, ransom demands, or ideological motives, adhering strictly to technical observation and infrastructure mitigation.
  • Exact financial cloud and CDN expenditure incurred during the incident: The specific monetary invoice resulting from origin auto-scaling volatility and 45,000 uncached requests per minute was not publicly disclosed.
  • Precise statistical false-positive rate across legitimate CI/CD runners: Comprehensive numerical figures detailing the exact percentage of legitimate developer pipelines temporarily impeded by targeted edge challenges were not detailed in the retrospective.
