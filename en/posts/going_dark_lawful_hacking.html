The Going Dark Debate - Access Moved From the Wire to the Device

What actually changed once encryption became the default, and which side pays for what

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the warrant is valid, the phone still will not open

A court issues a warrant, the handset is seized, and it stays locked. A provider is ordered to produce the contents of a conversation and hands over ciphertext, because that is all it has. Those two scenes are the starting point of more than a decade of policy argument about digital investigations. Clearing every legal hurdle and still being unable to read the material is what the phrase Going Dark names.

Whether that phrase describes the whole picture has been contested for over twenty years. Looking at the same period, one side says things that used to be visible have gone dark; the other says more is visible now than ever before. Both sides argue from facts. So the first useful step is to work out where the two claims actually diverge.

This article does not rule on which side is right. It puts the reasoning each side relies on, and the price each position exacts if it prevails, on the same scale. From there it follows where access actually moved, what new problems that move created, and which controls are being proposed to keep the result tolerable.

One thing to state up front. Nothing here describes how to bypass a lock or evade a defense. No tool names, no settings, no procedures. The subject is structure and balance, not execution. This is also not legal advice, and it does not assess any specific investigation past or pending. Statutes are cited at the level of provisions only, and concrete situations require professional counsel.

1. What Going Dark actually points at

The moment the phrase gained force is easy to date. In 2014 a major handset maker switched device storage to encryption by default and announced it could no longer unlock the devices itself, which closed a route that had previously existed. Around the same time, messaging services began turning on end-to-end encryption as a default. Where the provider holds no plaintext in the middle, an order to produce plaintext has nothing to act on.

A distinction matters here. What investigators object to is not the absence of authority but the nullification of it. The complaint is not that warrants are refused; it is that granted warrants cannot be executed as a technical matter. Miss that distinction and the debate slides into a different question, namely whether surveillance should be permitted at all. The actual dispute is about how to realize access that the law already allows.

  • Data in transit — with end-to-end encryption, traffic recovered mid-path is ciphertext. The flow is still observable; the content is not.
  • Data at rest — a locked device stays closed even after it is lawfully seized. Seizure and reading have come apart.
  • Provider-held data — in designs where the provider holds no key, the object of a production order simply does not exist. The order is valid and there is nothing to comply with.

2. The counter-argument: there is more data, not less

A body of academic work has read the same period in exactly the opposite direction. The privacy scholar Peter Swire described these years as a golden age of surveillance, and in February 2016 Harvard's Berkman Klein Center addressed the question head-on in the report Don't Panic: Making Progress on the Going Dark Debate. Its conclusion was that new consumer technologies will keep supplying governments with a wealth of data about individual movements and activities. Encryption does dim some communications, but other areas are becoming more illuminated, with connected devices opening fresh vectors.

Four concrete axes carry that argument. What makes it bite is that there is always a layer where encryption does not apply.

  • Metadata is generally not encrypted. Who contacted whom, when, for how long, and from which cell remains available independently of content. Relationship graphs and movement patterns can be reconstructed substantially without ever reading a message.
  • Backups follow different rules. A conversation can be end-to-end encrypted while a copy of it sits in cloud storage under different terms, and that copy becomes the practical point of access.
  • Opening the endpoint bypasses the encryption. End-to-end encryption protects the path, not the plaintext already rendered on the recipient screen. In front of an unlocked device the defense is simply not in play.
  • There are more recorders now. Vehicles, appliances and wearables keep logs that did not exist a decade ago. When one route closes another opens.

The two claims do not refute each other. Content got darker and context got brighter. The debate runs in parallel lines because each side uses the word data for a different layer. The table below sets out what each position relies on and what it accepts as the cost.

IssueThose seeking broader accessThose opposing broader access
Factual premiseCases exist where a valid warrant cannot be executedTotal accessible data grew over the same period
Core valueResponding to serious crime and protecting victims is a basic state dutyLimits on fundamental rights are justified only at minimum intrusion
Technical judgmentProcedural controls can make exceptional access manageableAn exceptional access path cannot tell an authorized holder from an intruder
Accepted costA lower security floor for every user of the systemSome cases where evidence is permanently unobtainable
Main rebuttal facedThe cost of failing against crime is shared across societyAccess demanded before less intrusive means are exhausted struggles on proportionality

The row worth pausing on is the accepted cost. Both sides pay something. Any account in which only one side bears a cost has misplaced the debate, in whichever direction. The European legislative argument over message scanning is where this tension is currently being fought in statutory text, and that axis is covered separately in EU Chat Control - Message Scanning Versus End-to-End Encryption.

3. Why exceptional access does not hold up, at the level of principle

When a demand for broader access is made concrete, it is usually phrased as a path only authorized agencies can use. That sounds reasonable. The difficulty is that cryptosystems cannot make that distinction.

Cryptography does not look at identity; it looks at keys. Present the right key and it opens, present the wrong one and it does not. The system has no way to tell whether the hand holding that key belongs to an officer with a court order or to a third party who took the capability. So the phrase about a key only good people can use works as a policy sentence but does not translate into an implementation spec. What remains is an operational question about where the key is kept and who can retrieve it, and that operational question is the risk itself.

In September 2015 the paper Keys Under Doormats, published in Oxford University Press's Journal of Cybersecurity, set this out in the form most widely cited since. Written by fifteen researchers in cryptography and security, it addressed not whether the policy was right but what the technical consequences would be.

  • Complexity rises. Building a surveillance ecosystem to accommodate exceptional access substantially increases system complexity and widens the room for operator error. That complex systems are hard to secure is a long-standing lesson of security engineering.
  • Concentrated targets appear. An additional access pathway becomes a point where attack value is compressed. Breaking it once reaches enormous numbers of users, which makes the return on effort disproportionate.
  • Forward secrecy is rolled back. Modern protocols are designed so that a compromised key does not expose past conversations. A requirement that past material remain openable later reverses that design directly.
  • Flaws become unpredictable. In an environment of millions of apps and globally connected services, adding new requirements raises the likelihood of unanticipated and hard-to-detect security defects.
  • There is more than one jurisdiction. If one state requires an access path, others will demand the same on the same rationale. How to design for many jurisdictions each holding their own key has no settled answer.

A decade on, none of those findings has been refuted. But there is a caution to observe. The argument does not by itself carry through to the conclusion that access demands are illegitimate. What the paper establishes is that exceptional access has a price, and that the price is paid collectively by all users. Whether to pay it is decided by societies, not by cryptography.

4. Where access moved: from the wire to the device

What actually happened over the past decade was not backdoor legislation. While the argument stayed deadlocked, investigative practice found another route: instead of breaking encrypted communications, it moved the point of access to the place encryption does not cover, which is the device itself.

The logic is simple. End-to-end encryption protects the segment between A and B. On A's screen and on B's screen the content is plaintext. So there is no need to solve the wire problem if reaching one endpoint yields the same result. That is the basic idea behind what is commonly called lawful hacking.

Three branches follow, and they differ in legal character and in the depth of intrusion.

  • Analysis of a seized device — take physical custody, then extract the data. A lock stops this branch at the same wall, but the seizure itself sits inside the traditional search-and-seizure frame.
  • Provider-held copies — target the copies that live outside the handset, such as backups. The procedure resembles an ordinary production order, but what is actually recovered varies enormously with what the subject stored and where.
  • Remote access — reach the device remotely to obtain its contents. Making that work requires getting past the device defenses, which brings with it the problem of holding undisclosed flaws. How the commercial surveillance-tool industry is structured is covered in Spyware Aimed at the European Parliament - What the Commercial Industry Asks Us.

The third branch became the center of gravity for an obvious reason. Unlike the first two, it only works if the target device is insecure. Investigative capability then depends on software defects, and at that moment the state has to choose between fixing a flaw and leaving it in place.

5. The problems that came with the move

Avoiding backdoors did not make the problem go away. The move created three new problems of a different character, and the control frameworks for these are comparatively less mature.

5.1 Fix the flaw or keep it

Remote access presupposes an unpatched defect. That defect does not sit only on the subject's handset; it sits on every device running the same software. If the state tells the vendor, everyone becomes safer and one investigative route closes. If it does not, the route stays open while somebody else may independently find and abuse the same flaw.

  • Balancing disclosure against retention — the United States documented this decision structure in the Vulnerabilities Equities Policy and Process charter released on November 15, 2017. The principle is that government-discovered vulnerabilities are disclosed unless there is a demonstrable law enforcement or intelligence reason to retain them.
  • It is not one agency's call — the charter builds in participation from departments including Commerce, Defense, Energy and Homeland Security alongside intelligence bodies, so that a single agency's investigative interest does not become the outcome by itself.
  • Retention carries a clock — vulnerabilities kept back are subject to annual review. Without that, retention becomes permanent and the process loses its meaning.

How faithfully the process runs in practice, and what share of findings is retained, is hard to establish from public material. Still, there is broad agreement that writing the decision criteria down at all is the minimum form for handling the question.

5.2 Proportionality: these do not belong on the same scale

A wiretap and device access are not the same measure under different names. The magnitude of intrusion differs.

  • Different time axis. Interception observes traffic during the authorized window. Device access reaches everything already accumulated. One is forward-looking observation; the other is recovery of the past.
  • Different scope. A modern personal device holds location history, contacts, photos, financial and health records alongside messages. A measure aimed at particular conversations effectively touches an entire life.
  • Third parties come along. Records belonging to correspondents, family and colleagues are already inside the subject's device. How to treat material about people not named in the warrant is a live question.

Which is why the real issue is whether controls attached to interception transfer intact. If nobody settles what a duration limit means for historical data, or how target specificity works when the object is an entire device, the controls survive in form and drain of substance.

5.3 Evidence integrity: there is no read-only search

Remote access carries one more awkward property for criminal procedure. There is no way in principle to reach a device without changing its state. Access succeeded means something ran on that device, and running leaves traces.

  • Alteration becomes contestable. Whether the recovered data was there originally, or was created or modified during the access, has to be demonstrated after the fact.
  • Verification gets sealed. The more the access method is withheld, the harder it becomes to exercise the verification a defense needs. If the method is not disclosed, the party challenging integrity has nothing to work from.
  • The chain gets longer. Where capability is procured externally, a third party enters the acquisition path and the stretch over which continuity must be proven grows.

This reads as a privacy issue but it lands squarely on the right to defend oneself and the credibility of the trial. When the method of access changes, evidence law has to follow, and that adjustment is still in progress.

6. Controls: what has to be in place to prevent abuse

Neither banning access outright nor allowing it without limit is a live option. Where the discussion actually converges is on which controls make that access bearable. The table sets out what each control is meant to prevent and where it weakens.

ControlRisk it addressesWhere it weakens
Prior judicial authorizationAccess decided by the executive aloneWhen the court is not given enough technical detail to review meaningfully
Specificity of target and scopeTargeted measures sliding into bulk collectionOnce an entire device is the object, specificity yields little
Duration limits and extension ceilingsTemporary measures becoming permanentDuration does not map cleanly onto recovery of historical data
After-the-fact noticeSubjects never learning they were affectedWhen deferral grounds are broad or the clock starts ambiguously
Independent oversight and published statisticsNobody knowing the scale of the regimeCoarse aggregation makes trends unreadable
Vulnerability handling processIndefinite retention of flaws for investigative gainCompliance is difficult to verify from outside
Evidence handling rulesIntegrity disputes and constrained defense rightsAn undisclosed access method makes verification impossible

A pattern repeats across the table. Failures come less from missing controls than from controls that were never translated for the new mode of access. Lay a wiretap-era safeguard over device access unchanged and the shape holds while the substance leaks out.

  • Make judicial review real — the court needs enough information about what is accessed and by what method for review to function as review.
  • Test less intrusive means first — a subsidiarity requirement, allowing the measure only where other methods cannot achieve the aim, already exists in most regimes. The open question is who verifies that judgment and how.
  • Rules for unrelated material — there must be a settled answer on separating third-party and case-irrelevant material and on when it is destroyed.
  • Publish the scale — annual counts and categories can be published without exposing any individual case. Without visible scale, the balancing debate becomes impressionistic.

7. Where the controls live in statute, with one worked example

Comparing provisions across jurisdictions is harder than it looks, because interception law and search-and-seizure law developed separately in each system. Rather than sketch several regimes thinly, here is one concrete statutory frame, drawn from the Republic of Korea, where the controls listed above appear as enacted text.

ElementProvisionSource
Prior authorizationCommunication-restricting measures for criminal investigation are executed under court permissionProtection of Communications Secrets Act, art. 6
DurationMay not exceed two months and must end as soon as the purpose is achievedArt. 6(7)
ExtensionWhere the grounds persist, an extension within two months may be requestedArt. 6(7) proviso
Aggregate ceilingTotal extensions may not exceed one year, or three years for enumerated offenses such as insurrectionArt. 6(8)
NoticeExecution must be notified within 30 days of the decision to prosecute or not to prosecuteArt. 9-2

What stands out is that this frame is built around communications in transit. Duration caps and a duty to terminate on completion suit a measure that catches a flow. Analysis of a seized device belongs to a different axis, governed by search-and-seizure provisions in criminal procedure.

  • The seam between the two axes is the contested part. Law governing the secrecy of communications and law governing the seizure of objects each matured on its own, and device access straddles both. Which set of requirements applies is argued continuously in scholarship and practice.
  • In the United States the documented piece is the flaw side, not the access side. The 2017 vulnerabilities charter records how disclosure and retention are weighed; it is not an authorization framework for investigative access, and this article does not attempt to summarize US interception statutes.
  • In the European Union the argument currently runs through message scanning. That is where the tension between detection duties and end-to-end encryption is being written into legislative text, and it is treated in the linked article rather than restated here.

To restate: this section describes the existence and wording of provisions, not a legal conclusion. Whether any particular measure was lawful depends on the facts and needs professional advice.

8. The premise that could shift and reopen everything

The past decade of deadlock rested on an implicit settlement. No legislature mandated backdoors, and practice met its needs through device access instead. That settlement leans on the premise that reachable defects keep existing.

In August 2026 the cryptographer Matthew Green published a post on his own blog that struck at exactly that premise. He argued that the spread of AI-assisted vulnerability discovery could make software too secure, and that if the workaround route which has kept the argument quiet narrows, demands for exceptional access will return to the front. He also noted the paradox that mandated backdoors would weaken the defenses of a country's own software and open a path for outside attackers.

That is a forecast, not an observation. The discussion under the original post pushed back. The most pointed objection is that, given how fast companies are shipping new functionality with AI assistance, the premise of defects running out is itself doubtful. If offense and defense are amplified by the same tooling, the direction of the net effect is not settled in advance.

  • What is solid — the reason the argument stayed quiet is the existence of a workaround, not agreement on principle. The record of the past decade shows that much.
  • What is not — whether that route actually narrows, and if so how quickly. No verifiable figures on this were available.
  • What to settle now — designing controls after the premise shifts is too late. It is cheaper to agree on what to demand before the argument reopens.

9. What this means for an ordinary user

Policy argument feels remote, but the conclusions land directly on individual choices. What helps is not a product recommendation but an eye for where your data sits and under which rules.

  • Storage location, not the app, sets the protection level. A conversation may be end-to-end encrypted while a copy of it lives somewhere with different terms, and that copy is the effective access point. Ask how many places the data exists and under what conditions before asking whether a service is safe.
  • Metadata is usually handled separately. Content protection and connection-record protection are different questions. Many regimes apply different thresholds to each, and a relationship graph emerges without any content at all.
  • With the device at the center, maintenance carries weight. Where access has moved to the endpoint, ordinary upkeep such as staying current is itself the defense. That is a matter of fundamentals, not of special technique.
  • Read regimes on two axes. Look not only at what is permitted but at how notice and oversight are designed. Permission without notice or oversight is effectively unbounded.

If the mechanics of encryption itself are the gap, Network Security From Basics to Practice Part 5: VPN and Encrypted Communication covers the fundamentals. The wider question of how surveillance capability expands inside democratic systems is treated in Digital Surveillance and Democracy - Privacy Under the Banner of Security.

Conclusion: no answer, but the questions are fixed

This argument lacks a clean resolution not because the participants are foolish but because two hard-to-reconcile demands are both legitimate. The demand that serious crime can be answered and the demand that rights be limited only minimally are neither of them disposable. Technology does not dissolve the tension either. Cryptography cannot read political preference, and an exceptional path is not designed to stay usable only by whoever built it.

So the practical question is not whether to permit access. It is under what conditions, reviewed by whom, and with what after-the-fact confirmation. If the price of avoiding backdoors was taking on vulnerability retention, proportionality and evidence integrity, then building controls for those three as carefully as the wiretap era built its own is the work that remains.

One thing is clear. When the mode of access changes and the controls do not, the form persists and the substance drains away. From either position that is a bad outcome: for those seeking wider access it erodes legitimacy, and for those seeking narrower access it leaves oversight as paperwork.

What could not be verified

The following could not be established in a form worth citing. Rather than fill the gaps with estimates, they are left open.

  • Volume statistics — annual counts of interception measures by jurisdiction, counts of device-access measures, and anything resembling a decryption success rate could not be confirmed. Definitions vary sharply by jurisdiction and counting rule, so nothing was cited without a source.
  • How the vulnerability process actually runs — the documented decision structure is public, but data showing retention rates or review compliance was not available.
  • Which measure remote access legally constitutes — how access that gets past device defenses is classified within an existing statutory scheme cannot be settled from provision text alone, so no claim was made.
  • Support for the defect-exhaustion forecast — the projection discussed in section 8 is one researcher's prediction, and no quantitative material confirming or refuting it was found.
