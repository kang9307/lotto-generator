Start From Frequency Ratios and Music Theory Becomes Rules

The octave at 2:1, the fifth at 3:2, and why the semitone ended up as the twelfth root of two - once you see the compromise, the rest turns into rules

Aug 23, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: the concepts are not the hard part, the names are

Plenty of people who read code fluently say they cannot read music. Ask them questions one at a time, though, and they rarely fail on the concept. What stops them is the vocabulary.

Take the term perfect fifth. Nothing in that name tells you what perfect means, what the five is counting, or why a distance is being called a number at all. An augmented fourth and a diminished fifth are the same sound on a keyboard and carry different names.

These names were never designed in one sitting. Writing pitches with letters goes back to the sixth century, staff notation that pins a pitch to a line settled in the eleventh, and the tuning in use today became standard long after that. Decisions from different eras sit stacked on top of each other, so of course the terminology is inconsistent. Think of an API that has kept backward compatibility twenty times over.

The trouble is that most introductions teach the names first. Reverse the order and the whole thing moves faster. There is exactly one thing at the bottom, the frequency ratio, and everything else follows from it as a rule. This article walks down that path: ratios, then temperament, scales, chords, notation, rhythm. At the end I mark where the approach stops working. Music is not mathematics, and leaving that out would make this explanation half an explanation.

The floor: pitch is repetitions per second

Sound is a change in air pressure. When that change repeats regularly, people hear it as a sound with pitch. How many times it repeats per second is the frequency, measured in hertz.

You only need to fix one reference point. The international standard ISO 16 sets the A in the treble stave, A4, at 440Hz. It was adopted in 1955, reaffirmed in the 1975 edition, and calls for tuning accurate to within 0.5Hz. There is no physical necessity in the number itself. It is an agreement. Before the standard existed, orchestral reference pitches differed from city to city.

With one reference and one rule, every other frequency is computed. MIDI assigns each note an integer from 0 to 127 and puts A4 at 69.

f(n) = 440 * 2 ** ((n - 69) / 12)

f(60) = 261.63 Hz   // middle C, C4
f(0)  =   8.18 Hz
f(127) = 12543.85 Hz

Two important things are already sitting in that short formula.

  • Pitch is exponential, not linear. Go up an octave and the frequency doubles rather than gaining a fixed amount. One step in a low octave and one step in a high octave are completely different sizes measured in hertz
  • A twelve is embedded in the exponent. Where that twelve comes from is the middle chunk of this article. It is a result of arithmetic, not a convention someone picked

We talk about adding and subtracting intervals, but what actually happens is multiplication of ratios. Take a logarithm and it becomes addition, which is why the cent turns up later as a unit.

Harmonics are what make consonance

Pluck a single string and you do not get one frequency. Along with the fundamental f, integer multiples such as 2f, 3f, 4f and 5f sound at the same time. That is the harmonic series. Which harmonics come out and how strongly is what creates timbre, and it is why the same A4 sounds different on a violin and a clarinet. What happens between those harmonics when two notes sound together is what consonance actually is.

Why 2:1 sounds like the same note

At a 2:1 ratio the upper note's harmonic set becomes a subset of the lower note's. The harmonics of 220Hz are 220, 440, 660 and 880; those of 440Hz are 440, 880 and 1320. Every upper component already exists below. Nothing new collides at all.

So an octave reads less like a different note and more like the same note at another height. The habit of using only seven letter names and repeating them in every octave comes from here. The repetition is not laziness, it reflects a physical fact.

The more complex the ratio, the rougher it gets

At 3:2, the perfect fifth, the overlap is smaller than an octave's but still substantial. The harmonics of 220Hz and 330Hz meet at 660Hz and again at 1320Hz. As you move to 4:3, 5:4 and 6:5 the meeting points thin out and instead you get more components that sit close together without coinciding.

Two components sitting close together produce beating. When that spacing falls inside the ear's critical band, it sounds rough. The study Plomp and Levelt published in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America in 1965 pinned this relationship down experimentally. Two points carry the argument.

  • Dissonance peaks at roughly a quarter of the critical bandwidth. Closer than that or further away, the roughness decreases
  • The critical bandwidth stays near 100Hz across the 100 to 1000Hz range, then widens in proportion to frequency above it. That is why chords voiced tightly in the low register turn muddy

So the summary is this. Simple integer ratios sound agreeable not because the numbers are pretty but because the harmonics either coincide or stand far enough apart that no rough beating appears. This is physics and auditory physiology, not arithmetic aesthetics. The explanation has a boundary too, which I come back to later.

Equal temperament is a compromise

If you take one thing from this article, take this section. Why you can play the same piece in any key, and what was given up for it, is decided here.

Stack twelve fifths and you do not land on an octave

Trust 3:2 and nothing else. Stack perfect fifths from any note and after twelve steps you arrive near seven octaves above where you started. Near.

(3/2) ** 12 = 129.746...
2 ** 7      = 128

129.746 / 128 = 531441 / 524288 = 1.013643...
= about 23.46 cents

That is roughly a quarter of a semitone. The leftover has a name, the Pythagorean comma.

This is not an arithmetic slip, it is a property that cannot be fixed. Two and three are different primes. No pair of natural number exponents makes a power of three equal a power of two. However many fifths you stack, you will never land exactly on an octave. Only approximations exist.

So what was given up

There are two options. Keep the pure ratios and give up transposition, or bend the ratios slightly and gain it. The historical tunings that lasted chose the first. They rang beautifully in certain keys and produced intervals you could hardly bear in distant ones.

Twelve-tone equal temperament chose the second. Keep only the octave exact at 2:1 and cut the space between into twelve pieces of equal ratio. One piece is a semitone and its ratio is the twelfth root of two, about 1.059463. Why a root rather than a division? Because pitch is exponential. Twelve multiplications have to land on exactly 2.

Now, starting from any note, going up seven semitones always yields a fifth of the same ratio. Transposition became a matter of shifting an array index. Keyboards no longer had to be retuned per key, and much later the integer representation in MIDI was built on top of the same decision.

Measuring the error in cents

A cent is a logarithmic unit for intervals. An octave is 1200 cents and an equal-tempered semitone is exactly 100. Alexander J. Ellis introduced it in the nineteenth century. It turns multiplication of ratios into addition, which makes comparing errors easy.

What the table shows is that the error is not spread evenly. The fifth is off by barely two cents, which is effectively inaudible. The thirds are off by 14 to 16 cents, far more. The slight tension you hear in a piano major triad compared with a just triad is not your imagination.

Which is why instruments with continuously adjustable pitch, and the human voice, have no reason to hold to equal temperament. When a choir or a string quartet pulls a chord toward just intonation moment by moment, it is taking back what equal temperament surrendered in the thirds.

Why twelve specifically

If you are going to divide the octave, how many pieces? The criterion is clear: how close a combination of steps can get to the just perfect fifth at 701.955 cents. Increase the number of divisions and look for the nearest value each time, and this is the picture.

  • 5 divisions: off by 18.0 cents / 7 divisions: 16.2 cents / 10 divisions: 18.0 cents
  • 12 divisions: 1.96 cents
  • 17 divisions: 3.9 cents / 19 divisions: 7.2 cents / 22 divisions: 7.1 cents, all worse than twelve
  • 24 divisions: 1.96 cents. Being a multiple of twelve, its fifth error is identical. Cutting the steps twice as fine does nothing for the fifth

No division below twelve gets the fifth within 16 cents. At twelve it abruptly drops under two. Twelve is not a mystical number, it is nearly the only workable answer among small ones. Understand why there are twelve and everything left is an array problem.

A scale is an array of intervals

Western scales use seven of the twelve notes rather than all of them. How you choose is what a scale is, and the choice is written as a single array.

major        = [2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1]   // semitones, sums to 12
natural minor = [2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2]

That one array resolves three things at once.

  • A key is a starting point. Start the same array on C and you get C major, start it on G and you get G major. Same array, different offset
  • The key signature is a computed result. That G major contains F sharp is not a fact to memorize. Walk 2-2-1-2-2-2-1 from G and the sixth degree has to be F sharp for the last step to come out as a semitone. The sharps accumulating one at a time as you move up by fifths is the same computation repeating
  • Modes are rotations. Rotate the major array five places to the left and you get 2-1-2-2-1-2-2, the natural minor exactly. That is why C major and A minor use the same notes. Same cyclic array, different entry point

Memorizing seven mode names and rotating one array are completely different tasks. The first is recall, the second is rotate(scale, n). The remaining question is why this array specifically, and the previous section answers it. Choosing seven notes this way maximizes the number of perfect fifths and fourths available inside the set. Grab any seven of the twelve and it does not work out that way.

Chords are intervals stacked on each other

A triad takes three notes from the scale array, skipping one each time. As indices, [0, 2, 4]. Written from the root in semitones it comes out like this.

  • Major triad [0, 4, 7] - a major third with a minor third on top. Roughly 4:5:6 in frequency
  • Minor triad [0, 3, 7] - a minor third with a major third on top. Roughly 10:12:15
  • Diminished triad [0, 3, 6] - two minor thirds
  • Augmented triad [0, 4, 8] - two major thirds

That the difference between major and minor is one note in the middle, moved by one semitone, is startling the first time you see it. The entire bright-versus-dark impression comes from a single semitone.

Stack one more and you get a seventh chord. [0, 4, 7, 11] is a major seventh, [0, 4, 7, 10] a dominant seventh. Inside the dominant seventh, the third and the seventh are six semitones apart, a tritone. In equal temperament that interval's ratio is exactly √2. It is the only interval that splits the octave precisely in half, and by the harmonic logic above it has the fewest coinciding components. So it sounds unstable, and that instability asks to be resolved. A large part of what we call harmonic progression is building and releasing that tension.

Fix a key and seven chords come with it. Start from each scale degree and stack the same way, and writing the results in Roman numerals gives I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°. Uppercase is major, lowercase minor, ° diminished. It falls out of the array automatically rather than being a list to memorize. Chord symbols are compressed notation in the same way. Cmaj7 is simply an instruction to stack [0, 4, 7, 11] with C as the root.

The notation is confusing for historical reasons

Notation is not a designed format, it is a format that has been patched for a very long time. Every point that looks strange has a story, and knowing the story usually settles it.

  • Why seven letter names (A to G). Writing pitches with Latin letters goes back to Boethius in the early sixth century. Names were attached to a seven-note system before the twelve-note system was organized, and the other five were bolted on later as sharps and flats of existing names. Seven is not natural, it just arrived first
  • Why a staff. In the eleventh century Guido of Arezzo established the practice of drawing lines to fix pitch. Earlier notation only sketched whether a melody rose or fell. The do-re-mi syllables also come from him, taken from the first syllable of each line of a Latin hymn, where the first was originally ut rather than do
  • Why vertical position is not linear. Moving up one step on the staff is sometimes a semitone and sometimes a whole tone, because the staff draws the seven-note system, not the twelve-note one. Semitone information is layered on separately through key signatures and accidentals. The data lives in two layers, and this is where beginners get stuck most often
  • Why the flat sign looks like a b. Because it is one. In medieval practice the note B was sometimes sung a semitone lower, and that version was called the round b (b molle) for its softness. That round b became ♭. The unlowered B was the square b (b durum), and ♮ and ♯ branched off from it
  • Why F sharp and G flat sound alike but are named differently. They are enharmonic. In equal temperament they are the same frequency; in just tunings they were not. The names inherited a distinction from the era when the tunings diverged. The distinction survives because function differs: whether a note is heading up or down is carried in its name
  • Why key signatures and repeat marks exist. A key signature declares the sharp once at the front instead of writing it every time an F appears. The repeat mark is compression from the era of hand copying, when paper and copying time were both expensive

Treat notation as a storage format optimized for an old medium and most of it explains itself. It evolved to be readable by people, sparing with paper and easy to copy by hand. It was never once shaped to be easy for a machine to parse.

Rhythm is a different axis

If pitch is the frequency axis, rhythm is the time axis. The two are independent and the rules are entirely different. The structure is actually simpler on the rhythm side.

  • Note durations divide by powers of two. Whole, half, quarter, eighth. Binary subdivision
  • A dot means 1.5 times. A dotted quarter is 1.5 quarters, two dots make 1.75. A geometric series translated into notation
  • Triplets are the escape hatch from binary subdivision. Dividing by three inside a system that only halves required its own notation
  • A time signature declares the subdivision rule. Six-eight has six eighth notes, but you feel it as two groups of three. How you group the same six is what separates genre feel
  • Syncopation is defying the expectation of the strong beat. There has to be an agreement about where the strong beat is before defying it means anything

That last item matters. Syncopation is not defined by durations alone. It is an effect that only works if a metrical grid already exists in the listener's head. That grid is not written on the page, it lives in convention. This is where explaining by structure starts to run out of road.

Where the analogy stops

If you finish here and conclude that music was really mathematics all along, you have it backwards. Mathematics explains the properties of the material and nothing more. Music is about what gets done with the material. Four places where the approach stops.

One. Preference for consonance is not universal

Explaining consonance through harmonic overlap is physically solid. It does not follow that everyone prefers consonance. A study published in Nature in 2016 reported that the Tsimane of the Amazon, who have almost no exposure to Western music, rated consonant and dissonant chords as about equally pleasant. In the same study, Bolivian city dwellers and US listeners clearly preferred consonance. The authors wrote that consonance preference appears to develop through exposure to particular kinds of polyphonic music.

Two. Equal temperament is not a world standard

Indonesian gamelan's slendro (five notes) and pelog (seven) are not equal divisions and there is no standard tuning. Each set is tuned to itself, and two villages will have audibly different pitches for the same scale degree. On top of that, paired instruments are deliberately tuned a few cents apart to produce beating, and that shimmer is considered beautiful. It is precisely the phenomenon I called the source of roughness earlier. Arabic, Turkish and Persian maqam traditions use intervals smaller than a semitone. The Western twelve-note system is not a universal grammar, it is one solution among several.

Three. Real instruments are not tuned to the theoretical values

The piano is the clearest case. String stiffness makes its harmonics come out slightly sharp of the integer multiples, a phenomenon called inharmonicity. So tuners stretch the octaves wider than theory. The resulting deviation, flat in the bass and sharp in the treble relative to equal temperament, is called the Railsback curve after the man who measured it in the 1930s. Turn that around: a well-tuned piano is not a piano that follows equal temperament exactly.

Four. Theory is description after the fact

The rules of harmony were not set by a committee. They are a later tidying-up of patterns repeatedly observed in European music of particular periods. Closer to a style guide than a compiler. Breaking a rule does not automatically produce bad music, and in fact much of music history moved in the direction of breaking them.

One more thing on top. Give the same score to two performers and you get two different pieces of music. Micro-timing, changes in dynamics, timbre, where a phrase is cut. That unwritten layer is more than half of what the listener receives. Knowing the structure is knowing the material, and knowing the material does not make you a cook. The wall that generative models playing along in real time run into is ultimately this layer.

What I could not confirm

The figures here were confirmed in public standards and papers or computed directly from the ratios. These are the gaps.

  • ISO 16 is confirmed only through the 1975 edition. Whether a later revision exists is something I could not establish beyond the catalogue listing
  • I did not state when equal temperament became standard. There is a long gap between theoretical description and practical adoption, and the transition differs by region and instrument. I found no primary source that would pin it down
  • How much of consonance perception the harmonic overlap theory explains has no current synthesis I could confirm. What is in the text is the Plomp-Levelt critical band relationship and the Tsimane result, nothing bridging them
  • I did not give specific cent values for the Railsback curve. They vary by individual instrument and by tuner
  • Precision of the cent values. The table values were computed as 1200 × log₂(ratio) and written to three decimals. That does not mean real instruments hold that precision
  • The critical bandwidth figures come from the 1965 study as reported. I did not cross-check them against later research

Where to start

One recommendation about order. Handle the sound first and attach the names later. Learning the names first is the main reason this field feels hard.

  • Generating frequencies yourself is step one. Emit a sine wave, double it, multiply by 1.5, multiply by 1.26 (2 to the 4/12, a major third). Experience what the ratios sound like first and the rest gets easier
  • Arrays come next. Run [2,2,1,2,2,2,1] in code to see which notes get selected, shift the starting point to transpose, rotate the array to build modes. Twenty lines will do it
  • Chords start as index application. Apply [0,2,4] to the scale array to pull a triad, then check the difference between [0,4,7] and [0,3,7] by ear. Hearing one semitone flip the impression beats ten lines of explanation
  • Handle rhythm separately. Do not mix it with pitch. Build a quarter-note grid and push a note one slot over to make a syncopation
  • Notation comes last. Learn the symbols once you know the structure and it takes days; start with the symbols and it takes months. The order is the whole thing

Being comfortable with MIDI helps. Note numbers are integers, so every experiment above becomes array manipulation. For making sound, the audio API built into the browser is enough, and if you want to look at waveforms directly you can start with something like an audio editor that runs in the browser with nothing to install.

One last note. This approach is good for understanding music theory quickly and insufficient on its own for making music. Knowing the structure shows you why things are as they are, cuts what you have to memorize enormously, and keeps you from getting lost when reading further material. That is what it buys. The rest is filled in by hours of listening and hours of hands on the instrument. Whether you use generative tools or play it yourself, that part does not shrink.
