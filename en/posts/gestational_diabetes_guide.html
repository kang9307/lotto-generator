Gestational Diabetes - Why It Happens Only in Pregnancy, and What Remains After Birth

From the resistance the placenta creates to the postpartum retest, only what the guidelines confirm

2026-09-01 Health & Wellness

When a mid-pregnancy test comes back with high blood sugar, two thoughts usually arrive together. What did I eat wrong, and is something going to happen to the baby. Neither question quite reaches the structure of gestational diabetes.

Gestational diabetes arises on top of a change that pregnancy itself creates. To keep sending glucose to the fetus, the placenta releases hormones that make the mother's insulin work less well, and that is design rather than malfunction. The problem appears when the pancreas cannot match the raised demand. The general account of blood sugar and insulin resistance is covered in the prediabetes and blood sugar control guide, so it is not repeated here.

Stated up front. This article is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow the judgment of the treating clinician. The thresholds given here are the framework clinicians use when reading results, not a tool for judging your own. During pregnancy, change nothing about food, activity or medication without discussing it with your clinician first.

Why it happens in pregnancy - the resistance the placenta creates

Pregnancy adds an organ the body did not have. The placenta is both the channel that carries nutrients to the fetus and an endocrine organ. Human placental lactogen, progesterone, prolactin, cortisol and growth-hormone-family peptides enter the mother's bloodstream from it.

One of the things these hormones do is interfere with how insulin acts. Insulin is the key that lets glucose in the blood into cells, and placental hormones make that key work less well. Glucose therefore lingers a little longer in the mother's blood and more of it crosses to the fetus. It is a mechanism for giving the fetus first claim on fuel.

Human placental lactogen rises in step with the size of the placenta. The further the pregnancy goes, the larger the placenta, the more hormone, and the higher the resistance. That is why gestational diabetes shows up in the middle and later part of pregnancy rather than at the start.

  • Definition — high blood sugar first detected during pregnancy in someone who did not previously have diabetes. It is a separate category from diabetes that predates the pregnancy
  • Hormones responsible — placental lactogen, progesterone, prolactin, cortisol and growth-hormone-family peptides released by the placenta
  • Why later in pregnancy — as the placenta grows, secretion rises and resistance accumulates

Resistance rises in normal pregnancy too - where the paths diverge

This is the most frequently misread point. Rising insulin resistance is not by itself evidence of gestational diabetes. Resistance rises in a completely uncomplicated pregnancy as well. The same amount of insulin doing less work as pregnancy advances is a change that happens to every pregnant person.

So the divergence is not on the resistance side but on the pancreatic side. When resistance rises, the body increases insulin secretion to balance it, and if that compensation is sufficient, blood sugar stays in range. When compensation cannot keep pace, blood sugar rises and the diagnosis is made. Pregnancy is, in effect, a stress test of the pancreas and its reserve.

Knowing this structure settles two things. First, gestational diabetes is not the result of anything the pregnant person did wrong. Second, the shortfall in pancreatic reserve that pregnancy revealed is not the kind of thing that disappears at delivery. That is the root of what section 12 returns to.

  • Resistance rises in normal pregnancy too. The rise in resistance is not itself a disease
  • Gestational diabetes appears when insulin secretion cannot rise to match it
  • That is why it also occurs in people who are lean and in people whose eating habits are not poor

Why testing falls at 24 to 28 weeks

The Korean Diabetes Association and the Korean Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology both advise screening at 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy for people without particular risk factors. Obstetric guidance in the United States uses the same window.

The influence of placental hormones grows in proportion to time. Test too early and resistance has not risen far enough, so someone whose compensating reserve is short can still test normal. Test too late and there is less time to start management and change the outcome of the pregnancy. Weeks 24 to 28 are the window where hormone effects approach their peak while enough of the pregnancy remains.

Risk factors change the sequence. The Korean Diabetes Association advises screening from early pregnancy for people at higher risk, and retesting at 24 to 28 weeks even when that early result is normal. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care further recommend that people with risk factors be assessed before 15 weeks for diabetes that predates the pregnancy but was never diagnosed. The two are managed differently afterwards, so the distinction matters.

  • Without risk factors — screening at 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy
  • With risk factors — test early first, and retest at 24 to 28 weeks even if normal
  • Commonly listed risk factors — older age, obesity, family history of diabetes, previous gestational diabetes, a previous large baby. Because it also occurs in people with no risk factors at all, screening is offered to every pregnant person

Why diagnosis has two routes - one-step and two-step

There is more than one way to diagnose it. The long-established two-step approach and the one-step approach that finishes in a single test are both in use. The two-step approach has traditionally been used in Korea, and since the Korean Diabetes Association also recommended the 75 g test in 2013, both are accepted.

The two-step approach splits the process. A screening test comes first, in which 50 g of glucose is taken without fasting and blood sugar is measured an hour later; if that is elevated, a 100 g test after at least eight hours of fasting confirms the diagnosis. Because screening and confirmation are separate, most people finish at the first stage. The one-step approach fasts from the outset and completes a single 75 g test.

Wanting to hold these numbers up against your own results is natural, but this is a good place to stop. Even for the same 100 g test, the verdict shifts depending on which criteria a hospital uses. These numbers are the starting point of a diagnosis, not its conclusion.

  • Under the 75 g criteria a diagnosis follows from a single value out of three; under the 100 g criteria two or more of four are required
  • The 50 g screening test requires no fasting, while the 100 g and 75 g diagnostic tests require at least eight hours of fasting
  • Which method you receive is set by hospital policy and your risk profile. It is not something the pregnant person selects

The two methods identify different numbers of people

The difference is not only procedural. The one-step approach has lower thresholds and diagnoses on a single abnormal value, so applied to the same population it classifies more people as having gestational diabetes.

One trial measured that difference directly. In a study that randomly assigned 23,792 pregnant people in the United States, 16.5 percent were diagnosed under the one-step approach and 8.5 percent under the two-step approach. A Korean journal review likewise reports that applying the IADPSG criteria raises prevalence roughly threefold compared with Carpenter-Coustan.

Yet in that same trial the pregnancy outcomes were essentially identical between the groups. Large-for-gestational-age births were 8.9 percent versus 9.2 percent, gestational hypertension or preeclampsia 3.6 percent versus 3.5 percent, and primary cesarean delivery 24.0 percent versus 24.6 percent. Nearly twice as many diagnoses, and the outcomes did not move. That is why neither method has been settled as clearly better, and why the guidelines have kept both.

  • Lower thresholds bring milder hyperglycemia into management. That is not a bad thing in itself, but there is not yet an assurance that diagnosing more improves outcomes
  • Adherence to the assigned method in that trial was 66 percent for one-step and 92 percent for two-step, a figure that likely reflects the burden of a single fasting test
  • Different criteria also mean a different verdict is possible at a different hospital. If you have results from a previous pregnancy, bring them to the visit

Why it is managed - the confirmed risks

The reason for managing it comes from the observation that hyperglycemia short of overt diabetes still connects to pregnancy outcomes. When maternal blood sugar is high, more glucose crosses the placenta, and the fetal pancreas responds by making more insulin. Insulin is also a growth signal, so the fetus grows larger than it needs to.

One caution belongs here. These risks were observed in unmanaged hyperglycemia; they are not a future fixed by the diagnosis itself. Outcomes after management begins depend far more on subsequent glucose control than on the numbers at diagnosis. A diagnosis is closer to information that something manageable has been identified.

  • Macrosomia — the Korean Diabetes Association cites birth weight above 4 kg as the reference point
  • Shoulder dystocia — the baby's shoulder catching during delivery. The larger the baby, the higher the chance
  • Neonatal hypoglycemia — it can occur when the glucose supply stops at birth. That is why newborn blood sugar is checked
  • Higher cesarean rate — fetal size and the difficulty of delivery rise together
  • Gestational hypertension and preeclampsia — these appear alongside it more often. That is what makes section 11 important
  • Also — preterm birth, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and jaundice. The Korean Diabetes Association states that the child's risk of metabolic syndrome doubles

The order of management - food and activity come first

Many people assume a diagnosis means injections immediately. It does not work that way. Food and physical activity are the first-line treatment. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care describe lifestyle change as the core of management that often suffices on its own, and state that 70 to 85 percent of people diagnosed under Carpenter-Coustan criteria manage with lifestyle alone.

Physical activity is generally framed as at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise a week, spread out. But exercise in pregnancy carries limits set by obstetric circumstances. Risk of preterm labor, cervical status and placental position all change the advice, so what exercise and how much must be settled with the treating clinician first.

Pharmacologic treatment is added when targets are not met. Insulin is the standard in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta. The same Standards of Care specify that two widely used oral glucose-lowering agents cross the placenta and should not be used as first-line therapy. Which medication to start and when is clinical territory, and this article does not cover it.

  • First line — dietary adjustment and physical activity, with self-monitoring of blood glucose attached
  • Second line — medication added when targets are not met. Lifestyle management does not stop; the two run together
  • Why insulin is standard — it does not cross the placenta, and there is long accumulated experience of its use in pregnancy
  • Needing medication is not a failure — pancreatic reserve is not something effort can increase

Self-monitoring - when to measure and what to record

Self-monitoring is not an accessory but the central axis of management. Gestational diabetes usually produces no symptoms, so you cannot tell from how you feel whether it is being controlled.

The measurement points are fasting and post-meal. Fasting glucose reflects what the liver released overnight; post-meal glucose shows how the body responded to what was just eaten. They are different pieces of information, and one alone is not enough. Targets are framed as below, but the target that applies to an individual is set by the treating clinician.

Measurement pointValue given in the Standards of CareWhat this value shows
FastingBelow 95 mg/dLThe baseline state independent of meals. The balance between overnight hepatic glucose output and basal insulin
One hour after a mealBelow 140 mg/dLNear the peak. What and how much was eaten shows here most clearly
Two hours after a mealBelow 120 mg/dLHow well glucose comes back down. Either the one-hour or the two-hour point is used

The value of a log lies in the pattern rather than in any single number. Consistently high morning fasting values and consistently high evening post-meal values are different situations calling for different responses. A log brought to a visit is far more useful when each glucose value sits alongside the time of measurement, what was eaten just before, and that day's activity.

  • Post-meal timing is counted from the moment the meal began, not from when it was finished
  • Cutting the next meal sharply in response to one high value is not advised. That adjustment creates other problems
  • How often and when to measure varies with the stage of management. The advice differs between diet-only control and control that includes medication

Food - not removing carbohydrate but dividing it

The most common misconception is that cutting out carbohydrate solves it. In pregnancy that direction is wrong. The minimum intakes given in the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care for pregnancy are 175 g of carbohydrate, 71 g of protein and 28 g of fiber. That carbohydrate minimum is calculated from maternal function and the glucose the fetal brain requires.

So what is adjusted? Not the elimination of the total but the division of how much arrives at once. The same quantity concentrated in one meal sends post-meal glucose up sharply, while the same quantity spread across several lowers the peak. Eating protein, fat and vegetables alongside it slows absorption further.

Specific meal composition and quantity require looking at gestational week, weight change, activity level and the glucose log together, so they cannot be prescribed here. A hospital nutrition consultation is far more accurate. Only the direction is set out below.

  • Divide rather than cut — keep the daily total while reducing how much lands in one sitting
  • Change the type — whole grains, vegetables and legumes are absorbed more gradually than refined carbohydrate
  • Do not eat it alone — the peak is lower when carbohydrate is eaten with protein, fat and fiber
  • Look at liquid sugar first — sugar arriving in a drink is absorbed faster than food that is chewed
  • This is not a weight-loss diet — weight management in pregnancy is set by the treating clinician. Do not read this as a reason to cut calories on your own

Ketones - why extreme restriction is risky

When carbohydrate is cut sharply, the body breaks down fat for energy and ketone bodies form in the process. This matters in pregnancy because a pregnant person forms ketones more readily. Glucose is continuously drawn off toward the fetus, so when supply falls, the threshold for shifting to fat breakdown is low.

The Standards of Care handle the issue practically. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care state that fasting urine ketone testing may be useful to identify those who are severely restricting carbohydrates. Ketones are treated less as a target in themselves than as a signal that dietary adjustment has left the safe range.

On top of that, a sharply reduced-carbohydrate diet loses other nutrients along with it. Trial results indicate a higher risk of micronutrient deficiency when intake drops to 165 g a day or below. That is why dietary adjustment in pregnancy is not decided on blood sugar alone.

  • Carbohydrate restriction lowers blood sugar at the cost of risk on another axis, ketones and nutritional deficiency
  • Do not cut carbohydrate further on your own because targets are not being met. That is the point at which to discuss medication
  • The widely circulated extreme low-carbohydrate and fasting protocols do not transfer to pregnancy. They were designed for people who are not pregnant

Signs that call for care without delay

Gestational diabetes itself usually produces no symptoms. So if a symptom appears, the first thought is that it signals something else. The items below are not for waiting until the next check-up; they call for contacting the hospital or going to an emergency department at that moment.

Seek care without delay in these situations.
  • Fetal movement has decreased or feels different — if the baby is not moving as usual, call rather than wait. This is not something to check on the next day
  • A severe headache, or one that will not settle — particularly a headache that pain relief does not improve or that keeps worsening
  • Vision is wrong — blurring, flashing lights or spots, sensitivity to light, temporary loss of sight
  • Pain below the breastbone or under the right ribs — easy to dismiss as indigestion, but it is a sign seen in preeclampsia
  • Sudden swelling, severe breathlessness, rapid weight gain
  • Vomiting that will not stop and keeps bringing food back up — if you are on medication, blood sugar can swing sharply
  • Cold sweat, trembling hands, severe dizziness, confusion — these can be hypoglycemia while on medication. Get the response plan in advance
Headache, visual change, epigastric pain and swelling appearing together are a known combination suggesting preeclampsia. Since it accompanies gestational diabetes more often, it is right to have even one of them checked.

The purpose of this list is not to raise anxiety but to draw the line in advance where waiting is not an option. Most of the time, checking finds nothing wrong. When you are unsure, calling is always the right choice.

After birth - the most practical part of this article

When the placenta leaves the body at delivery, the hormones that created insulin resistance leave with it. Blood sugar in someone who had gestational diabetes therefore usually returns to normal after birth. Many people take the story to end there.

But recall the structure from section 2 and something remains visible. Pregnancy was a stress test of pancreatic reserve, and gestational diabetes was the result that the reserve fell short. The demand is gone, but the fact that the reserve was short stays exactly where it was.

A systematic review published in BMJ in 2020 put numbers on this. Pooling 20 studies and 1,332,373 people, it found that the risk of type 2 diabetes in those with a history of gestational diabetes was about 9.51 times that of those without, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 7.14 to 12.67. Cumulative incidence kept climbing: 9.22 percent at 1 to 5 years after delivery, 12.06 percent at 5 to 10 years, and 16.15 percent beyond 10 years. The American Diabetes Association also describes lifetime risk at 50 to 60 percent.

The postpartum retest is in practice the step most often skipped. The period right after birth leaves the least room to attend to one's own tests, and the sense that blood sugar has returned to normal erases the felt need for it. Booking the retest before discharge is the simplest answer to that.

  • The retest uses a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test because fasting glucose alone misses some cases
  • A normal result does not remove the risk. That is why follow-up every 1 to 3 years is recommended
  • High risk also means a group with high preventability. The earlier it is found, the longer the window for intervention
  • The retest window differs by guideline. The Korean Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology advises 6 to 8 weeks after delivery, then at least every 3 years

Recurrence in a later pregnancy

Someone who has had it once is likely to have it again. The Korean Diabetes Association gives a recurrence rate of over 50 percent. A nationwide study using Korean health insurance data found that of 15,086 people diagnosed in a first pregnancy between 2015 and 2021, 37.6 percent had a recurrence. The spread between the two figures comes from differences in population and diagnostic criteria.

The same study looked at recurrence factors. Recurrence was more likely where the first pregnancy involved gestational hypertension, where the gestational age at the first delivery was earlier, and where the first baby's birth weight was higher.

In practice this information is used in one place. Screening will begin early in the next pregnancy. A prior history is a leading high-risk factor, and testing is repeated at 24 to 28 weeks even when the early result is normal. Telling the clinician when the previous diagnosis was made and which test was used helps.

  • Recurrence rates range from 37.6 percent to over 50 percent depending on the source. Either is reason enough for early testing
  • A recurrence is not harder to manage than the first time. You start already knowing what to do
  • Checking blood sugar between pregnancies makes it much easier to distinguish pre-existing diabetes early in the next one

The relationship with breastfeeding

For someone who has had gestational diabetes, breastfeeding has been observed to connect not only to the baby's benefit but to the mother's later glucose status. A United States study that followed more than 1,000 people diagnosed with gestational diabetes for two years after delivery is frequently cited.

In that study about 12 percent of participants progressed to type 2 diabetes within two years, and incidence fell in graded fashion with the intensity and duration of breastfeeding. Moving from formula feeding alone toward exclusive breastfeeding, the two-year incidence was 35 to 57 percent lower. As an observational study it cannot establish cause, but the direction was consistent.

That said, what is possible varies with the mother's and the baby's condition. Not being able to breastfeed does not mean management has failed. The postpartum retest is needed regardless, and it is by far the more certain step.

  • Longer and more intensive breastfeeding was associated with lower incidence. This is not a study that established causation
  • Blood sugar can still fluctuate while breastfeeding. If you were on medication in pregnancy, check whether postpartum adjustment is needed
  • Dietary and energy needs while breastfeeding differ from pregnancy. Do not carry the pregnancy meal plan forward unchanged

Correcting the misconceptions

Some of what circulates about gestational diabetes is at odds with the facts, and that gap changes behavior. Three are worth naming.

First, it is not caused by eating too much sugar. The cause is the gap between the resistance the placenta creates and the pancreatic reserve available to meet it. The real harm this misconception does is self-blame and extreme dietary restriction, and as section 10 showed, that restriction creates risk of its own.

Second, it does not end at delivery. Blood sugar usually returns to normal, but a roughly 9.51-fold risk of type 2 diabetes remains. Without the postpartum retest there is no way to notice the change in between.

Third, a diagnosis does not mean a bad outcome. The confirmed risks came from unmanaged hyperglycemia, and most people reach target range with food and physical activity alone.

  • Lean people do not get it — weight is only one of several risk factors
  • Fasting the night before makes the result normal — the point of the test is to see how the body responds to a glucose load. It only means something if you prepare as instructed
  • Insulin reaches the baby — the very reason it is standard in pregnancy is that it does not cross the placenta. Some oral agents do
Once more. This article does not replace diagnosis or treatment. The thresholds and targets that apply to an individual vary with history and the course of the pregnancy. During pregnancy, change nothing about food, activity or medication without discussing it with your clinician first. If any of the signs in section 11 appear, do not wait for the next appointment; call or go to an emergency department.
