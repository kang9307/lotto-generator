Milk Thistle and the Evidence - Silymarin, Liver Enzymes, Cochrane, and the Mushroom Antidote

What silymarin actually is, why so little of it gets absorbed, why the randomised evidence sits at different heights in alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis and fatty liver, what liver enzymes hide as a surrogate, and why the hospital infusion and the capsule on the shelf are two different stories

Aug 11, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before we start: this article is general health information about milk thistle (silymarin) and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. It is not written to promote or discourage any particular product. Every dose mentioned below is a value used in a study, not a recommendation. If you have an abnormal liver enzyme result or a diagnosed liver condition, take medication regularly, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk with a doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement.

Introduction: how far the phrase good for the liver actually reaches

Ask about a liver supplement almost anywhere and this name comes back. The bottle gets picked up on the day a check-up shows ALT a little above the reference range, or during a season of heavier meals and heavier drinking.

This is not an ingredient without evidence. Dozens of randomised trials in humans exist, and a purified form of the compound is used as a hospital injectable in Europe. The problem lies elsewhere. The answer flips depending on which disease you study and which endpoint you measure. Liver enzymes, an intermediate marker, have produced a steady stream of favourable reports. Mortality and complications, the endpoints that matter, produced no difference in the Cochrane review.

Sales pages quote the first kind. So this article does not argue that milk thistle is good or bad. It measures the height of the evidence disease by disease and explains why those heights differ. Diet and exercise for fatty liver live in a separate piece, the guide to reversing fatty liver disease; here the subject is the supplement ingredient itself. For the same treatment applied to other ingredients, see the CoQ10, saw palmetto and creatine breakdowns.

1. What sits under the name silymarin

1.1 Not one compound but a mixture

Milk thistle is a plant native to the Mediterranean region, and what people take is an extract of its seeds. LiverTox, the US National Institutes of Health resource on drug-induced liver injury, identifies the active constituent as silymarin and describes it as a mixture of flavonolignans including silibinin (silybin), silychristin and silydianin.

That is why several names circulate. Silymarin is the name of the group; silybin is the single component usually described as the most active one. Separating the two on a label makes product comparison far easier.

  • Milk thistle extract: the raw material. A large number here is not by itself a good sign.
  • Silymarin: the marker constituent group. On labels written as extract 000mg (silymarin 00mg), the figure in brackets is the real basis for comparison.
  • Silybin / silibinin: a single compound within silymarin. The hospital injectable is this compound purified, and it is not the same object as the capsule.
  • Isosilybin, silychristin, silydianin: the other flavonolignans present. Their proportions shift with the extraction and purification method.

1.2 What it does at the cell level

Laboratory work describes three lines of action: direct scavenging of free radicals, an anti-inflammatory effect that raises hepatic glutathione and inhibits activation of the inflammatory signalling pathway NF-κB, and an antifibrotic effect that blocks activation of the collagen-producing hepatic stellate cells.

Reports that silymarin prevented toxin-induced liver injury go back decades, and LiverTox notes those results in animal models and cell culture. None of that paragraph is a result in humans. The distance between a plausible mechanism and a clinical effect is unusually long for this ingredient, and holding that in mind is the key to the trial results below.

2. Absorption - putting a poorly soluble molecule into a watery gut

Silybin dissolves poorly in water. The gut works in a watery medium, so a compound that will not dissolve has little chance to be absorbed in the first place. Reviews put the fraction of ingested crude silymarin extract absorbed from the digestive tract at roughly 20% to 50%.

What happens afterwards is not favourable either. Peak plasma concentration arrives in about two to four hours and the plasma half-life is around six hours, so this is not a compound that lingers. Most of what is absorbed is then conjugated in the liver as glucuronide and sulfate and excreted in bile, with a portion re-absorbed from the gut in an enterohepatic loop.

Those constraints are why formulation genuinely matters here.

  • Micronisation: reducing particle size to increase surface area. The simplest way to speed dissolution.
  • Phospholipid complexation: binding silybin to phosphatidylcholine to raise lipid affinity. The best-known approach aimed at improving absorption.
  • Standardised extract: trials generally use material standardised to a defined silybin content, which also means the product on the shelf may not be the material that was tested.
  • The total-weight trap: a product with a large extract weight and a small silymarin content is not the same product as its opposite. Read the brackets.
Keep these separate: being well absorbed and producing a result in the body are two different sentences. Formulation marketing is almost entirely about the first one, and sections 4 onward are about the second. The question that decides the matter is whether a trial bridges the two.

4. Alcohol-related liver disease - what Cochrane could not find in mortality

This is the use that carries the reputation, and it is where the evidence points the other way.

The Cochrane Hepato-Biliary Group pooled 13 randomised trials with 915 participants who had alcoholic and/or hepatitis B or C virus liver disease. Compared with placebo or no intervention over a median of six months, the results were as follows.

  • All-cause mortality: RR 0.78 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.15). The interval crosses 1, so the direction cannot be fixed.
  • Complications of liver disease: RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.83 to 1.09). No difference.
  • Liver histology: no significant effect reported.
  • Liver-related mortality: pooling all trials gave RR 0.50 (95% CI 0.29 to 0.88), a reduction. Restricted to high-quality trials, it became RR 0.57 (95% CI 0.28 to 1.19) and the significance disappeared.
  • Adverse events: RR 0.83 (95% CI 0.46 to 1.50). No different from placebo.

The fourth line is the point of this section. A signal that appeared in the full pool vanished once the weaker trials were removed. Fewer than a quarter of the included trials reported adequate allocation concealment, and fewer than half were double blind. The authors put their conclusion this way: Our results question the beneficial effects of milk thistle for patients with alcoholic and/or hepatitis B or C virus liver diseases and highlight the lack of high-quality evidence to support this intervention.

Only one thing changes the course of alcohol-related liver disease: drinking less, or not at all. Milk thistle has never been studied as a substitute for that, and the trials above gave it for months to people who already had liver disease rather than around episodes of drinking. The idea that a supplement offsets alcohol is not only unsupported; it is dangerous precisely because it postpones the decision to stop.

5. Hepatitis B and C - the numbers from the US multicentre trial

In viral hepatitis, one trial largely settled the argument: the multicentre double-blind placebo-controlled study published in JAMA in 2012.

Participants were 154 people with chronic hepatitis C and ALT of 65 U/L or greater who had already failed interferon-based therapy. They were randomised to silymarin 420mg, 700mg or placebo, three times daily for 24 weeks. Using higher than customary doses was the deliberate design choice.

  • The primary endpoint was ALT of 45 U/L or less, or below 65 U/L provided that represented at least a 50% fall from baseline.
  • Two participants in each group reached it: placebo 3.8%, 420mg 4.0%, 700mg 3.8% (P ≥ .99).
  • Mean ALT decline did not differ between groups either (P = .75).
  • HCV RNA levels showed no difference (P = .54).

The authors concluded that higher than customary doses of silymarin did not significantly reduce serum ALT levels more than placebo in participants with chronic HCV infection unsuccessfully treated with interferon-based therapy. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health summarises its own funded work in the same direction: two studies, one on hepatitis C and one on non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, did not show benefits from supplementation.

Hepatitis B is thinner still. Patients with hepatitis B appear in the Cochrane review but only pooled with the other conditions, and no body of evidence large enough to support a hepatitis B specific conclusion is apparent. Chronic hepatitis B and C both have established antiviral treatment whose targets are viral suppression and long-term outcome, not an enzyme number. That is not a place for a supplement to occupy.

6. Non-alcoholic fatty liver - where the results split hardest

This is the most interesting area and the easiest to misread. Sorting the results by endpoint makes the picture clear.

6.1 The two trials that used histology as the primary endpoint

A Malaysian trial randomised 99 patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis to silymarin 700mg three times daily or placebo for 48 weeks. The primary endpoint was a fall of 30% or more in the NAFLD activity score, reached by 32.7% versus 26.0% - no significant difference (P = .467).

A trial supported by an institute of the US National Institutes of Health randomised 78 non-cirrhotic patients to 420mg, 700mg or placebo for 48 to 50 weeks. The primary endpoint was a fall of at least 2 points in the activity score, reached by 19%, 15% and 12% - again no separation (p = 0.79). Normalisation or reduction of ALT and AST did not differ either. The authors wrote that the effect remains inconclusive, pointing to the substantial number of patients enrolled who did not meet the entry histological criteria.

6.2 Yet fibrosis measures moved in a different direction

The secondary endpoints of the Malaysian trial split the picture. Fibrosis improved by at least one stage in 22.4% versus 6.0% (P = .023), and liver stiffness fell by 30% or more in 24.2% versus 2.3% (P = .002). The authors concluded that silymarin may reduce liver fibrosis but this remains to be confirmed in a larger trial.

A signal in a secondary endpoint does not carry the weight of a primary one, because measuring many outcomes makes it likely that some will reach significance by chance. It is not a signal to dismiss either, which is exactly why the authors asked for confirmation.

6.3 The pooled enzyme analyses show a different picture

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in Annals of Hepatology pooled 26 randomised trials and 2,375 patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. The reported results were as follows.

  • ALT: effect size -12.39 (95% CI -19.69 to -5.08)
  • AST: -10.97 (95% CI -15.51 to -6.43)
  • Total cholesterol: -0.85 (95% CI -1.23 to -0.47); triglycerides: -0.62 (-1.14 to -0.10); LDL: -0.81 (-1.31 to -0.31); HDL: 0.46 (0.03 to 0.89)
  • Hepatic steatosis on histology: odds ratio 3.25 (95% CI 1.80 to 5.87)
  • Insulin resistance (HOMA-IR): -0.37 (95% CI -0.77 to 0.04). The interval crosses zero.

The authors concluded that silymarin can regulate energy metabolism, attenuate liver damage, and improve liver histology in NAFLD patients. That points the opposite way from the two trials above.

Why the split? The 26 pooled trials include many small studies of varied design, and most measured outcomes with blood tests and imaging rather than biopsy. The two trials that set a biopsy primary endpoint against placebo failed at that point. Pooling many small heterogeneous trials often yields a significant average that a single large, rigorous trial then overturns; this is not rare in clinical research. The more accurate reading is that both results are true and they answer different questions.

What sits at the centre of treatment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is not in dispute: weight loss, diet and exercise come first, and a supplement does not take that place. For reading the rest of a check-up panel, see interpreting blood test results.

7. Using liver enzymes as evidence - the surrogate trap

This is the most practical section of the article. ALT and AST are enzymes inside liver cells, and they leak into the blood when those cells are damaged. The number is therefore a signal that liver cells are being injured right now. A signal, not the prognosis.

The two do not always travel together, and that matters. Cochrane found no difference in mortality or complications while several trials reported falling enzymes. A surrogate improving while the hard endpoint does not is a recurring pattern across medicine, and when it happens the hard endpoint is the one to trust.

There is a more concrete problem underneath. Raised liver enzymes have many possible causes.

  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, now the most common cause in many populations.
  • Chronic hepatitis B and C, where treatment is established and delay is costly.
  • Alcohol-related liver disease.
  • Drug and supplement induced liver injury, which includes herbal and dietary products as well as prescriptions.
  • Biliary disease, autoimmune liver disease, inherited metabolic disorders.
  • Causes outside the liver entirely - heavy exercise or muscle injury raises AST.
If a test comes back with raised liver enzymes, the order of operations is fixed. Identify the cause first. Pushing the number down with a supplement means a treatable cause stays hidden while the diagnosis is delayed. With chronic viral hepatitis in particular, where antiviral treatment changes the long-term course, losing several months is an expensive choice. An abnormal result points to a clinic, not to a shelf.

8. Intravenous silibinin in mushroom poisoning - the same name, a different story

The sentence it is also used as an antidote for mushroom poisoning is true. The moment it is transplanted onto a capsule product it becomes false.

Amatoxins in the death cap (Amanita phalloides) enter hepatocytes, block RNA polymerase and cause fulminant hepatic failure. A preparation of purified silibinin given intravenously has been used in Europe as an antidote for amatoxin poisoning, and it has been supplied in the United States through a clinical trial protocol. The proposed mechanism is that silibinin acts on hepatic transport proteins, blocking re-uptake of the toxin into liver cells and interrupting its enterohepatic circulation.

The distinctions that matter are these.

Vomiting, diarrhoea or abdominal pain after eating wild mushrooms is an emergency department visit, immediately. Amatoxin poisoning is described as following a course in which early symptoms settle and liver failure develops a day or two later, which makes waiting because the person feels better particularly dangerous. A milk thistle product at home plays no part in that situation. Bring a piece of the mushroom or a photograph if you can.

9. Adverse effects and safety - mild, with conditions attached

Safety is this ingredient's strong suit. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health states that milk thistle, when taken orally, appears to be well tolerated, and that the most common side effects are digestive symptoms such as bloating, nausea, and gas. In the Cochrane review adverse events did not differ from placebo (RR 0.83), and the 78-patient trial reported that even higher than customary doses were safe and well tolerated.

LiverTox assigns milk thistle a hepatotoxicity likelihood score of E, the category for agents unlikely to cause clinically apparent liver injury, noting that despite widespread use it has not been implicated in serum enzyme elevations or acute liver injury.

The conditions attached are these.

  • Daisy family allergy: allergic reactions are possible in people allergic to ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold or daisy.
  • Product quality: the same agency notes that some products have been found to contain amounts of silymarin substantially different from the label, or to be contaminated with pesticides, microorganisms or mycotoxins. A safe ingredient and a safe bottle are separate questions.
  • Combination products: a bottle holding several ingredients does not inherit the safety profile of milk thistle alone. Check what else is in it.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: data are limited, and caution is advised.

10. Interactions - what to check first

In vitro work shows silymarin affecting drug-metabolising enzymes. Whether that matters in people is a different question, and the summaries are fairly consistent. LiverTox notes minimal documented herb-drug interactions despite the in vitro enzyme effects, and other sources describe the effect as weak at recommended doses.

Weak is not none. The guidance from the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health is simple: if you take any type of medicine, talk with your health care provider before using milk thistle. The items below recur across sources.

11. Regulatory position and how to read a label

In the United States a dietary supplement is not approved by a regulator before it is sold. Responsibility for safety and for truthful labelling rests with the manufacturer and distributor, and structure-function claims carry a statement that the claim has not been evaluated by the regulator. An advertisement that says FDA approved about a supplement is itself a warning sign.

That framework changes what a label is worth reading for. The useful items are these.

  • The Supplement Facts panel: the amount per serving and the number of servings. Marketing copy on the front is not part of this.
  • Standardisation in brackets: extract 000mg (silymarin 00mg). The bracketed figure is the basis for comparison, not the larger number in front of it.
  • Serving size and servings per day: this is what the trials expressed as milligrams three times daily, and where products differ most.
  • Third-party testing marks: given the documented gap between labelled and actual silymarin content, independent verification is worth more here than usual.
  • Other ingredients: combination liver formulas often add herbs whose safety records are less clean than milk thistle's own.
  • Disease claims: no supplement may claim to prevent, treat or cure disease. Copy that says detoxifies the liver or treats hepatitis has already crossed the line, and the rest of that label deserves the same scepticism.

12. How the rules differ by country - the same bottle, a different baseline

Cross-border purchases make this practical rather than academic. The regulatory layers differ.

  • United States: no pre-market approval; the manufacturer carries responsibility for safety and labelling, and structure-function claims come with a disclaimer that the regulator has not evaluated them.
  • Japan: foods with function claims are a notification system based on the operator's own responsibility, not a state approval, and they sit at a different level from foods for specified health uses, which the state does review.
  • China: health foods run on a dual track of registration and filing, and labels and inserts may not carry disease prevention or treatment claims.
  • South Korea: milk thistle extract is listed as a functional ingredient for health functional foods, and a product meeting the standards may carry a defined claim statement. Notably, the exact permitted wording and daily intake range sit in the official code, which is why this article does not reproduce the numbers.

The common thread is one sentence. In no country does approval mean that a clinical effect has been established. Regulation sets what may be printed; the height of the effect is set by the trials in sections 3 to 6.

13. What this article could not confirm

For accuracy, the items outside this article's evidence base are gathered here. This means not confirmed here, not does not exist.

  • The exact permitted claim wording and daily intake range under Korean regulation: these sit in an official code annex and were not reproduced without checking the source text.
  • How national clinical guidelines treat silymarin: whether alcohol-related or steatotic liver disease guidelines make a recommendation on this ingredient was not verified in the guideline text.
  • Mortality figures for intravenous silibinin: survival percentages are quoted from case series, but the material is uncontrolled and the figures were not carried over.
  • Whether formulation differences translate into clinical differences: no trial pitting a phospholipid complex against a plain extract on clinical endpoints was identified.
  • Hepatitis B specific evidence: the Cochrane review pooled it with other conditions and no standalone body of evidence was identified.
  • Use as prophylaxis against drug-induced liver injury: no conclusion was confirmed on co-administering it with specific medications for that purpose.
  • Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding: only the statement that data are limited was confirmed.
  • Product-level registration status in Japan and China: only the shape of each system was confirmed, not whether specific products are registered.

14. Criteria you can use standing in front of the shelf

  1. If your liver enzymes are raised, check that the order has not been reversed. Identifying the cause comes first; comparing products comes after. In that case the starting point is a clinic, not an aisle.
  2. Ask which disease and which endpoint the claim refers to. A claim built on falling enzymes and a claim built on mortality sit at different heights. Almost no advertisement uses the second.
  3. Read the figure in brackets. Silymarin content, not total extract weight, is the basis for comparison.
  4. Start from your medication list. Diabetes medication or an anticoagulant means a conversation before a purchase.
  5. Recall any daisy family allergy. If ragweed, chrysanthemum or daisy has caused a reaction, avoiding this is the safer default.
  6. Treat detox and treat as red flags. A label that crosses that line has told you how to read its other claims.
  7. Hold one baseline: this bottle does not substitute for drinking less. In alcohol-related liver disease, one intervention still changes the course.
  8. With a combination product, look at what else is inside. Milk thistle's safety record does not extend to the whole bottle.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Does milk thistle lower liver enzymes?

The answer depends on the endpoint. A 2024 meta-analysis pooling 26 randomised trials and 2,375 patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease reported lower ALT and AST in the silymarin arms (ALT effect size -12.39, 95% CI -19.69 to -5.08). Results run the other way too. In a US multicentre randomised trial of 154 people with chronic hepatitis C, higher than customary doses over 24 weeks produced primary endpoint response rates of 3.8% on placebo, 4.0% on 420mg and 3.8% on 700mg. There is a more important distinction underneath. ALT signals that liver cells are being injured; it is not the prognosis itself. The Cochrane review found no difference in mortality or complications of liver disease. A falling number and a changed disease course are not the same sentence.

Q2. Does taking milk thistle protect the liver on a night of drinking?

It has never been tested for that. The Cochrane review pooled 13 randomised trials and 915 patients with alcoholic and/or hepatitis B or C virus liver disease and found no significant difference in all-cause mortality (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.15) or complications of liver disease (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.09). Those trials also gave the supplement for months to people who already had liver disease; none of them studied a dose taken around drinking. In alcohol-related liver disease the intervention that changes the course is reducing or stopping alcohol. A supplement does not stand in for that, and treating it as though it might is itself a way of delaying diagnosis and treatment.

Q3. Does milk thistle help fatty liver disease?

This is where the evidence splits most sharply. Both trials using liver histology as the primary endpoint missed it. A Malaysian trial of 99 patients gave 700mg three times daily for 48 weeks; a 30% or greater fall in the activity score occurred in 32.7% versus 26.0% (P = .467). A US trial of 78 patients funded through the National Institutes of Health found a 2-point or greater reduction in 19%, 15% and 12% of the arms (p = 0.79). In the Malaysian trial, however, fibrosis fell by at least one stage in 22.4% versus 6.0% (P = .023), and the authors wrote that this needs confirmation in a larger trial. So there is a signal in enzymes and in some fibrosis measures, and no confirmation on the histological primary endpoint. The interventions with established effect in fatty liver remain weight loss, diet and exercise.

Q4. It is used as an antidote for mushroom poisoning, so is it not a strong ingredient?

Same plant, completely different story. What is used in Amanita poisoning is purified silibinin given intravenously, used in Europe as an antidote for amatoxin poisoning. It is given inside a hospital alongside intensive care and fluid management, and the described regimen is a weight-based loading dose followed by a continuous daily infusion. Swallowing a capsule is a different route with a different blood concentration. There are also no randomised controlled trials, for ethical reasons; the evidence base is uncontrolled case material. If symptoms follow eating wild mushrooms, the correct action is an emergency department immediately. The bottle in the cupboard plays no role in that situation.

Q5. Who should check with a clinician first?

Four groups come first. One, anyone taking medication regularly. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health advises talking with a health care provider before using milk thistle if you take any type of medicine. In vitro enzyme effects are described as weak at recommended doses, but there is no reason to stack products without checking. Two, people on diabetes medication, because an additive glucose-lowering effect is described and hypoglycaemia is the thing to watch. Three, people allergic to plants in the daisy family, since cross-reactivity is reported in those who react to ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold or daisy. Four, anyone with an abnormal liver enzyme result. Finding the cause comes first, and pushing the number down with a supplement delays the diagnosis.

Conclusion

Starting with what can be stated firmly: silymarin is not a single compound but a mixture of flavonolignans including silybin, and laboratory work shows antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic activity. It dissolves poorly in water, so oral absorption is limited, and what is absorbed is metabolised quickly and excreted in bile. Oral use has a mild safety profile, and its liver injury likelihood score sits in the lowest category.

The clinical evidence sits at different heights by disease. In alcohol-related and viral liver disease, the Cochrane review found no difference in mortality or complications, and even the liver-related mortality signal disappeared once the weaker trials were removed. In hepatitis C, doses higher than customary did not lower ALT more than placebo. In non-alcoholic fatty liver, a meta-analysis of enzymes and lipids reported improvement while the two trials with a histological primary endpoint missed it. The fibrosis signal remains awaiting confirmation.

One sentence in particular needs putting back in place. What treats mushroom poisoning is purified silibinin infused in a hospital, and the capsule on the shelf does not do that job. Advertising splices the two together often, but the substance, the route and the setting all differ.

In the end, reading this ingredient reduces to one line. Claims about intermediate markers have accumulated some support, claims about disease course are not yet confirmed, and claims that it substitutes for drinking less or for getting diagnosed hold at no point at all. Somewhere on that boundary, and after a conversation with a clinician who knows your results and your medication list, is the safer place to decide.

Medical disclaimer: this article provides general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Every dose mentioned is a value used in a study, not a recommendation for any individual. No supplement, milk thistle included, substitutes for the treatment or diagnosis of liver disease. If a test shows raised liver enzymes, identifying the cause comes before taking a supplement. Decide whether and how to take it in consultation with a doctor or pharmacist, and seek advice first if you are being treated for liver disease, take diabetes medication or anticoagulants, are allergic to plants in the daisy family, are scheduled for surgery, or are pregnant or breastfeeding. Do not reduce or stop prescribed medication on your own. If symptoms follow eating wild mushrooms, go to an emergency department without delay.
