Models Started Saying They Do Not Know

Knowledge cutoff, refusal scope, abstention training, confidence calibration — one symptom with four causes, and all four need different responses

Aug 17, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: It used to just tell me this

About a week after switching to a new model version, the same complaint always surfaces. It used to just answer this, and now it will not. The number of clarifying questions goes up, answers get shorter, and where there used to be a plain statement there is now I am not certain. The experience is unmistakable, but naming it is not. So it usually collapses into one word: dumber.

The problem with that word is not that it is wrong. It is that it prescribes nothing. A diagnosis of dumber tells you nothing about what to do next. Behind the same symptom sit at least four distinct causes, and each arises in a different layer of the system: one in the data, one in the policy, one in the scoring, one purely in the wording.

The four branches have different causes and different fixes. One of them will not yield to any amount of prompt editing and resolves only through external lookup. One disappears the moment you change a single line in how you ask. One should not be pushed against at all. Lump them together and no response fits.

Four things to take away.

  • The basis for splitting into four — divide by which layer changed. This is the spine of the article
  • Why abstention gets trained — what happens when a scoreboard rewards guessing
  • How to weigh the other side equally — which side loses is decided by the task
  • Diagnosis and practice — finding out which branch you hit, and getting answers in a verifiable shape

Whether one specific version got better or worse is not this article's concern. That argument returns every time a model changes, and it returns with the same structure each time. Knowing the structure means not restarting the argument from scratch.

1. Four things that getting more ignorant can point to

Start by decomposing. What users compress into it says it does not know is one of the four below, or an overlap of several. The dividing criterion is not the symptom but which layer changed.

1.1 How the branches were drawn

  • ① Knowledge cutoff — the data layer. The question falls outside the time boundary of the training corpus, so there is no content to begin with. This is genuine not-knowing
  • ② Refusal scope — the policy layer. A boundary drawn by safety alignment was hit. The decision not to speak is independent of whether the content exists
  • ③ Abstention training — the objective layer. The content is there, but confidence sits below threshold and abstaining was chosen. It moves whenever the scoring method moves
  • ④ Confidence and wording strength — the expression layer. Nothing was withheld and nothing is missing; only the tone softened. Same information, fewer plain assertions

The order carries meaning. Higher up, the answer does not exist; lower down, the answer exists and only its delivery changed. With ① and ② there is nothing to extract, or nothing you should be extracting. With ③ and ④, changing how you ask usually changes the shape of what comes back.

Table 1. The four branches

In practice they overlap. It is common for ① to be the real cause on a time-sensitive topic while the model answers in the hedging language of ③. That is why judging the branch from the symptom alone is almost always wrong, and why the diagnostic section later in this article exists.

2. Branch ① Knowledge cutoff — the time boundary is not a single line

The best known branch, and the most frequently misunderstood. The misunderstanding sits in imagining the cutoff as one clean line.

It is not. Training corpora draw heavily on web crawl dumps, and those dumps hold far more old documents than recent ones. The result is a gap between the published cutoff date and the point in time the model actually knows about. The Dated Data work measured that gap head-on (arXiv:2403.12958), and its conclusions matter in practice.

  • Effective and reported cutoffs differ. For newer models in particular, resource-level effective cutoffs were observed to diverge drastically from the reported date
  • The cutoff varies by topic. Sub-resources within the training data carry different effective dates, so one domain can be current while another stopped years earlier
  • The boundary is a slope, not a cliff. Reliability decays gradually as you approach the cutoff, and fast-moving topics degrade much sooner

One practical implication follows immediately. Asking a model for its own cutoff is not a trustworthy diagnostic. That date is itself a learned or injected string, and as just described it does not match the effective per-domain boundary.

So this branch does not yield to prompting. The only response that works is moving from pulling facts out of model weights to looking them up at runtime and supplying them. The article that prompted this discussion aims at exactly this point: externalise knowledge into search and tool calls and let the model concentrate on reasoning. Behind it sits the observation that facts go stale quickly while reasoning procedures persist. Being able to reflect current information without re-baking weights is the real gain of that architecture.

3. Branch ② Refusal scope — over-refusal is already a measured phenomenon

The second branch has a different character. Whether or not the model knows the content, an answer does not appear once a safety policy boundary is hit. It is the branch users find most frustrating, and simultaneously the branch where this article does not discuss circumvention.

One thing to establish first: harmless requests getting blocked is not a user illusion. It is a distinct research subject under the name over-refusal or exaggerated safety, with several dedicated benchmarks.

  • XSTest — 250 hand-crafted prompts that look unsafe but are benign, measuring whether models incorrectly refuse safe queries out of over-sensitive safety alignment
  • OR-Bench — a large-scale over-refusal benchmark of 80,000 safe prompts spanning 10 harmful categories such as violence, privacy and hate (OpenReview)
  • FalseReject — a resource aimed at reducing over-refusal by improving contextual safety through structured reasoning (arXiv:2505.08054)

The definition of over-refusal captures this branch well: a harmless query refused because it superficially resembles a malicious one. The cause sits in the shape of the boundary, not in user intent. Conservatively set or miscalibrated boundaries sweep up anything even tangentially touching a sensitive topic.

What these benchmarks admit about themselves is the interesting part. They report how often refusals happen and which prompts trigger them, but they cannot reveal the shape of the refusal boundary itself, nor whether a model contradicts itself across paraphrases of the same intent. That limitation connects directly to the diagnostic method below.

A line drawn here. This article does not cover phrasing tricks for getting past refusals. The diagnostic section does involve varying the form of a question, but the purpose is identifying which of the four branches you hit, not crossing a boundary. If the answer is ②, the correct response is not to rephrase and retry — it is to state the purpose and context of the request accurately, or to find the path that properly fits the task.

4. Branch ③ Abstention training — what happens when scoring rewards guessing

The third branch is the actual centre of the current argument. Here the model has the content, but confidence sits below threshold and it picks not saying.

4.1 Why guessing got bred in

Why Language Models Hallucinate by Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang (2025-09-04) reframed this as a scoring problem. The paper's analogy is precise: like a student guessing on a hard exam, a model guesses when uncertain, producing plausible falsehoods instead of acknowledging uncertainty.

The reason is simple arithmetic. On a scoreboard that counts only accuracy, leaving an unknown item blank scores a certain zero while guessing has an expected value above zero. Abstention always loses and guessing always wins. Repeat training, evaluation and ranking against that scoreboard and the guessing habit gets reinforced systematically. The paper explains this as statistical pressure arising from binary classification error.

4.2 What changes when you raise the abstention rate

A comparison the same work presented on SimpleQA shows the heart of this branch in a single table.

Table 2. SimpleQA results for two models with different abstention rates

ProfileAbstention rateAccuracyError rate
Rarely abstains1%24%75%
Frequently abstains52%22%26%

Read it this way. Accuracy is effectively identical at 24 and 22 percent. On a leaderboard carrying only accuracy the two are indistinguishable, and the first is marginally ahead. But what a user actually experiences is not accuracy — it is how often a wrong answer arrives, and there the values separate by nearly threefold, 75 against 26 percent.

Two completely different products emerge from the same scoreboard. The second withholds more than half its answers, so it definitely creates the impression of being unhelpful. That impression is real. What stays invisible is that the price bought a two-thirds reduction in wrong answers, because errors you never received are never experienced. This asymmetry is the structural reason the dumber impression forms larger than the underlying change.

4.3 The proposed fix — write the threshold into the instructions

The paper's prescription is not to build yet another hallucination-specific benchmark but to fix the instructions of existing mainstream evaluations — stating the grading rule on the exam paper itself.

Answer only if you are more than t confident, since mistakes are penalised t/(1−t) points.

The suggested values are t=0.5 (penalty 1), t=0.75 (penalty 2) and t=0.9 (penalty 9), the same principle as negative marking on human exams such as the SAT or AMC. The key shift is that the decision threshold moves from implicit to explicit. The idea transfers directly into prompts, as the practice section below shows.

5. Branch ④ Confidence and wording strength — when the tone changed, not the content

The fourth branch is the one most often misdiagnosed. Here the model neither abstained nor failed to know. It simply says the same thing less assertively.

Research on this layer studies epistemic markers, the expressions that verbally signal degree of certainty. The findings run against intuition enough to be worth knowing.

  • Models tend not to generate epistemic markers, and when they do they lean toward strengtheners. That is why verbalised confidence skews overconfident, though calibration tends to improve as model capability rises
  • Injecting weakener expressions often makes calibration worse rather than better. In the largest models the opposite can hold and weakeners genuinely improve it (arXiv:2507.10587)
  • The effect is not English-only. Multilingual models show the same overconfidence, and they react sensitively to differing linguistic norms for expressing uncertainty

Spotting this branch in practice is simple: extract the answer as items. If the count of factual items surviving after you strip the qualifiers and cushioning matches what you used to get, information did not shrink and only the packaging changed. Specifying a table or list output usually makes the symptom disappear.

Do not treat this branch as trivial, though. Tone genuinely governs whether a person bothers to verify. That is the subject two sections down.

6. The friction users experience is real

Explaining the structure does not license dismissing the friction as misperception. It is real, and the items below follow logically from the branches above.

  • The impression that it withholds what it knows. By definition of branch ③ this is exactly what happens. The content exists and did not come out because confidence sat below threshold, so the user impression matches reality
  • More clarifying questions. Training a model to request conditions instead of guessing when uncertain increases round trips. What finished in one exchange now takes three
  • Neighbouring questions blocked too. Precisely the phenomenon over-refusal research addresses: an obviously harmless purpose whose surface brushed a sensitive topic
  • Longer answers with no more information. Branch ④. Cushioning expands and the burden of picking out the needed items shifts to the user. Inside an automated multi-step flow, a single abstention stalls every downstream step

One thing to be clear about. Community reports of the form this version is definitely worse are anecdotes. Each describes something genuinely experienced, but a strong bias applies — satisfied users do not post and dissatisfied users do — so they cannot serve as a sample. The basis for the list above is not an aggregation of community reaction; it is that each item follows from the mechanisms in the preceding sections.

7. The other side — fewer unfounded assertions is a real gain

The other side deserves equal weight. Something was bought with the increased abstention, and that gain is perpetually undervalued in the argument because it is so much quieter than the friction.

The most direct evidence is Table 2 above. Raising the abstention rate from 1 to 52 percent cost 2 percentage points of accuracy while the error rate fell from 75 to 26 percent. To call that a bad trade you would have to argue that receiving a wrong answer is cheaper than receiving none, and for most tasks it is not.

The reason is that the cost of a wrong answer does not end with the wrong answer.

  • A confident wrong answer makes you skip verification. A hedged answer sends the user off to check; a flatly asserted one flows straight into the next step. The tendency for people to over-rely on confident models has been observed repeatedly across languages (arXiv:2507.06306)
  • Wrong answers get more expensive downstream. An error that was quoted, written into a report or embedded in code costs more to reverse the later it surfaces
  • Abstention is recoverable and a wrong answer may not be. If you get no answer you look elsewhere; something you accepted as fact and moved past does not reopen

It is also worth noting that the ability to say I do not know is a capability that has to be trained separately. If a model cannot judge what it does not know, hallucinations keep coming. From that angle abstention is not a missing capability but an added one.

8. Which side loses is decided by the type of work

So asking which side is right is a badly formed question. The same abstention is a feature in some work and pure friction in other work. One axis splits them: is the cost of a wrong answer or the cost of no answer larger?

  • Where abstention wins — medicine, law and tax where the penalty for error is asymmetrically large; factual statements a human will quote verbatim; the basis for hard-to-reverse decisions; flows that pass automatically to the next stage
  • Where abstention loses — drafting, brainstorming, output a human will rewrite entirely, work where errors surface immediately, exploratory work where retrying is cheap

Compressed into one sentence: if a wrong answer has a path to pass unverified, abstention wins; if every output goes through human hands anyway, abstention is a cost. That is why code generation sits in an ambiguous position. In a repository with dense tests a wrong answer is caught immediately; where no verification exists it flows straight through. Splitting the decision line by task type is covered separately in the argument over quitting AI coding.

Practically, the right move is different settings for different work. Trying to serve both kinds of task from one default guarantees complaints from one side.

9. Diagnosis — separating not knowing from choosing not to say

Now the actual determination. The goal is not to extract an answer but to learn which branch you are on, because the branch fixes the next action. For ① attach search, for ② change the approach itself, for ③ fix how you ask, for ④ specify the output format.

9.1 Four checks

  • Check 1 — shift the date. Ask about the same topic at a clearly older point in time. Detailed answers for the past that blur as you approach the present indicate ①. Identical blocking regardless of date rules it out
  • Check 2 — ask a neighbouring benign question. Pose something in the same domain that is unambiguously harmless. If that is blocked the same way, ② is likely — a sign the boundary is drawn broadly at topic level
  • Check 3 — ask for an explicit confidence level. A low confidence value accompanied by a partial answer means ③. That response is itself evidence the content exists
  • Check 4 — re-request with a specified format. If asking for a table or item list yields as many factual items as before, it was ④. Information did not shrink; only the packaging differed

9.2 The texture of the response differs

The four branches also decline in different textures, which separates them a fair way on its own.

  • ① is empty inside. Following up produces requests for conditions or material rather than facts, and it often blurs after starting confidently
  • ② is formulaic. The same refusal wording repeats, and while it says what it cannot do, it does not enter the topic
  • ③ is conditional. It can say this much and lacks confidence beyond that point, so the boundary is drawn inside the content
  • ④ is not a refusal at all. Everything is there, yet little remains after reading

Checking whether the same intent asked several ways produces mutually contradictory responses is also useful. That is precisely the gap over-refusal benchmarks admit they cannot see, and individual users can check it directly with their own work questions. If only the phrasing changed yet some forms answer and others block, the issue is not the content — it means the boundary is caught on the surface.

Once more. The purpose of asking in varied phrasings is diagnosis. If Check 2 or the contradiction test lands on ②, stopping there is the correct move. Continuing to rephrase is circumvention rather than diagnosis, and it falls outside this article. ② is not a prompting problem — it is a matter of reconstructing the request or finding the path that fits the task.

10. Practice — getting answers in a verifiable shape

Once you know the branch, the response is short. The point is not to dig harder for content but to shift how you ask so that answers arrive in a verifiable shape. A side effect is that three of the four branches then separate themselves automatically.

Table 3. Practical response by branch

BranchWhat to put in the requestWhat it resolvesRemaining limit
① Knowledge cutoffRoute through search and tools, and have it output the verification dateStale facts stated in the present tenseThe retrieved source can itself be wrong
② Refusal scopeState the purpose and context of the request. Failing that, find another pathCases where intent is harmless and only the surface is sensitiveIf the boundary is actual policy it does not move
③ Abstention trainingWrite a confidence threshold into the instructions. Below it, write as much as is known and mark the rest unknownWithholding wholesale despite knowingVerbalised confidence skews overconfident
④ Confidence and wordingSpecify the output format. Receive it as tables, items or discrete claimsLength inflation from cushioningFormat does not guarantee factual accuracy

10.1 Three standing requirements

Three things are worth keeping permanently in a prompt.

  • Require sources. Attach evidence to every factual claim, and require it to write that none exists when none does. Without that line the model fills blanks with something plausible
  • Require explicit confidence. Transfer the threshold proposal above into the prompt: assert plainly only above a given confidence, and attach a number or grade below it
  • Receive it in verifiable form. Instead of one block of prose, split it by claim so that which items have evidence and which are empty separates visually

The third pays off most, because splitting claims makes it possible to choose what to verify and what to let pass. Embedding this as a team process rather than a personal habit is covered step by step in building fact-checking into your workflow.

10.2 What not to do

  • Do not relax just because you asked for sources. Weights and external sources can both be wrong, and attaching a citation does not guarantee accuracy. This is a recurring objection in discussions of this topic
  • Do not read confidence figures as probabilities. Verbalised confidence skews overconfident. Use it as a relative ordering within the same model rather than an absolute value
  • Do not apply one threshold to all work. Reflect the split from section 8 directly in your settings
  • Do not try to solve it by switching models. Branches ① and ② only change shape when you switch. Model migration being closer to a last resort is discussed in choosing models by latency

11. What could not be verified

Collecting in one place what this article could not put evidence behind.

  • Refusal rate trends for specific models or versions. No public time series measuring refusal rates across generations by a consistent method is presented here. Table 2 is a contrast between two specific models, not an industry trend
  • The figures cited by the article that prompted this. The original presents a SimpleQA leaderboard top score of 53 percent and hallucination rates of 80 to 82 percent for small models on knowledge benchmarks, attributing them to an aggregation service. This article did not independently verify those measurements and carries them only as the original's claims
  • The frequency distribution across branches. There is no known figure for what share of real user complaints are ① versus ③. The four branches are a framework for separating causes, not a frequency distribution. Whether reinforcing abstention degrades other capabilities is also outside this article
  • How far knowledge externalisation actually works. The design of moving facts into tools has objections. One holds that reasoning about human behaviour and complex phenomena requires factual knowledge as a precondition; another argues that general models derive emergent capability from broad knowledge. Which is right is unsettled

Conclusion: why the same argument circles at every model change

The same argument returns with each new model. One side says capability dropped, the other calls it imagination. It restarts from scratch every time because both sides are talking about different branches using the same word.

The capability-dropped side is usually experiencing ③. Things it used to be told are withheld, so the loss is real. The imagination side is usually looking at ④. Extract the items and the content is unchanged, so nothing shrank. Both are correct from where they stand. Meanwhile ① and ② never enter the discussion at all and get folded into dumber.

So the conclusion is not that one side wins.

  • Do not give the symptom a single name. The four branches sit in different layers, and different layers need different responses
  • Abstention rate is closer to a setting than a performance metric. Raise it and errors fall while friction rises; lower it and the reverse. A scoreboard that reads only accuracy cannot see the trade — identical accuracy can hide a threefold difference in error rate
  • Knowing the structure means not fighting from scratch at the next migration. The four checks survive model changes intact

Whether a model knows what it does not know is likely to matter more than what it knows. As lookup-based architectures settle in, the job left to the model becomes judging whether the current situation calls for a lookup. A model good at that judgement will say it does not know more often. Whether you read that as capability loss depends, in the end, on what you intend to do with the answer.
