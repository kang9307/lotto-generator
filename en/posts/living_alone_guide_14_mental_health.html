Sometimes it gets lonely

They say living alone is great, but sometimes it gets lonely. Weekends at home alone can feel depressing, like nobody would even notice if I disappeared...

These feelings are normal. Today, I'll share how to maintain good mental health while living alone.

Before anything else, one thing is worth stating plainly: far more people feel this way than you would guess. In June 2025 the World Health Organization's Commission on Social Connection published a report titled From loneliness to social connection, which estimated that between 2014 and 2023 about 16% of the world's population — roughly one in six people — experienced loneliness. And although loneliness is often imagined as a problem of old age, the numbers point the other way: 20.9% of adolescents aged 13 to 17 and 17.4% of young adults aged 18 to 29 reported it, higher than most other age brackets.

The same report links social disconnection to elevated risk of heart disease, stroke, depression, anxiety and cognitive decline. That is not meant to alarm you. Read the other way round, it says something more useful: loneliness is not a character flaw or a lack of willpower, it is a condition common enough that international health bodies treat it as a public health issue. Feeling it often when you live alone is not strange — it is closer to a predictable result of the environment.

So this article is not about willing yourself out of it. It is about adjusting your environment and habits in small ways, and about knowing where help actually is when you need it. If you want a deeper look at loneliness itself, Part 18: Dealing with Loneliness covers that ground too.

Why living alone can make things feel heavier

Some days feel disproportionately heavy in an apartment where you live alone, even when nothing particular has happened. That is not weakness. It is because living alone structurally removes a few things most people never notice they were relying on. Once you know what is missing, you can respond to it specifically.

Nobody is there to reflect your mood back to you

When you live with someone, they notice before you do. A passing "you're quiet today" acts as a mirror that lets you see your own state from the outside. Living alone, that mirror is gone. The slide happens without commentary, so you tend to catch it late — often only once it has been going on for a while. This is exactly why the signal table further down exists. It replaces the observation another person used to do for you with a written record.

The external cues that anchor your rhythm disappear

In a shared home, the day is divided up automatically: breakfast at a certain hour, someone getting ready for work, a show you watch together in the evening. Live alone and every one of those markers vanishes, and the boundaries of the day blur with them. You can sleep until noon on a Saturday and nobody comments; you can cook noodles at 3 a.m. and it is the same. That freedom is one of the genuine pleasures of living alone — but it is also precisely the condition under which sleep and meal timing drift.

Warning signs get spotted late

Recovery — physical or emotional — is far easier the sooner you notice something is off. Living alone delays that noticing. You can go days without contacting anyone and nothing happens. You can skip meals or let the place fall apart and no one points it out. The problem is that these small changes push each other along: sleep slips, so mornings collapse, so you get no daylight, so sleep slips further.

The advantages of living alone remain intact

To avoid a misreading: none of this says living alone is bad. People who live alone have complete control over their own time and can design the conditions for recovery from scratch. Some things are actually easier alone — setting bedtime entirely to your own body, arranging the whole place to your taste, taking a full day off to recover without explaining it to anyone. Know what is missing, fill it in deliberately, and living alone becomes an advantageous setup for managing your mental health rather than a liability.

The recording method should be as simple as possible. One line a day in a calendar or notes app — your sleep hours and a mood rating from 0 to 10 — is plenty. After two or three weeks, a vague sense that "things have been hard lately" turns into a concrete fact: "my wake time has been slipping by two hours since two weeks ago." That difference matters later too. If you do end up seeing a professional, that record is a far more precise starting point than "I just feel bad."

1. Create Daily Routines — how routine affects mental health

Why It Matters

  • Regular routines help mental health
  • Easy to lose structure when alone
  • Small accomplishments boost mood

Routines work because they conserve willpower. When your mood is low, the hardest single task is deciding what to do. Simply opening your eyes and working out how to spend the day burns a surprising amount of energy. A routine skips that decision entirely: if you have already agreed with yourself to do a set thing at a set time, your body moves first even on bad days. A routine is best understood as a safety device you build while you feel fine, so you can use it when you do not.

The other key piece is your wake time. Bedtime is easily pushed around by whatever the evening brings, but wake time is something an alarm can control. Fix your wake time and you fix when you see daylight, which in turn settles when you naturally get sleepy at night. Let wake time float and everything else drifts with it. If sleep itself is the persistent problem, our Complete Guide to Improving Sleep Quality goes deeper.

Recommended Routines

  • Morning: Wake up at consistent time
  • Exercise: Even brief movement helps
  • Meals: Eat properly, even if simple
  • Night: Put phone down before bed

Trying to start all four at once is where most people fail. Pick the single easiest one and hold it for two weeks instead — usually wake time, since it is the most controllable. Succeed there and the others start following on their own. Note also how much weight the words "even brief" and "even if simple" carry. Ten minutes of walking counts as exercise, and a sandwich from the corner store beats nothing at all. The belief that it is not worth doing unless done properly is the single most common reason routines collapse.

And if a routine breaks for a few days, do not file it as a failure. What matters is not an unbroken streak but how quickly you come back after a break. Three days off and back on day four means the routine is still alive.

2. Stay Connected

Human Connection

  • Call family once a week
  • Meet friends regularly
  • Real conversation beats social media

The common misunderstanding about connection is that it requires meeting a lot of people. What actually matters is not the count but predictability. Having one person you speak with every Thursday evening provides more steadiness than seeing ten people once every two months. Knowing in advance when you will next talk to someone is itself what makes the interval bearable.

If you lean introverted, adjust the format rather than the volume. If group settings drain you, one-to-one calls are better; if calls are too much, short messages still count as connection. Relationships forced past your comfort threshold do not last. Setting a minimum you can actually sustain is far more practical than aiming high.

Online Communities

  • Interest-based groups
  • Hobby clubs
  • Can be helpful when used moderately

Online communities help under one condition: they need to be places where something is exchanged, not just consumed. A feed where you only watch other people's lives eats time without increasing any sense of connection, and often worsens your mood through comparison. A community where people ask and answer questions about a shared interest feels categorically different, because even brief exchanges are still interactions. Check whether the structure is one where you leave something and someone responds to it.

Complete isolation isn't good. Feeling connected to someone matters.

Weak ties count as connection too

Deep relationships are not the only relationships. The owner of the cafe you frequent, the person who is always at the gym at your hour, the clerk at your usual store — shallow contact with people whose names you do not know meaningfully reduces the isolation of a day. These have the advantage of carrying no emotional obligation, which makes them easy. In periods when meeting people feels like too much, aiming for "encounters" rather than "conversations" is a perfectly valid strategy. On building neighborhood ties specifically, Part 13: Neighbor Relations goes into more detail.

3. Enjoy Alone Time

Mindset Shift

  • Alone ≠ lonely
  • View solo time as "me time"
  • Lots of things you can enjoy solo

The same block of time alone can feel entirely different depending on one variable: whether you chose it. A Saturday afternoon you deliberately set aside for yourself and a Saturday afternoon left over because nobody called run at completely different speeds, even though the clock says the same thing. Which points to a practical move: designate a few hours of the weekend in advance as "alone time I picked." Saturday morning is a book at a cafe, Sunday evening is one film — once it has a name, the same hours become planned rather than leftover.

Solo-Friendly Activities

  • Binge-watching shows
  • Solo restaurant visits
  • Reading
  • Gaming
  • Learning new hobbies
  • Walking

One distinction is worth drawing across this list: consumption versus production. Binge-watching and scrolling are easy to start and immediately pleasant, but leave little behind, so the days you spend most on them often end with the strongest sense of emptiness. Cooking, an instrument, writing, exercise — anything that leaves a result or a visible bit of progress — are harder to begin but the satisfaction lasts far longer afterwards. Neither is bad and you need both. But if you look back on the past week and find it has tilted entirely one way, that balance is worth adjusting.

4. Coping When Feeling Down

Mild Cases

  • Get outside (even just to a store)
  • Get some sunlight
  • Listen to favorite music
  • Take a warm shower
  • Reach out to a friend

What every item on that list has in common is that they all move the body first. Deciding to "think differently" while your mood is low almost never works. Stepping outside or taking a shower, by contrast, can be executed whether or not your mind has come along. So these are meant to be used in the order of "I feel bad, therefore I am doing this now" — not "I'll do it once I feel better."

One addition: the lower the day, the smaller the target should be. Instead of "exercise," make it "put on shoes." Instead of "clean," make it "wash one cup." Surprisingly often, that single small action is what changes the direction of the day.

When It's Serious

  • Don't try to handle it alone
  • Open up to someone close
  • Seek professional counseling
  • See a doctor if needed

Defining "serious" for yourself is hard. A practical yardstick is daily functioning. Feeling bad is one thing; that feeling collapsing basic living — work, school, meals, hygiene — is a different thing. Once the second has begun, it is safest to treat the "tough it out alone" stage as over. What is needed at that point is not more willpower but another person's hands.

If feeling down lasts more than 2 weeks, seek professional help. There's no shame in it.

If it is more than you can carry right now, stop reading and reach out first.

Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 24/7) · text HOME to 741741 · SAMHSA 1-800-662-4357 · in an emergency, 911. You do not need to know what to say. Starting with "I'm just having a really hard time" is enough — the counselor takes it from there.

5. Stress Relief

Healthy Methods

  • Exercise: Most effective
  • Writing: Journaling emotions
  • Hobbies: Something to immerse in
  • Sleep: Get enough rest

Of these four, writing has the lowest barrier to entry — no equipment, no venue. The method is not demanding either: do not try to write well, just move whatever is in your head onto the page in whatever order it arrives. Worries that loom enormous while circling in your mind turn out, once written down, to be shorter and more specific than expected. It helps to think of it as writing to name the feeling rather than to get rid of it.

Exercise has the biggest effect but also the highest barrier. So rather than starting with a big decision like a gym membership, attach it to something you already do — getting off one stop early on the way home, taking the longer route to the store. If what you are dealing with looks more like chronic depletion, see our Burnout Recovery Complete Guide; if tension and worry are the main issue, the Anxiety and Stress Management Complete Guide is the better companion piece.

What to Avoid

  • Excessive drinking
  • Binge eating
  • All-night phone scrolling
  • Suppressing emotions

What makes these four problematic is a shared trait: they make now easier at the cost of making later harder. Alcohol may feel like it helps you fall asleep, but it degrades sleep quality and drags the next day's mood down with it. Scrolling all night does stop the thinking for a while, then wrecks the following morning and closes the loop. That said, there is no need to berate yourself for having done any of them. They are mostly signals that you are struggling right now, not moral failures. Reading them as signals and looking at what sits underneath is far more useful.

A word on suppressing emotions as well: advice to "stay positive" often becomes an extra burden, because pretending you are not struggling costs energy too. More often than not, acknowledging plainly that this is hard right now is where recovery actually starts.

6. Environment Management

Home Environment

  • Keep it tidy (mess affects mood)
  • Ventilate often
  • Let sunlight in
  • Decorate with things you love

Tidiness and mood are entangled in both directions. A messy place drags your mood down, but a low mood is also why the tidying stopped. So rather than treating "my place is a wreck" as evidence against yourself, read it as a gauge reporting your state. Keep the unit of action small too: instead of a deep clean, "set a 10-minute timer and clear only what is visible," or "empty the sink before bed." Maintaining just the area around your bed and the sink makes a noticeable difference.

Lighting matters more than people expect. Bright white light at night makes falling asleep harder, so shifting to indirect or warm-toned lighting after evening helps. Conversely, keeping curtains open to make the space as bright as possible during the day sharpens the contrast between day and night.

Digital Environment

  • Reduce depressing content
  • Limit comparison-inducing social media
  • Phone away before sleep

The highest-yield move in digital housekeeping is cleaning up notifications. Turning off alerts for apps that never genuinely need immediate attention is easier and lasts longer than trying to reduce screen time by force. The feeling of unfinished tasks piled up every time you unlock the phone is itself a source of chronic low-grade tension.

The other worthwhile move is keeping your charger outside the bedroom. Making the phone physically out of reach has a far better success rate than resolving to stay off it before bed. A clock or a smartwatch covers the alarm.

Why asking for help is so hard

"Reach out when you're struggling" is easy to say and genuinely difficult to do. And the difficulty usually has reasons behind it. Knowing the reasons makes them addressable.

  • Feeling like a burden — everyone is busy, and it seems like your problems would be taking their time.
  • The weight of having to explain — not feeling up to recounting the whole story from the start, so you say nothing at all.
  • Fear of the reaction — being brushed off as no big deal, or triggering excessive worry; both feel uncomfortable.
  • Believing you should handle it yourself — a strong sense that adults are supposed to sort out their own problems.
  • Fear that saying it makes it real — putting it into words feels like admitting the state, so you postpone.
  • Worry about stigma — concern that mentioning mental health will make people see you differently.

Most of these grow out of one assumption: that you have to explain everything. But asking for help is not an act of full disclosure. You can start without having your state sorted out, and in fact that is the normal case. The table below collects sentences you can actually use, by situation.

Often, receiving it with "that sounds rough" does more than any solution you could offer. And if they seem to be having a genuinely hard time, looking up the support channels above together with them is where the practical help lies.

When to see a professional — lowering the threshold

The most common misconception about professional support is that you have to be bad enough to qualify. The reality is the opposite. Going before it gets severe is closer to what counseling is actually for. You do not wait for illness to get a health check, and checking in on your mental state works the same way.

Points at which it is worth considering

  • Low mood or lethargy that has lasted more than two weeks
  • Interference with basic daily functioning — work, school, meals, hygiene
  • Methods you have tried yourself repeatedly failing to shift anything
  • Trouble falling asleep, or sleep that never leaves you rested, continuing on
  • People around you saying more than once that you seem different lately
  • Unexplained physical symptoms that checkups find no cause for
  • Simply thinking that you could use help — that alone is reason enough

Common misconceptions about counseling

  • "I'm not bad enough to take up their time" — services do not filter people by severity, and earlier cases are generally more straightforward to work with.
  • "Going means I'll be put on medication" — counseling and medication are separate, and what is appropriate is decided with a professional. This article recommends no specific treatment.
  • "It will go on a record and count against me" — if that concerns you, ask directly about confidentiality and record handling when you book. It is a reasonable question.
  • "Once I start I'll have to keep going" — plenty of people attend once, get a read on their state, and leave it there.
  • "What if the counselor isn't a fit?" — you can change. The first counselor does not have to be the last one.

One practical way to lower the threshold: look it up while you are fine, not while you are struggling. Noting down the number of a nearby clinic or community mental health center, and its hours, prevents the very common failure mode where the moment you need it, you no longer have the energy to search.

When there is nobody around to help

"Tell someone close to you" is the piece of advice that lands worst when nobody comes to mind. Maybe you moved recently, maybe a chapter of relationships closed, maybe there were never many close ties to begin with. In that situation, the fact of having no one to tell becomes its own additional weight.

Here is what is worth remembering. Helplines exist precisely for when there is nobody close by. Lines like 988, the Crisis Text Line and the SAMHSA Helpline do not ask whether you have a support network. Because they deal only with your present state and not with a relationship, many people find them less burdensome than talking to someone they know. There is no backstory to supply, and no awkwardness afterwards.

If you do need to build connection from scratch, work upward from the lowest rung.

  • Step 1 — manufacture encounters: use the same place at the same time repeatedly. A cafe, a library, a gym — anywhere conversation is optional works.
  • Step 2 — groups with a purpose: a class, a workshop or a study group is far easier than a gathering whose purpose is socializing, because the activity supplies the agenda and you never have to invent conversation.
  • Step 3 — recurring contact points: choose weekly activities over one-off events. Relationships are built by frequency more than intensity.
  • Step 4 — leave a way to reach you: after a few encounters, set up a light channel of contact. It is fine if closeness does not follow immediately.
  • Supplementary — volunteering: standing on the giving side rather than the receiving side often restores a sense of self-efficacy faster than anything else.

It also helps to accept up front that this takes time. Relationships do not form in a few weeks. What carries you through that interval is exactly the routine and the helplines described above — structure holding the line until people arrive to fill it.

7. Checklist

This checklist is not a diagnostic or evaluative instrument either. It is not there so you can score how well you did, but so that what you did today becomes visible. Missing items is not in itself a warning sign.

Daily

  • ☐ Wake up at consistent time
  • ☐ Eat proper meals
  • ☐ Get outside briefly

Weekly

  • ☐ Contact family/friends
  • ☐ Do something you enjoy
  • ☐ Check in with your emotions

Once a month

  • ☐ Skim back over the past month's sleep record
  • ☐ Compare your recent self against the rows in Table 1
  • ☐ Check whether you tried anything new
  • ☐ Confirm a local support number is saved in your phone

The third list is there for a reason. Daily and weekly items are at the mercy of that particular day, but at a monthly resolution the trend becomes visible. Changes that were impossible to judge day by day show a clear direction across four weeks. And if that direction is steadily downward, that is precisely the moment to actually use one of the channels listed above.

Frequently asked questions

Q. If I check off a lot of the items, do I have depression?

No. None of the tables in this article are diagnostic tools. A high count does not indicate an illness; it is a reference signal that you may be carrying an unusual amount right now. Diagnosis only ever comes from assessment by a qualified professional.

Q. Does the old 1-800-273-8255 number still work?

988 became the primary three-digit number for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline nationwide in July 2022. The older ten-digit line still routes to the same network, but 988 is the one to save — it is shorter, works by call or text, and is what current materials point to.

Q. I can't afford counseling.

988, the Crisis Text Line and the SAMHSA National Helpline are free. FindTreatment.gov lists sliding-scale and low-cost providers, workplace EAPs typically include free sessions, and students can use campus counseling. See Table 4 above, and check your health plan's behavioral health benefit before assuming it is out of reach.

Q. Will my employer find out if I use the EAP?

EAP counseling is operated on a confidentiality basis, with employers receiving only aggregate usage data rather than individual records. Specifics vary by provider, so the most reliable move is to ask about confidentiality directly when you book.

Q. I don't need it now, but I might later.

Looking into it while you are fine is exactly right. Save 988 and the number of a local mental health service in your phone today. When the moment arrives, there is often no energy left for searching.

Wrap-up

Living alone doesn't mean handling everything alone. It's okay to ask for help when you're struggling. That's completely normal.

Reduced to one line, this article says: living alone removes certain things, that is a matter of structure rather than character, and structure can be changed piece by piece. With no one to reflect your mood, a written record stands in. With no cues to anchor your rhythm, alarms and routines stand in. With no one beside you, helplines stand in.

And if you do only one thing today, make it this: save 988 and one local mental health number into your phone. It does not matter that you do not need them right now. That is the point — you may not have the energy to look them up when you do.

Recovery rarely arrives dramatically. It goes better for a few days, dips again, and improves in small increments through that oscillation. A day that dips again does not erase the work that came before it. Having ups and downs is what recovery normally looks like.
