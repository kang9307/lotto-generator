Copyright in AI-Generated Work - What Companies Actually Have to Decide

Not who owns it, but what goes into the contract, the registration and the internal policy - Korean practice as of July 2026

Jul 27, 2026 Law & Legal
Legal disclaimer — This article is general information and practitioner-oriented commentary. It is not legal advice. For a specific matter, consult a lawyer or, in Korea, the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (dial 132 with no area code). The statutes, government guides and foreign frameworks cited here were verified as of July 2026, and this field sees frequent amendment. Before anything that fixes a legal outcome — signing a contract, filing a registration — re-check the primary source at that moment. Note also that Korean government guides are reference documents, not binding interpretations; the final determination belongs to the courts.

Introduction: why asking who owns AI art is the wrong question

The topic usually reaches a company the same way. Someone notices that an outsourced design deliverable smells of AI. Engineering mentions that they use a coding assistant. Marketing asks legal how much of a generated banner actually belongs to the company. And the question that follows is almost always a single line: so who owns AI-generated work?

That question is unhelpful not because it is wrong but because the answer lets you decide nothing. You hear that protection requires human creative contribution, you leave the meeting, and the contract template is unchanged, the acceptance criteria are unchanged, and the internal policy is unchanged. The questions that actually matter sit one layer down.

  • How should the rights clause in the outsourcing contract be rewritten? Is one line assigning all copyright enough?
  • Can the character, logo or manual we built be registered? If so, what do we need to start retaining today?
  • Do we have to ask suppliers whether they used AI? If we ask, with what wording — and if we do not, what exposure remains?
  • If a training-data dispute lands, who bears it? How far does a vendor indemnity actually reach?
  • How many lines belong in the internal policy — one prohibition, or twelve procedural items?

This article covers those five. It does not cover copyright fundamentals such as the split between moral and economic rights, which are handled in a separate primer. It does not narrate the course of overseas litigation over training data. Security and privacy questions about putting company data into an AI tool are dealt with in another piece. This one looks at exactly one axis: how an organisation designs and documents its rights in what the model produces.

1. Starting point: Korean law requires a human

Every practical judgment traces back to one line of statute. Article 2(1) of the Copyright Act defines a work as follows.

A work means a creative production that expresses human thoughts or emotions. (Copyright Act, Article 2(1))

Article 2(2) of the same provision defines an author as the person who created the work. Those two lines are the basis for treating AI-only output as falling outside protection in Korea. There is no special anti-AI clause; the definition of the protected subject matter itself contains the word human.

1.1 If a company can be an author, why not an AI?

A common objection follows immediately: a corporation is not a human either, yet it can be an author. That is correct. Article 9 provides that where a work made in the course of employment is published under the name of a legal entity, the entity is the author unless a contract or employment rule provides otherwise (for computer program works, publication is not required). Article 2(31) defines such a work as one created by a person engaged in the entity's business, in the course of that business, under the entity's planning.

But the structure of that rule answers the objection. The work-made-in-employment regime relocates authorship of a work created by people to the entity. It does not declare that a non-human can perform the act of creation. There has to be human creation at the origin before the status can be moved. AI does not substitute for that origin — that is the premise on which the current guides rest.

1.2 What no protection really means: an unowned state

This is the point most often misread in practice. Saying AI-only output is not a work does not mean it belongs to the AI vendor, and does not mean it has been placed in the public domain. It is closer to a state in which no copyright ever arose. The consequences are concrete.

  • If a competitor lifts an AI image your company produced, you may have no copyright ground on which to object
  • The same logic runs the other way — but only within copyright. Unfair competition rules, contractual obligations and trade mark rights remain fully alive
  • No copyright does not mean free to use. The tool's terms of service may restrict commercial use, and output substantially similar to an existing work is its own problem
  • For anything that will serve as a brand asset — image, character, slogan — you need to consider protection outside copyright: trade mark filings, design filings, contractual exclusivity and confidentiality

This framing is the one that lands with executives. It is not an abstract point of law that AI work lacks copyright; it is a business risk that a competitor may copy your logo and you may be unable to stop them.

2. Three government guides: what exists as of July 2026

Korea has not enacted a separate AI copyright statute. Instead the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Copyright Commission have issued guides in sequence. As of July 2026 three lines of guidance are used in practice.

2.1 How much legal weight these carry — state the limit

There is a sentence that must accompany any internal document citing them. These guides are reference material without binding force, not authoritative interpretations. The fair use guide itself acknowledges that it cannot give a definitive answer on legality and that the outcome ultimately rests on a court's overall assessment.

So why read them at all? Three practical reasons.

  • Registration practice actually runs on this framework. Examination at the Korea Copyright Commission refers to it. If registration is the goal, you match the standard
  • Having followed government guidance is itself evidence if a dispute arises. It guarantees nothing, but it can be offered as circumstantial support that reasonable care was taken
  • It gives you a single internal baseline. Converging judgments that previously varied by department is worth something on its own

2.2 What is still missing: a TDM exception

One more thing to note. Proposals to add a dedicated text and data mining (TDM) exception to the Copyright Act have circulated for years and bills have been introduced, but as of July 2026 the Copyright Act in force contains no dedicated TDM provision. The clearest evidence is that the February 2026 guide interprets the existing Article 35-5 (fair use of works) rather than a new article. Korea remains at the stage of case-by-case assessment under a general fair use clause.

3. Registration: what gets registered and what does not

Registration is not a condition of protection. Korea follows the no-formalities principle, so rights arise on creation. Companies register anyway for presumption and enforceability against third parties. It sharply reduces the burden of proving when something was made.

The difficulty is output involving AI. The June 2025 registration guide sets out controllability and predictability as the axes: could the person decide what was to be expressed and lead the method and process of expressing it, and could they realise it as intended?

StagePoints toward creative contributionPoints away from it
Input Using a human-drawn sketch, a photograph taken by the person, or a manuscript they wrote as the input Spontaneous prompts with no settled purpose. Prompts are treated as closer to instructions
Post-production Adding substantial creative work through correction, addition or editing, such as iterative localised revision Trivial changes such as a slight colour shift or a resize
Selection and arrangement Selecting and arranging material from multiple outputs to serve a particular theme or intent Mechanical listing in whatever order the outputs arrived
Effect of registration Registration takes effect only on the portion with human creative contribution. Where a person wrote a script and AI generated the video, the explanation given is that the script side is the protected subject matter and the video itself is not

3.1 A false filing is a criminal matter

The registration guide directs applicants to distinguish AI-generated portions from human-created portions in the filing. There is a provision every practitioner should know here. Article 136(2) of the Copyright Act provides that a person who makes a false registration under Articles 53 and 54 may be punished by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine of up to 30 million won, and the two may be imposed together.

In other words, concealing AI use and filing as though everything were human-made is not merely a question of a filing being refused — it engages a criminal provision. Writing that sentence verbatim into the registration section of your internal policy is an effective way to stop staff drifting toward the convenient omission.

What this article deliberately does not contain — There is nothing here about how to get a filing past examination or how to keep AI use out of view. That approach runs directly into the provision above. The premise throughout is to state the use of AI accurately and, on that footing, document as precisely as possible the scope that can be protected.

4. Provenance records: the only evidence that works for both registration and disputes

Reading the standard above raises an obvious question. How do you prove that substantial creative work was done?

There is only one answer: you have to retain the process. This is the lowest-cost item in the whole article and the one most often skipped. Reconstructing a process after the fact, when only the final file survives, is effectively impossible — both at the moment of filing and years later in a dispute.

4.1 Provenance fields you can copy straight into a spreadsheet

Below are the fields to capture for AI-assisted output managed as a company asset. A single spreadsheet or a set of fields in your asset system will do. The rule is one row per file.

  1. Output identifier — final filename, storage path, hash where feasible
  2. Contributor — the person who made the creative judgments. A named individual, not an account
  3. Work period — start and completion dates. A span, not a single timestamp
  4. Tool and version — product name, model name, plan type (consumer or enterprise)
  5. Nature of the input — text prompt only, or a human-made original such as a sketch, photograph or manuscript
  6. The human-made input itself — if one was used, retain that source file alongside
  7. Generation attempts and candidates — how many were produced and how many were selected
  8. Selection rationale — one or two sentences on why that candidate. This is the evidence for selection consistent with intent
  9. Post-production log — which tool changed what. Keep layered files, edit history and intermediate saves
  10. Boundary between AI and human portions — record areas, segments and proportions in words. This is the raw material for the registration filing
  11. Similarity check results — the date a reverse image search or plagiarism tool was run and what it returned
  12. Publication and delivery history — where and when it was used, and the form of any AI-generated label applied

Twelve lines looks heavy, but in practice it is three minutes at the end of a job. And output without this record is hard to register and defenceless in a dispute. If forced to rank them, items 4, 9 and 10 are the core, and item 8 delivers the most value per line written.

4.2 Retention period and custody

  • Retain for a meaningful period after use has ended. Disputes tend to surface after something has been withdrawn
  • Store on shared team storage, not a personal machine. Evidence vanishing with a departing employee is the most common failure
  • For outsourced work, require delivery of these records by contract. The clause drafts in the next section cover exactly that
  • Take care that the record itself does not absorb personal data or trade secrets. Prompts containing customer information do occur in practice

5. Clauses to put in the delivery contract

This is the heart of the article. The wording below is an illustrative draft for discussion, not language to lift verbatim. It must change with deal structure and bargaining position, and it needs counsel review before signature. What is stable is the set of issues that must be addressed, and there are five.

5.1 Clause 1: disclosure and prior approval for generative AI use

The first requirement is simply knowing. Without knowledge no downstream control can be designed.

Article X (Disclosure of generative AI use)
1. Where the Supplier intends to use generative AI tools in producing deliverables under this Agreement, it shall notify the Client in writing of the tool name, purpose and scope of application before such use and obtain approval.
2. On delivery of the final output, the Supplier shall submit a provenance statement separately identifying the portions generated by generative AI and the portions created by humans.
3. If generative AI is found to have been used without the notice under paragraph 1, the Client may require rework or replacement delivery of the affected output.

Two practical points. First, this is not a prohibition. Blanket bans are not observed, and an unobserved ban simply drives the use underground. Second, scoping approval at tool level keeps it manageable: upscaling for retouching approved, final concept generation not approved.

5.2 Clause 2: rights, split between assignment and licence

This is the most important clause and the one most often drafted wrongly. The familiar line — all copyright in the deliverables is hereby assigned to the Client — is not sufficient on its own, for a simple reason: rights that never arose cannot be assigned. Where AI-only portions are mixed in, the assignment language runs empty over them.

Article X (Rights in deliverables)
1. In respect of portions of the deliverables in which copyright subsists, the Supplier assigns to the Client, upon payment in full, all economic rights in copyright including the right to produce derivative works.
2. In respect of portions in which copyright does not subsist or in which subsistence is unclear, the Supplier agrees that the Client may use, reproduce, modify and distribute them without restriction as to term, territory or purpose, and shall assert no rights against such use.
3. The Supplier shall not provide the deliverables or intermediate output generated in the course of production to any third party, nor reuse them in its other engagements, save with the Client's written consent.
4. The Supplier shall not exercise moral rights in the deliverables against the Client or persons designated by the Client.

The load is carried by paragraphs 2 and 3. Where copyright cannot deliver exclusivity, contractual obligation fills the gap. The reuse restriction in paragraph 3 in particular may be the only available mechanism to stop the same supplier delivering something very close to your concept to another client. Where there is no copyright there is no infringement claim — but there is still a breach of contract claim.

5.3 Clause 3: representations, and why unlimited warranties backfire

Clients routinely demand a line reading the Supplier warrants that the deliverables infringe no rights of any third party. With AI in the loop that wording is dangerous for two reasons. First, it is a promise the supplier cannot meaningfully perform, so it only lengthens negotiation. Second, an unperformable warranty invites dispute and, against a small supplier with no balance sheet, ends up worthless.

Article X (Representations and warranties)
1. The Supplier represents and warrants that it holds proper rights of use in the materials used to produce the deliverables.
2. The Supplier represents and warrants that, to the best of its knowledge, the deliverables do not infringe third-party copyright, and that it has performed the similarity check procedure set out in Article X prior to delivery.
3. The Supplier represents and warrants that it has complied with the terms of service of the generative AI tools used and has confirmed that commercial use of the output is permitted under those terms.
4. If a third party asserts rights in the deliverables, each party shall promptly notify the other and the parties shall agree the response.

The shift is clear: from does not infringe to has performed this procedure. Warranting the process rather than the outcome gives the supplier something it can accept and the client something it can actually verify. Paragraph 3 pulls tool terms into scope, reflecting the reality that some tools restrict commercial use of output on free plans.

5.4 Clause 4: the similarity check that makes the warranty real

Article X (Similarity check on deliverables)
1. Before final delivery the Supplier shall perform the following checks and record the results in the provenance statement.
  (a) Images and video: search for similar images using reverse image search or comparable public tools
  (b) Text: check for duplicated passages using a plagiarism detection tool
  (c) Software: verify open source licences and check for similar code
  (d) Names and marks: search trade mark registers for prior registrations
2. Where the check identifies elements markedly similar to an existing work or mark, the Supplier shall inform the Client before delivery and propose an alternative.

The value of this clause lies in shrinking the space for did not know. Generative output resembling a specific work is a reported phenomenon, and a user who cannot see the training data cannot predict it in advance. Where prediction is impossible, mandating a check after the fact is the realistic response. Item (d) in particular is expensive to omit for anything destined to become a brand asset.

5.5 Clause 5: liability caps sized to the supplier

Article X (Scope of liability)
1. Where the Supplier breaches the representations and warranties in Article X and the Client suffers loss, the Supplier shall compensate that loss, provided that, absent wilful misconduct or gross negligence, aggregate liability shall not exceed X times the contract price.
2. The cap in paragraph 1 shall not apply where the Supplier breaches the disclosure obligation in Article X or records false information in the provenance statement.

Paragraph 2 is the design. Cap the liability, but break the cap for concealment. That gives the supplier an economic reason to disclose honestly. It is the cheapest way a contract can shape behaviour.

6. What the buying side has to decide separately

Even a well-drafted contract leaves one thing open: you must first decide what exclusivity you actually need. The table below sets out how the required protection differs by use.

Use of the outputWhat you needDoes copyright deliver itPractical instrument
Blog illustrations, internal deck imagery Enough to be able to use it. Exclusivity unnecessary Not required Only confirm the tool terms permit commercial use
Brand logo, character, product appearance Exclusivity essential. Others must be stoppable Hard to expect for AI-only portions Trade mark and design filings in parallel. Insert a human creative step and keep the provenance record
Advertising copy, slogans Exclusivity plus priority Short phrases attract limited protection regardless of AI Consider trade mark filing; secure evidence of first use
Software delivered to a client Clear title plus licence hygiene Possible for parts a person designed or modified Open source licence scanning, commit history retention, contractual reuse ban
Reports and design documents for customers Accuracy and accountability Possible where structure and expression are creative Fact verification, named authorship, a decision on AI disclosure

6.1 The risk that someone else can generate the same thing

One issue rarely raised in meetings deserves it. Similar instructions given to the same tool can produce a similar output again. That is not an infringement problem; it is a brand distinctiveness problem. When something resembling your character shows up on a competitor's banner and copyright is unavailable, what remains is trade mark law and unfair competition doctrine. For anything intended as a brand asset, the answer is to put the filing on the schedule from the start.

7. Who carries the training-data risk

This question only resolves once it is split into two layers: training and output and distribution. Discussions go in circles because practitioners merge them.

7.1 Training: primarily the model developer's domain

The fair use guide published on 26 February 2026 addresses the training stage. Article 35-5 of the Copyright Act requires the following four matters to be considered in assessing fair use.

  1. The purpose and character of the use
  2. The type and purpose of the work
  3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the work as a whole
  4. The effect of the use upon the current or potential market for or value of the work

The part of the guide that matters most in practice is the indication that commercial purpose or a web crawling method does not on its own place training categorically outside fair use. It also recommends securing permission through prior engagement with rights holders as the better course for avoiding dispute. To repeat: it is not binding.

If your company does not train models itself, this belongs in vendor due diligence. Ask the provider of the tool you intend to adopt about its training-data policy and copyright posture, and keep the answer in writing. Not receiving an answer is itself information.

7.2 Output and distribution: where the user company is actually exposed

This is the real risk zone for a company. Even where training was lawful, output substantially similar to a specific work becomes the problem of whoever released it. Lawful training is the developer's shield, not the user's.

  • Internal policy should therefore say check output similarity, not verify training sources. The latter is an instruction a user cannot carry out
  • Some commercial AI services offer copyright indemnity programmes on paid or enterprise plans. These are typically limited to particular plans and carry conditions such as not disabling supplied safeguards and not circumventing provided filters. Read the applicable terms directly at the time of adoption
  • An indemnity does not licence skipping internal checks. These programmes generally address defence costs and damages, not service interruption or reputational loss
  • Instructions that name a real person, a specific character or a named artist's style materially raise similarity risk. It is worth addressing them explicitly in policy

8. Internal policy: a twelve-item checklist

Everything above condenses into a single internal page. Trim it to your size, but items 1, 3, 5 and 7 should survive at any scale.

  1. Scope — which deliverables in which work streams. Defining it as every document in the company guarantees non-compliance. Start with externally published and client-delivered material
  2. Approved tool list — the tools and plans the company has contracted for, plus the constraints that apply to personal free accounts (possible restrictions on commercial use)
  3. Provenance obligation — the twelve fields above; who records them, for what, and when
  4. Required human creative step — for brand-asset grade output, mandate a human creative stage. No final approval on prompting alone
  5. Similarity check procedure — mandatory before external release, with method and record format by content type
  6. Prohibited instruction types — instructions naming a specific artist, brand or real person as an imitation target are banned or made subject to prior approval
  7. Registration procedure — which output is a registration candidate, the requirement to separately describe AI-generated portions, and the criminal exposure for false registration under Article 136(2)
  8. Supplier management — attach the standard clause set as an annex to the policy; apply to new contracts and at renewal
  9. AI labelling policy — first determine whether you are a regulated party, then decide, independently of that, how far to label for customer trust reasons
  10. Third-party claim procedure — who is notified and who decides when a complaint arrives. This stops an individual replying alone
  11. Training — at least annually. Getting the point across to design and marketing staff that AI work may lack copyright pays the highest return
  12. Revision cycle — review twice a year. Guidance in this field keeps moving; new guides appeared in 2023, 2025 and 2026

8.1 Sentences to keep out of the policy

  • Use of AI is entirely prohibited — not observed, and it pushes disclosure underground, increasing rather than reducing exposure
  • AI output has no copyright and may be used freely — this ignores tool terms and similarity risk
  • Do not mention AI use externally — this invites false statements at the registration stage, and becomes a regulatory breach if labelling duties apply to your business
  • Staff will use their judgment — without criteria, departments diverge, and when something goes wrong the blame lands on an individual

9. Going abroad: how the US and EU differ

If the service does not stay inside Korea, two more axes matter. The short version: the broad direction on human authorship is similar, but the regimes and procedures are not the same. Filing away the thought that the US is the same will cost you later.

DimensionKoreaUnited StatesEU
Basis The human requirement in Article 2(1) plus non-binding government guides Copyright Office examination practice, reports and registration decisions The AI Act is a regulatory instrument, not a rule on copyright ownership
Prompt-only input Treated as unlikely to amount to creative contribution The January 2025 report concludes prompts alone do not, on current technology, provide sufficient control Governed by member state copyright law
Leading example Published individual determinations are not accumulated to the same degree as in the US Zarya of the Dawn (21 February 2023) — text and the selection and arrangement of images accepted, individual generated images not Not applicable
Where companies actually get caught Separate description at filing; rights allocation in delivery contracts The practice of disclaiming AI-generated portions in a filing AI Act Article 53 — training content summary disclosure for general-purpose model providers

9.1 United States: similar logic, different procedure

On 21 February 2023, in the Zarya of the Dawn matter, the US Copyright Office declined protection for the individual images generated with Midjourney while maintaining registration for the text and the selection and arrangement of the images. Its reasoning was that a person supplying prompts does not actually form the resulting images.

The report Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability, released on 29 January 2025, then concluded that purely AI-generated material is not copyrightable and that, on currently generally available technology, prompts alone do not confer enough control for the resulting work to be authored by a human. Human authorship perceptible in the output, creative selection, coordination or arrangement, and creative modification of output do attract protection.

The direction is close to Korea's. It is not identical. Registration carries different weight in the US, where it functions as a prerequisite to suit, and the filing practice for disclaiming AI-generated material is run separately. Do not assume material prepared to Korean standards can be filed unchanged in the US.

9.2 EU: a regulatory axis, not an ownership rule

The EU AI Act does not determine who owns the output. Instead Article 53 imposes obligations on providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models. The provision most often cited in practice is Article 53(1)(d), requiring publication of a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training, following a template provided by the EU AI Office. Article 53(1)(c) requires a policy to comply with Union copyright law, including respect for TDM opt-outs.

The timeline is worth recording. GPAI obligations began to apply on 2 August 2025, and the AI Office published the training content summary template on 24 July 2025. Enforcement powers and penalties are reported to become fully operative on 2 August 2026.

  • A Korean company that builds its own general-purpose model and offers it in the EU may be a directly regulated party
  • A company that only consumes someone else's model is not a direct addressee of Article 53. If it serves the EU, however, other axes of the AI Act such as transparency duties, and contractual information requests, still need review
  • Where a model is substantially further trained or modified, there is discussion that provider status can shift. Large-scale fine-tuning warrants legal review
  • This is work for the regulatory function, not the copyright function. Naming the responsible team separately in policy reduces confusion

10. Five misconceptions worth correcting

  • I wrote a great prompt, so I hold copyright — both the Korean guides and the US report point toward treating prompts as closer to instructions. Whether the prompt text itself is a protectable literary work is a separate question, and it does not create copyright in the output
  • The terms of service grant me rights in the output, so I have copyright — terms govern the relationship between you and that vendor; they cannot determine whether copyright arises. Terms do not manufacture a right that does not exist. What you gain is closer to a contractual position that the vendor will not assert rights against you
  • AI output is in the public domain — a state where rights expired is not the same as a state where subsistence is in question. Tool terms, similarity and personality rights all remain in play regardless
  • Registration protects everything — registration takes effect on the portion with human creative contribution. A certificate does not extend protection over AI-generated portions
  • We labelled it as AI-generated, so the copyright issue is settled — labelling belongs to the user protection and transparency axis and is a different problem from ownership. Treating them as one loses both

11. AI labelling duties: the axis not to confuse with copyright

One regulation frequently conflated with copyright deserves separation. The Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of a Basis for Trust (Act No. 20676, promulgated 21 January 2025) took effect on 22 January 2026. It includes a duty on generative AI businesses to indicate that output was produced by generative AI.

  • For ordinary output, commentary describes human-perceptible labels or machine-readable methods such as watermarks and metadata
  • For synthetic audio, imagery or video hard to distinguish from reality, a label the user can clearly perceive is described as required
  • The duty is directed in principle at businesses providing generative AI products and services. Whether it reaches a company that merely uses such tools depends on business structure, so establish first whether you are a regulated party
  • Even outside the duty, sectors such as advertising, journalism and public procurement carry their own disclosure conventions or buyer requirements. Separate legal duties from voluntary policy in the document
Note on source verification — The effective date (22 January 2026) and act number (No. 20676) of the Framework Act were confirmed at the Korean Law Information Center. However, the article number for the labelling duty (Article 31) and the penalty figure (administrative fine of up to 30 million won) come from secondary sources — several law firm and specialist commentaries that agree with one another — because the statute page renders through script and the article text could not be read directly. Verify the provision at the Korean Law Information Center before relying on it.

Conclusion: what you can settle in thirty days

This is a subject it is easy to postpone until a complete answer arrives. Meanwhile output keeps accumulating, and what remains on inspection is a pile of files with no record of process. With an order of operations, most organisations can put the basics in place inside a month.

  1. Week 1 — take stock. List output published externally or delivered to clients in the past year that involved AI. The trick is to ask without sanction attached
  2. Week 2 — distribute the provenance sheet. Issue the twelve-field spreadsheet and start recording for work from here. Do not force retroactive reconstruction
  3. Week 3 — settle contract language. Send the five clause drafts to legal review and apply them to new contracts. Fold them into existing contracts at renewal
  4. Week 4 — one policy page and training. Cut the twelve-item checklist to fit your size, and run an hour of training for design, marketing and engineering staff
  5. Ongoing — review twice a year. When government guidance is updated, amend the policy annex with it

Compressed into one sentence: copyright in AI output is not a problem of discovering who owns it, but a design problem of using contracts and records to cover the gap left by the premise that protection may not exist. Contract supplies the exclusivity copyright cannot; provenance supplies the proof of creative contribution; a separate labelling policy handles regulation. Putting those three in their proper places is the best available move — and, at this point in time, an entirely achievable one.
