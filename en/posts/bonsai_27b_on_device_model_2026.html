Bonsai 27B: What It Takes to Run a 27B-Class Model on a Phone

How PrismML compressed Qwen3.6 27B down to 3.9GB, and what local agents can (and can't) do yet

Jul 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: A 27B-Class Model in Your Pocket

On July 14, 2026, on-device AI startup PrismML released Bonsai 27B. The headline is simple: a 27B-class model runs on a phone for the first time. What makes this announcement notable isn't the number itself, but the fact that "on-device LLM" has, until now, effectively meant small models in the hundreds of millions to low single-digit billions of parameters. Gemini Nano and Apple's on-device foundation models are both quite small in class - nobody had put a full 27B-class model into phone memory before.

This piece isn't a rehash of PrismML's press release. After briefly summarizing what was actually announced, I want to work through why on-device LLMs keep coming up as an important topic, why fitting a 27B-class model on a phone is a genuinely hard problem even by arithmetic, what techniques close that gap, and what it actually means for a compressed model like this to support tool calling, vision, and computer use. I'll only cite performance numbers PrismML has published, and I'll be explicit about what remains unverified.

1. What Is Bonsai 27B?

1.1 Core Specs and Two Compression Builds

PrismML is an on-device AI company backed by Khosla Ventures, Cerberus, Google, and Samsung. Bonsai 27B is a multimodal model built on Alibaba's Qwen3.6 27B, shipped in two extreme-compression builds. The first is a ternary build, representing weights with three values (-1, 0, +1) plus FP16 group-wise scaling, for an effective 1.71 bits per weight and a 5.9GB footprint. The second is a 1-bit build, using only two values (-1, +1) for an effective 1.125 bits per weight, shrinking to 3.9GB. Both builds support a 262K-token context, ship a vision encoder compressed to 4 bits, and support speculative decoding for lossless acceleration. It is released under the Apache 2.0 license, with weights published on Hugging Face.

1.2 Why It Earned the "First" Label

27B as a parameter count isn't unusual on its own. What is unusual is keeping that scale while shrinking the footprint down to something a phone can hold. Per PrismML, the 1-bit build fits within the iPhone 17 Pro's memory budget. Most models marketed as "on-device capable" so far were, in effect, born small - built from the start with 3B-8B parameters. Bonsai 27B took the opposite path: compress a model that was born large. That's what makes this release interesting, in my view.

2. Why On-Device LLMs Matter

I think on-device LLMs keep coming up not because of one killer advantage, but because five separate benefits overlap.

First is privacy. Whether it's summarizing a document or reading text out of a photo, finishing the job on-device rather than sending the input to the cloud closes off one whole path for data leakage. This matters more for apps handling sensitive input - medical records, financial documents, internal company data. Second is offline operation. Inference continuing without a network connection is not just convenience; it's a reliability question. Third is latency. With no round-trip network call, responses are instant, and this is felt directly in latency-sensitive interactions like real-time camera recognition. Fourth is cost. Cloud APIs bill per token, but once inference finishes on the user's own device, that marginal cost disappears - though, as I'll get to, it merely shifts into battery and heat cost instead. Fifth is data sovereignty. For regulated industries or enterprise customers, which country's servers data passes through is often a core contractual and compliance question, and on-device execution removes that question entirely.

None of these five is individually an overwhelming edge over the cloud. But agentic apps that continuously look at a user's screen, documents, or location to act on their behalf often need all five at once. I think that's exactly why Bonsai 27B goes out of its way to emphasize support for computer-use agents.

3. The Hard Problem of Fitting 27B on a Phone

Even compressing a 27B-parameter model with standard 4-bit quantization still costs roughly 27,000,000,000 x 4 bits / 8 ~= 13.5GB in raw weights alone. Add the KV cache, the residual high-precision parameters in embedding and normalization layers, and runtime overhead, and the real-world footprint commonly climbs to around 18GB - a rule of thumb widely shared in the local-LLM community.

The problem is that 2026 flagship smartphones typically carry 8-16GB of total RAM. And of that, the share a single app can actually claim - after the OS and background processes take their cut - is often only about half. In other words, 4-bit quantization alone makes putting a 27B-class model on a phone close to arithmetically impossible. This is the 18GB wall this release has to answer to.

Getting past that wall means choosing one of two paths: make the model born smaller (cut the parameter count itself), or compress an already-large model far more aggressively than standard 4-bit. Bonsai 27B chose the latter, landing at 1.71 and 1.125 bits - about as low as an integer-value representation can practically go.

4. How You Shrink It: Compression Techniques in General

4.1 Quantization: From 4-Bit Down to 1-Bit

Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent model weights. Training is normally done at 16 bits (FP16/BF16), and dropping to 8 or 4 bits has been the most widely used practical compromise so far. 4-bit cuts the footprint to a quarter of the original while keeping quality loss relatively small, which is why it became the de facto standard in the local-LLM community.

Go lower - 2-bit, 1.58-bit, 1-bit - and the story changes. With weights limited to only two or three possible values, representational loss grows sharply. This territory is best known through Microsoft's BitNet b1.58 research line, whose key idea isn't simple post-training quantization but training from scratch at low precision, or an equivalent recalibration process, to reconstruct weights around ternary values. It's reasonable to read Bonsai 27B's approach as an extension of that same lineage - extreme low-bit representation, partly compensated by a group-wise FP16 scale factor to recover some representational power.

4.2 Pruning and Distillation

If quantization is about representing each weight with fewer bits, pruning is about removing low-importance weights, neurons, or attention heads outright - shrinking the model's structure itself, which multiplies the compression effect when combined with quantization. Distillation works differently: a large, heavy "teacher" model's outputs are used to train a smaller "student" model to mimic it, reproducing similar response quality with far fewer parameters than the original. Most on-device models combine these three techniques - quantization, pruning, and distillation. Bonsai 27B appears to have kept the parameter count (27B) fixed and pushed quantization to its extreme instead, which I read as a choice to preserve more of the original model's knowledge than a model trained small from the start would retain.

4.3 Activation Compression and the KV Cache

Shrinking the weights alone isn't enough. During inference, each layer's activations and the KV cache - which stores the attention keys and values of previous tokens - also consume memory. Supporting a long context like 262K tokens is especially demanding here, since the KV cache grows proportionally with sequence length, risking that gains from weight compression get eaten back up by KV cache growth as context lengthens. Activation compression, attention-structure optimizations like grouped-query attention, and speculative decoding (which cuts the actual number of forward passes needed) all help offset this. That Bonsai 27B also supports speculative decoding suggests, to me, that weight compression alone wasn't enough to reach usable real-world speed.

5. Tool Calling, Vision, and Computer Use: What "Local Agent" Actually Means

What Bonsai 27B emphasizes isn't simple chat but multi-step reasoning, structured tool calls, vision, and computer-use agent loops. Why this matters becomes clear once you unpack what "agent" actually entails. An agentic app reads the screen (vision), plans what to do (reasoning), actually presses buttons or calls APIs (tool calling / computer use), and loops back to check the result. When that loop goes through a cloud round trip, network latency accumulates at every step, and the entire contents of the user's screen get sent out at every step too.

Running that whole loop locally means two things at once: privacy, because screen content never leaves the device, and responsiveness, because each step's latency is determined purely by compute time rather than network conditions. What I want to stress here, though, is that in PrismML's own published benchmarks, tool-calling performance takes the biggest hit from low-bit compression of anything measured. The 1-bit build's tool-calling score is 66.0 versus 80.0 for the full-precision model - a steeper drop than any other category. The picture of a local agent is appealing, but it's ironic that accurate tool calling - arguably the core capability an agent needs - is the biggest casualty of compression.

6. The Trade-offs: Accuracy Loss, Heat, and Battery

Across the average of 15 benchmarks PrismML published, the ternary build retains about 95% of full-precision performance, and the 1-bit build about 90%. Those averages look small on their own, but what matters is how unevenly the loss is distributed across domains. Math and coding score 91.7-93.4, a relatively small drop, while vision tasks score 59.6-65.2, the steepest drop of any category. Tool calling, mentioned above, also falls into the high-loss group. In other words, behind the "90-95% retained on average" framing is a reality where the loss is anything but even.

Heat and battery rarely show up in a benchmark table, but they're arguably felt even more directly in real-world use. Running a 27B-class model on a phone's NPU/GPU, even briefly, means a substantial compute load, which translates into heat and battery drain. The speed figures PrismML published - up to 163 tokens/sec on an RTX 5090, 87 tokens/sec on an Apple M5 Max - are desktop- and workstation-class hardware numbers, and the sustainable token-generation speed and thermal profile on an actual phone SoC still need separate, independent verification. That's exactly why I'm not making claims about real phone-measured speed in this piece.

7. The 2026 On-Device AI Ecosystem: Where Bonsai Sits

Bonsai 27B didn't arrive in a vacuum. As of 2026, Apple runs its own on-device foundation model on A18 Pro-class NPUs, falling back to Private Cloud Compute only when necessary. Google's Gemini Nano ships on Snapdragon 8 Gen 3-class or newer devices and Tensor G4 or newer, and Qualcomm's Snapdragon 8 Elite and similar chips deliver over 40 TOPS from the NPU alone. Meta's Llama family has also targeted on-device deployment through lightweight variants.

Within this ecosystem, most "on-device AI" so far has actually meant small models in the hundreds of millions to low billions of parameters - Gemini Nano and Apple's on-device model included. What makes Bonsai 27B stand out is that it isn't playing in that small-model league at all; it's dragging a much bigger league - a 27B-class parameter model - into the same hardware budget through extreme compression. Market research shows that as of Q1 2026, more than half of smartphones shipped globally are classified as "generative-AI capable," meaning NPU hardware is already substantially in place. The hardware is ready, and I'd read an attempt like Bonsai 27B as trying to raise the weight class of the model you can put on top of it, one notch at a time.

8. What This Means in Practice

I'd summarize the practical takeaways in three points. First, if you build apps, the priority right now isn't shipping a 27B-class on-device model to production - it's figuring out which features genuinely require local execution. Sensitive document processing, offline-required features, and workloads where repeated calls rack up API cost are the strongest candidates. Conversely, for tool-calling and agent work where accuracy is non-negotiable, a dual setup with a cloud fallback is the safer bet for now, given that tool calling shows the steepest benchmark drop under low-bit compression.

Second, ordinary users should be wary of assuming "on-device is always better." Privacy and latency are clear wins, but for work requiring vision or sophisticated tool use, a large cloud model is still likely to win on quality. Third, read this trend as a shift in hardware requirements, not a relief from them. As extreme-compression models proliferate, competition over next-generation NPU and memory bandwidth is likely to intensify, not ease off. I expect advances in compression to be spent mostly on squeezing bigger models into the same memory budget, rather than on lowering hardware requirements overall.

Conclusion

Rather than taking "a 27B-class model runs on a phone for the first time" at face value, it's more accurate to read Bonsai 27B as a signal that on-device LLMs are expanding beyond the small-model tier into a genuinely bigger weight class. The combination of ternary and 1-bit quantization, a 4-bit-compressed vision encoder, and speculative decoding shows the current frontier of compression techniques. At the same time, the pronounced drops in tool calling and vision prove, in numbers, that compression isn't free. As of July 2026, real-world verification of this model - especially battery, heat, and felt speed on an actual phone - remains work for the community to do. For developers evaluating on-device AI, the sensible reading of this release isn't "everything now runs locally," but rather a prompt to re-measure exactly how much can be moved local at this point.
