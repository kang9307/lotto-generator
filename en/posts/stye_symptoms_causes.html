Stye Symptoms and Causes

Which gland it is decides everything - warm compresses, why never to squeeze, and when to go in

Aug 11, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: one eyelid is swollen, and your hand is already moving

You look in the mirror and the edge of one lower eyelid is raised and sore to the touch. A few hours later a small yellow point appears. At exactly this moment most people reach up and try to squeeze it, and that is close to the worst available choice in this condition.

Styes are extremely common. In South Korea alone, national health insurance data published in July 2017 counted about 1.72 million people treated for a stye in 2016. Common conditions attract folk remedies, and a large share of the remedies circulating for this one amount to driving an infection deeper into the tissue.

The decision that actually matters is elsewhere. A lump on the eyelid behaves entirely differently depending on which gland it grew from: whether it hurts, whether it clears in days or drags on for weeks, whether an antibiotic has any role. And in rare cases, a lump that keeps returning to the same spot is not inflammation at all.

This piece starts with that distinction, then covers how to run a warm compress properly, why squeezing is off the table, which signs mean this is no longer a waiting game, and what to check when styes keep coming back.

Medical disclaimer
This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription, or treatment. Nothing here should be used as a reason to start or stop any medication on your own. In particular, do not use antibiotic eye drops or ointment without a prescription, and do not reuse leftover eye medication. Drug choice, dose, and duration must be decided with a clinician or pharmacist. Do not squeeze a stye or attempt to lance it with a needle. Children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults, immunocompromised people, and those with chronic conditions such as diabetes may need different management. If redness spreads beyond the eyelid onto the face, or you develop fever, pain in the eyeball itself, reduced vision, or restricted eye movement, seek care immediately rather than waiting.

1. Which eyelid gland does a stye come from

Korea's national health information portal defines this family of conditions as inflammatory disease of the various secretory glands of the eyelid. That single line already contains the point. The eyelid holds several glands with different jobs, and which one blocks or becomes infected determines the name and the course.

Get the anatomy straight and the rest follows.

  • Meibomian glands - arranged vertically inside the firm tarsal plate of the eyelid, opening in a row of pores along the lid margin. They produce the oily layer that caps the tear film.
  • Glands of Zeis - sebaceous glands attached to the eyelash follicles, sitting close to the skin surface.
  • Glands of Moll - modified sweat glands near the lash roots, also superficial.

Following that portal's classification directly, an external hordeolum is an infection of a gland of Zeis or Moll, and an internal hordeolum is an infection of a meibomian gland. A chalazion is a different animal. There, the outflow of a meibomian gland is obstructed and the trapped oil causes the trouble, producing a granulomatous inflammation that is not an infection.

StatPearls pushes the distinction further and calls a chalazion a chronic sterile lipogranuloma, explaining that lipid breakdown products leak into surrounding tissue and provoke a granulomatous inflammatory response. In other words, a chalazion has no bacteria to fight. That is the premise behind everything said about antibiotics later on.

2. Location dictates symptoms - why an internal hordeolum hurts more

The same organism infecting the same kind of oil gland produces different symptoms, and the reason is what the gland is trapped inside.

The glands of Zeis and Moll hang off the lash follicles just under the skin. When pus collects there, the direction of least resistance is outward, which is why the Korean portal describes the external form as one that hardens over a few days, becomes less painful, forms an abscess, and eventually drains through the skin. You can see the yellow point with the naked eye.

A meibomian gland is different. It sits inside the tarsal plate, a dense sheet of connective tissue with almost no room to expand. The same volume of swelling generates far more pressure, which is why an internal hordeolum is often the more painful of the two. The pus also heads the other way, toward the conjunctival surface inside the lid. You have to evert the eyelid to see the yellow point at all, which is another way of saying that no amount of pressing from the outside will bring anything out.

A chalazion is a different event from the start. Oil accumulates behind a blocked duct, leaks into tissue, and the body walls it off the way it handles a foreign body. That walling-off is the granuloma. With no infection, there is usually no heat and no pain, but a firm nodule the size of a small bean can sit there for weeks or months.

Hold that structure in mind and the common questions answer themselves.

  • Why doesn't mine hurt? - It is probably a chalazion, driven by trapped oil rather than infection.
  • Why does nothing come out when I press it? - An internal hordeolum is inside the tarsal plate, and a chalazion has no pus to express in the first place.
  • Why did the pain stop while the lump stayed? - The acute phase passed and the granuloma remained. This is a common sequence, not a setback.
  • Why does a clinician flip my eyelid inside out? - The pus point of an internal hordeolum is only visible on the conjunctival side.
  • Why is my eye watering and gritty? - The swollen lid margin brushes the corneal surface with every blink.

3. Causes - staph, blocked oil glands, and daily habits

The infectious cause is narrow. The Korean portal attributes styes to staphylococcal infection of the eyelid glands, and StatPearls reports that around 90% to 95% of lesions are due to Staphylococcus aureus, with S. epidermidis second. Asan Medical Center's disease library gives the same 90-95% range.

But staph already lives on your skin. So the real question is why this gland, why now, and the answer is usually that drainage got blocked or that bacteria were handed a route in.

  • Meibomian gland dysfunction - when the oil thickens and the duct clogs, stagnant oil pools and gives bacteria a place to grow. It is the direct cause of chalazia and the background condition for many internal hordeola.
  • Rubbing your eyes - this transfers organisms from the hand straight to the lid margin. It is why public health advice on prevention leads with handwashing and not rubbing.
  • Contact lenses - inserting and removing lenses means repeatedly touching the lid margin with fingertips, and loose cleaning or storage habits compound it.
  • Eye makeup - waterline eyeliner in particular is drawn directly over the meibomian gland openings. Old mascara and liner, and sleeping in makeup, push in the same direction.
  • Blepharitis - the American Academy of Ophthalmology notes that blepharitis is very common in people with oily skin, dandruff, or rosacea. Chronic lid margin inflammation makes glands easier to block.
  • Shared towels and cosmetics - the NHS prevention advice includes not sharing towels.

Korean national data fits that picture, and it is worth reading as a jurisdiction-specific illustration rather than a universal statistic. In an analysis released in August 2011 covering 2010, women accounted for 58.8% of stye consultations against 41.2% for men, and the monthly rate peaked in August at 416 per 100,000 people, ahead of September (404) and July (401). The highest age-sex group was women in their teens, at 7,158 per 100,000. A 2017 release covering 2016 again put people in their teens, twenties, and thirties at the top.

The summer peak was attributed at the time to more outdoor activity and the personal hygiene lapses that come with it. It is also worth noting that the age bands where makeup and contact lens use begin sit right under the peaks of the curve. A stye is a disease of contact more than a disease of constitution.

4. Natural course - how long is it reasonable to wait

Knowing the expected timeline cuts down both needless anxiety and dangerous self-treatment. Styes and chalazia run on different clocks.

  • Stye - the NHS says it usually improves within a week. StatPearls reports that most cases resolve within a week to 10 days without complications and that uncomplicated styes clear in 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Rupture of an external stye - the MSD Manual Consumer Version states that a stye tends to rupture after 2 to 4 days. Internal styes rarely rupture spontaneously.
  • The order of symptoms - the Korean portal describes an external hordeolum that starts slightly red, swollen, and itchy, quickly becomes markedly swollen and painful, then over a few days hardens with decreasing pain as an abscess forms and finally drains through the skin. Less pain with a persisting lump is a normal stage, not deterioration.
  • Chalazion - the MSD Manual says these disappear without treatment within 2 to 8 weeks. StatPearls puts most resolution within a month. The Korean portal reports that about 50% can resolve with conservative management.
  • Size - the Japanese Ophthalmological Society describes chalazia ranging from under 5 mm to over 1 cm, appearing in both upper and lower lids and in patients from infancy to old age.

In short: a painful stye is measured in one to two weeks, a painless chalazion in weeks. A chalazion unchanged at week three has not failed treatment. That patience comes with a condition, though — if any of the signs in the next section are present, you are not on a clock anymore.

5. Emergency and warning signs - when this stops being a waiting game

There is essentially one route by which a stye turns dangerous: infection that had been confined to the eyelid crosses into the space behind it. A thin sheet called the orbital septum separates the lid from the orbit. Infection in front of the septum swells the lid; infection behind it involves the compartment holding the eyeball itself. The latter is orbital cellulitis, and it is an emergency.

Seek care immediately - the signs to act on now
(1) Redness and swelling spread beyond the eyelid onto the cheek, forehead, or side of the nose (2) Fever (3) Pain in the eyeball itself, especially pain on moving the eye (4) Reduced or blurred vision (5) The eye looks pushed forward (6) The eye will not move properly, or you see double (7) The lid is swollen shut (8) Pain or swelling is severe and hard to tolerate
In the United States, call 911; in the United Kingdom, 999 or NHS 111 for urgent advice. Children can progress faster, so act earlier on the same signs. Do not squeeze or manipulate the lesion in this situation. Orbital cellulitis is treated as an admission-level emergency, because delayed treatment can lead to vision loss and intracranial complications.

One more line worth reading backwards: the NHS states that with a stye your eye may be red and watery but your vision should not be affected. Turn that around and it becomes a rule. If vision has changed, you are no longer looking at a simple stye.

6. Why warm compresses are the standard, and how to do them

Warm compresses sit at the centre of care for a mechanical reason. Meibomian oil is soft near body temperature and thickens as it cools. When a duct is obstructed, holding warmth long enough thins the stagnant oil and gives it a path out. Increased local blood flow, clearing inflammatory products, is the secondary aim.

Korea's national health information portal calls warm compresses an effective and frequently used treatment, specifying roughly 40 to 43 degrees Celsius and about 10 minutes twice a day. The numbers differ between sources, and that variation is itself information.

The spread has a simple explanation: almost no randomized trial ever set these numbers. As covered below, a Cochrane review found no trials at all of non-surgical treatment for acute internal hordeolum. So rather than memorising one row, take the common denominator: warm, long enough, repeated.

In practice, what separates a useful compress from a useless one is heat retention.

  • Wash your hands first. This routine involves touching the lid margin.
  • A damp cloth cools in a minute or two. Without rewarming midway, you deliver less than half the time on the label.
  • Hotter is not better. Eyelid skin is among the thinnest on the body and burns easily. Test on the inner wrist and settle on comfortably warm.
  • Microwaved compresses can hold hot spots. Shake to even the heat, check the temperature, then apply.
  • Apply with the eye closed. This is time spent delivering heat, not a rubbing exercise.
  • Afterwards, sweep the lid margin gently toward the lashes. That is massage, not expression. If it hurts, you have crossed from care into injury.
  • Rest contact lenses and eye makeup until it clears. Both the AAO and the NHS advise avoiding them while an infection is present.
  • Do not share towels. It is the simplest way to cut household transmission.

7. Why you must not squeeze it - the force goes the wrong way

The American Academy of Ophthalmology states that you should not squeeze or try to pop a stye or chalazion, because it can spread the infection. The NHS adds that you should not remove an eyelash yourself. Guidance from the Beijing Municipal Health Commission is the bluntest of the three: it forbids squeezing a stye that has not yet come to a head or has already ruptured, warning that spreading the infection can cause cellulitis and cavernous sinus septic thrombosis.

Why squeezing is treated so severely comes down to the direction of force. Finger pressure does not lift pus out; it drives it along the path of least resistance, into the tissue planes. Behind the eyelid is the compartment containing the eyeball, and the veins of the central face have routes that connect toward the interior of the skull. This is not a site to treat like a pimple anywhere else.

  • Do not press or squeeze with fingernails. An internal hordeolum is inside the tarsal plate, so there is nothing outside pressure can reach.
  • Do not lance it with a needle, pin, or anything similar. Holding a needle in a lighter flame is not sterilisation; it mostly deposits soot.
  • Do not pull out eyelashes. The NHS names this explicitly.
  • If it has already burst, do not squeeze the rest out. Wipe away what drains with clean gauze and wash your hands.
  • Do not ask someone else to squeeze it for you. A better view does not make the pressure safer.
  • Do not strap it down with a patch. An eye patch is not a treatment, and the pressure does not help.

If drainage is genuinely needed, both the decision and the procedure belong in a clinic. The Korean portal states that when an abscess does not drain or persists, an incision is made to drain it. There is no way to imitate that outcome by hand.

8. Antibiotic drops and ointment - when they help, and how thin the evidence is

This is where misconceptions cluster. Taken in order:

First, antibiotics generally do not work on a chalazion, because it is not an infection. The MSD Manual states that antibiotics are usually not effective for chalazia.

Second, most styes also clear without antibiotics. The MSD Manual says antibiotics usually do not help much, because most styes tend to resolve within a few days even without them. StatPearls notes that little evidence demonstrates a benefit from topical antibiotics, while adding that erythromycin ointment for 7 to 10 days has nonetheless been recommended.

Third, prescriptions are still written for a reason. The Korean portal explains that applying antibiotic ointment to the lid margin can prevent the infection from spreading to neighbouring lash roots, shortening the treatment period. The aim, in other words, is closer to containment than to dissolving the lesion. When infection has spread beyond the eyelid into cellulitis, systemic antibiotics are required.

Fourth, the floor under all of this is thinner than most people assume. A Cochrane review published in 2017 on non-surgical interventions for acute internal hordeolum — comparing warm compresses, lid scrubs, antibiotics, and steroids against observation or placebo — found no randomized trials at all. The authors concluded there was no evidence either for or against the effectiveness of these treatments. That means untested, not disproven, but it is a firm brake on anyone insisting a particular drop is essential.

From which some practical rules follow.

  • Do not start antibiotic drops or ointment without a prescription. Whether they are indicated depends on which lesion this is, and that requires looking and palpating.
  • Do not reuse leftover eye medication. An opened bottle can be contaminated at the tip, and last time's prescription is not evidence about this episode.
  • Be especially careful with steroid combination drops. Used without knowing the cause, they can create a situation that is bad for the cornea.
  • Do not share a family member's bottle. The tip has already touched someone else's lid.
  • Do not stop or switch after a day because it has not cleared. Duration and changes are the prescriber's call.

9. Incision, drainage, and steroid injection - how the call is made

Sometimes conservative care is not the end of it. The thresholds are broadly time, size, and how much trouble it is causing.

  • Incision and drainage of a stye - the Korean portal describes incising to drain when an abscess will not discharge or persists. StatPearls says incision and drainage may be performed if the stye develops into an abscess or fails to resolve within 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Incision and curettage of a chalazion - the Korean portal describes incising and scraping out the contents when the lesion is large enough to cause discomfort or has not improved over a long period.
  • Intralesional steroid - the Korean portal notes that a small amount of steroid is occasionally injected into the lesion. StatPearls describes intralesional steroids when infection has been excluded. The Japanese Ophthalmological Society likewise describes injecting a steroid suspension into the lump.
  • Recurrence after a procedure is possible - Asan Medical Center notes that styes commonly resolve on their own but also commonly recur. A procedure clears the present lesion; it does not change the underlying tendency.
  • Removed tissue may be examined - particularly in adults and older patients with repeated lesions, pathology is considered alongside. That is the next section.

Whether to move sooner or later depends on where the lesion sits, whether it presses on the visual axis, and on age and ability to cooperate with a procedure. As the Japanese Ophthalmological Society notes, chalazia occur from infancy to old age, and the same size lesion is judged differently in different patients. This decision belongs in the consulting room, not on the internet.

10. When they keep coming back - meibomian glands and lid hygiene

If you get a stye every few months, the problem is rarely bad luck with bacteria. It is far more often the condition of the lid margin producing repeats. The Korean portal states that warm compresses for about 10 minutes twice a day together with lid cleansing can prevent the glands from clogging and thereby prevent styes and chalazia. Treatment and prevention being the same routine is characteristic of this condition.

The AAO describes the mechanics of lid hygiene concretely. Wet a clean washcloth with warm water, wring it out, and hold it over closed eyes for at least 1 minute. Then, using a washcloth, cotton swab, or lint-free pad soaked in baby shampoo diluted in warm water, gently scrub the base of the eyelashes for about 15 seconds. Do this daily when crusting is present.

  • Clean the base of the lashes. The target is the line the lashes emerge from, not the skin of the lid.
  • Do not scrub hard. Roughly 15 seconds of gentle motion is the whole instruction.
  • Dedicated lid cleansers exist and are an alternative if diluted baby shampoo irritates.
  • Rotate your cosmetics. Do not keep mascara and liner indefinitely, and discard anything you were using when a stye appeared.
  • Skip the waterline. That line sits on top of the meibomian gland openings.
  • Always remove makeup before bed. Residue on the lid margin is raw material for the next blockage.
  • Cut contact lens wearing time and re-audit your hygiene. A few days in glasses alone can widen the interval between episodes.
  • Look at your skin too. Seborrhoeic dermatitis and rosacea travel with blepharitis.
  • If the oil layer keeps failing, look at the whole tear film. Meibomian gland dysfunction moves together with dry eye.

One caveat: hygiene routines take time to show results. Trying it for a few days, seeing nothing, and quitting is equivalent to never having started. Twice a day sustained over several weeks beats switching methods.

11. The one thing that must be excluded - recurrent chalazion and sebaceous carcinoma

This section is not intended to frighten anyone. A lump that recurs on the eyelid is overwhelmingly likely to be just a chalazion. The problem is that the rare exception looks exactly like one.

Korea's national health information portal states explicitly that when a chalazion recurs frequently at the same site in an adult, a biopsy is performed to distinguish it from a malignancy such as sebaceous gland carcinoma. The Japanese Ophthalmological Society notes that in older adults, a lesion taken for a chalazion may contain a malignancy such as sebaceous carcinoma, and that a workup including pathology is needed when it will not clear. The MSD Manual states that chalazia that do not resolve with treatment should be biopsied to rule out eyelid cancer, and StatPearls says recurrent chalazia should be biopsied to rule out sebaceous cell carcinoma. The same recommendation appearing in four independent places is itself the measure of its weight.

The numbers explain the emphasis. According to AAO's EyeNet on eyelid sebaceous carcinoma:

  • It accounts for approximately 5 percent of malignant eyelid tumours.
  • Mean age at diagnosis runs from the late 50s to the early 70s, with most studies finding a female predominance.
  • Incidence is reported to be higher among Asian populations.
  • It is notorious for being misdiagnosed as a persistent or recurring chalazion or blepharitis, producing an average delay of one to three years.
  • An incorrect initial pathologic diagnosis has been reported in 40 to 75 percent of cases.
  • Earlier series described mortality rates of 18 to 30 percent, with more recent reports suggesting improved survival.

So the signs actually worth watching are these. Any one of them warrants an ophthalmology review.

  • It comes back in the same spot. That is different from lumps appearing at varying locations.
  • Eyelashes are falling out over the lesion. Inflammation alone does not readily do that.
  • Chronic blepharitis persists on one eyelid only. EyeNet advises considering this diagnosis in middle-aged or older patients presenting with unilateral blepharitis.
  • The architecture of the lid margin is breaking down, or the lesion is ulcerating.
  • It keeps returning to the same site despite treatment and procedures.
  • The lesion is yellowish and firm and grows over time.
  • You are middle-aged or older, in which case identical findings are weighed more heavily.

To put it plainly: the risky habit is shrugging and saying, there it is again. The disease is rare. Missing it is not.

12. Common misconceptions

  • You catch a stye by looking at someone else's - a folk belief in several countries. No infection transmits by gaze. Contact transmission via towels, cosmetics, and hands is real, so manage that instead.
  • Popping it makes it heal faster - the opposite. Both the AAO and the NHS say not to squeeze or burst it, citing spread of infection.
  • A chalazion is just an old stye - they are different conditions. One is a bacterial infection, the other a granulomatous response to trapped oil. What is common is a chalazion remaining after an acute episode passes.
  • Prophylactic antibiotic drops prevent styes - nothing supports self-directed preventive instillation. What the Korean portal offers as prevention is warm compresses and lid cleansing.
  • Folk remedies such as potato slices, salt, or tea tree oil - no clinical evidence supporting their effect on styes could be identified. Salt water and heated salt pouches carry irritation and burn risk, and essential oils should not be put into the eye.
  • An eye patch speeds healing - a patch is not treatment, and the pressure does not help.
  • A stye means you cannot use the eye - the NHS states vision should not be affected by a stye. If vision has changed, this is no longer only a stye.
  • Children get off more lightly - there is no basis for that. In a child, a lid that becomes markedly more swollen, or a child who turns listless and floppy, needs care sooner.

13. What could not be established

Items this article deliberately leaves open.

  • The optimal temperature, duration, and frequency of warm compresses - 40 to 43 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes twice daily is the Korean portal's figure, but no randomized trial fixing that value could be identified. It is also why recommendations range from 5 to 15 minutes and from 2 to 5 times a day.
  • The effectiveness of non-surgical treatment for acute internal hordeolum - the Cochrane review found no randomized trials at all. Warm compresses, lid scrubs, antibiotics, and steroids all sit in an evidence vacuum in that specific setting.
  • The spontaneous resolution rate of chalazia - the Korean portal's roughly 50% and the MSD Manual's 2 to 8 weeks are observational figures, and sources word them differently.
  • How much eye makeup or contact lens wear raises stye risk - no quantified figure could be identified. Being named as a risk factor and having a measured risk ratio are different claims.
  • Recent national statistics - the Korean figures cited here cover 2016 (released July 2017) and 2010 (released August 2011). No official analysis for later years could be identified, and no equivalent national dataset for the US or UK was located.
  • A consumer-facing clinical guideline from a national ophthalmology society - no such published guideline document was available to cite for this condition.

14. Frequently asked questions

Q. How long does a stye take to heal?

Usually 1 to 2 weeks. The NHS says most improve within a week, and StatPearls puts uncomplicated styes at 1 to 2 weeks. An external stye tends to rupture after 2 to 4 days per the MSD Manual. A chalazion runs differently: 2 to 8 weeks in the MSD Manual, with about 50% resolving under conservative care per the Korean portal. A painless lump lasting weeks is a normal course, not a failure.

Q. Is squeezing really that bad?

Yes. The force works the wrong way, driving pus into tissue planes instead of out. The AAO prohibits it on the grounds of spreading infection, and Beijing Municipal Health Commission guidance names cellulitis and cavernous sinus septic thrombosis as complications. Needles and pulling out lashes are equally off-limits. If drainage is needed, it is done by incision in a clinic.

Q. How exactly should I do a warm compress?

The Korean portal's figure is about 40 to 43 degrees Celsius, 10 minutes, twice a day. The variable that matters is heat retention: a damp cloth cools within a minute or two, so rewarm it midway. Hotter is not better, and eyelid skin burns easily. Afterwards sweep the lid margin gently, without trying to express anything.

Q. Can I reuse leftover eye drops?

No. An opened bottle can be contaminated, and a past prescription is not evidence about a present symptom. Steroid combination products are especially risky. It is also worth remembering that the benefit of topical antibiotics is not itself established evidence.

Q. It keeps recurring in the same spot. Can I leave it?

See an ophthalmologist. The Korean portal advises biopsy in adults with repeated recurrence at the same site, to exclude sebaceous gland carcinoma. Most such lumps are just chalazia, but per EyeNet, eyelid sebaceous carcinoma is misdiagnosed as chalazion often enough to produce an average one-to-three-year delay. That is more true still if lashes are falling out or chronic inflammation persists on one lid only.

Conclusion: two things are enough to remember

First, keep your hands off it. The only parts of stye management a person's hands can improve are delivering warmth and keeping the lid margin clean. Squeezing always sends infection inward. Most styes are finished in one to two weeks and chalazia shrink slowly over weeks. Nearly everything done to hurry that along actually lengthens it.

Second, two kinds of signal are not for waiting. One is loud: redness spreading onto the face, fever, pain in the eyeball or on moving the eye, reduced vision, a protruding eye, or an eye that will not move. That is no longer a disease confined to the eyelid; it needs care immediately, and in children you should act earlier on the same signs. The other is quiet. A lump that returns to the same spot, lashes that fall out, chronic blepharitis on one lid only — these get ignored longer precisely because they do not hurt.

Between those two, most styes pass with warmth, a clean lid margin, and a few days. If they keep coming back, the thing to change is not the treatment but the daily conditions: makeup habits, contact lens hours, cleansing before bed, and two warm compresses a day.

Medical disclaimer, once again
This article is general health information and does not replace diagnosis, prescription, or treatment. Do not squeeze a stye or lance it with a needle. Do not use antibiotic eye drops or ointment without a prescription, and do not reuse leftover medication.
