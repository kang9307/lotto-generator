Covered Call Strategy Basics and How to Optimize the Payoff

Selling upside for cash - what the numbers actually leave you with

Introduction: What the phrase 1 percent a month quietly leaves out

While deposit rates sit in the low single digits, the phrase monthly distribution has become close to a magic word among retail investors, particularly in Korea. Cash lands in the account every month and the annualized distribution yield prints in double digits. The engine that manufactures much of that cash is an option strategy called the covered call. Products built on it have exploded in size. According to research from the Korea Capital Market Institute, net assets of covered call ETFs listed in Korea grew from 122.3 billion won at the end of 2022 to 24.55 trillion won as of the end of May 2026, while the number of funds rose from 6 to 50. That is roughly a two hundredfold increase in three and a half years.

The problem is that the language used to sell these products focuses almost entirely on the money you receive. Yields of 12, 15 or 20 percent are printed large, while the source of that money and the price paid for it are printed small. A covered call is not a free cash machine. It is a trade that sells the right to profit from a large rise and converts it into cash today. Something has to be sold for the money to arrive, and what is being sold is the upside of your own portfolio.

This article works through two layers. The first is the payoff structure of the strategy itself, presented purely in tables and numbers so you can see exactly where profit is cut off and where loss remains. The second is the covered call as a packaged product, meaning the high-yield ETF. Why distributions differ from returns, what the net asset value erosion debate actually describes, and how taxation splits depending on where a fund is listed. Finally it states plainly who should not use this strategy. The goal is neither to promote covered calls nor to condemn them, but to break the assumption that a high yield automatically means a good product.

1. The structure - what you buy and what you sell

1.1 Two positions combined into one strategy

A covered call holds two positions at once.

  • Long the underlying: you actually own the stock or ETF. In standard listed options markets one contract typically corresponds to 100 shares.
  • Short a call: you sell someone else the right to buy that asset at a set price, the strike, and receive an option premium in cash.

Selling a call by itself, a naked call, carries theoretically unlimited loss if the stock soars. But if you already own the shares, exercise simply means delivering shares you already hold. The risk is covered, hence the name. Note carefully that covered does not mean protected from loss. It is a technical term meaning the upside risk is covered. The downside is not covered at all, and that is the single most common misunderstanding among beginners.

1.2 Where the premium comes from

An option premium splits into intrinsic value and time value. If the strike sits below the current price the option already has exercise value, and on top of that sits time value reflecting the remaining life of the contract and expected volatility. The covered call seller harvests time decay as time value bleeds away. The price of that harvest is handing any sharp rally to the option buyer.

The key point is that a premium is not newly created wealth. Option prices already embed the market's expectation of how much the underlying will move, expressed as implied volatility. A fat premium therefore means the market expects large moves, and large moves include large declines as well as large rallies. Saying you found a stock with generous premiums overlaps heavily with saying you found a risky stock.

1.3 Three formulas

At expiration the payoff reduces to three expressions, simplified here by ignoring commissions, taxes and dividends.

  • Maximum profit = (strike price − purchase price) + premium received, fixed once the underlying trades above the strike.
  • Breakeven = purchase price − premium received, effectively a slightly lower entry cost.
  • Maximum loss = (purchase price − premium received) × number of shares, realized in the extreme case where the underlying goes to zero.

Three lines are enough to expose the shape. There is a ceiling above and almost no floor below. The premium is not a shield against decline. It merely moves the starting line of the loss slightly lower.

2. Breaking the payoff into numbers

2.1 The example setup

To see the structure precisely, here is a hypothetical case. It exists to demonstrate arithmetic, not to recommend any security.

  • Underlying: hypothetical stock A, currently 100,000 won per share
  • Position: 100 shares, a purchase of 10 million won
  • Option sold: one call contract, one month to expiry, strike 105,000 won
  • Premium received: 2,000 won per share, 200,000 won total, or 2.0 percent of the position

Plugging into the formulas, breakeven is 98,000 won, maximum profit is 7,000 won per share or 700,000 won in total, and maximum loss is 9.8 million won. That maximum profit is 7.0 percent of the 10 million won invested, and it can arrive within a single month. The catch is that not a single won beyond it can ever be collected.

Read the table in three zones.

  • Below the strike (70,000 to 105,000 won): the covered call is always ahead by the 200,000 won premium. This is the entire reason the strategy exists.
  • Above the strike (110,000 won and up): the covered call is nailed to 700,000 won while the gap widens without limit. At plus 40 percent it forfeits 3.3 million won.
  • In a crash: at minus 30 percent the covered call still loses 2.8 million won. The 200,000 won premium offset only 6.7 percent of the damage.

One point deserves emphasis. The premium is the same fixed amount regardless of what the stock does, while the upside surrendered grows without limit as the stock rises. Gain fixed, opportunity cost variable. The moment someone multiplies 2 percent a month by twelve and calls it 24 percent a year, that asymmetry has already been ignored.

2.3 The annualization trap

Multiplying a 2 percent monthly premium by twelve produces 24 percent a year. For that to hold, every one of the following must be true for twelve consecutive months.

  • The same premium level must remain available each month, yet premiums shrink when volatility falls.
  • The price must stay below the strike each month, because being called away triggers re-entry costs.
  • The underlying price must hold or rise, because a decline costs more than the premium earns.
  • Commissions, slippage and taxes must not eat the difference.

The odds of all four holding simultaneously for a full year are low. That is why covered call performance must be judged on total return, meaning distributions plus the change in net asset value, rather than on premium income. Applying that single rule changes how most product marketing reads.

3. The four dials of optimization

There are effectively four variables you can adjust. Optimization means turning these dials to match the market regime and your own objective, not squeezing out the largest possible premium.

3.1 Dial one - strike selection

The closer the strike sits to the current price, the larger the premium and the higher the probability the shares are called away.

This is precisely why products advertising the highest yields tend to write strikes near the money. Extracting the maximum premium requires selling almost all the upside. A high distribution yield is a signal that more upside was sold, not a signal of superior management.

3.2 Dial two - expiry selection

Shorter expiries decay faster and turn over more often, but each round collects a smaller premium while commissions and spread costs accumulate across more trades. That turnover is exactly what weekly and daily option strategies are chasing. Longer expiries collect more per trade but keep the upside locked up for longer, and time decay is slower than in the final stretch before expiry. Practitioners often cite the 30 to 45 day window as a conventional compromise, but that is a rule of thumb rather than an answer.

3.3 Dial three - the volatility regime

Premiums scale roughly with implied volatility, so in theory you should sell when volatility is high and stand aside when it is low. In practice, volatility spikes when markets are falling or bracing to fall. Writing calls at that moment collects a fat premium while the underlying may lose far more. The rule that bigger premiums mean bigger risk holds without exception.

3.4 Dial four - rolling and assignment

As expiry approaches there are three broad choices.

  • Let it expire: if the price sits below the strike the option dies worthless and the premium is kept in full. The simplest outcome.
  • Accept assignment: above the strike the shares are sold at the strike. Fine if that was a planned exit, costly in fees and taxes if you meant to keep the position.
  • Roll: buy back the existing call and sell a later expiry, rolling out, or a higher strike, rolling up. This can recover some upside, but the buyback can cost more than the new premium brings in, so rolling is not automatically profitable.

American style options can also be exercised early. In particular, early exercise can occur just before an ex-dividend date when the buyer wants the dividend, so dividend calendars and option expiries need to be managed together. Many investors buy an ETF specifically to avoid this operational burden and pay a management fee for the privilege.

4. Which market conditions help and which hurt

A covered call is not a direction neutral tool. It is a bet with an explicit market view, namely that the underlying will not rise sharply.

The last row hurts most in practice. You absorb the full drawdown, then fail to participate in the recovery because the calls cap you. Entering the next cycle with a lower base and no recovery means premiums keep arriving while capital never rebuilds. That is the structural background to the erosion discussed next.

5. Selling calls yourself versus buying a covered call ETF

5.1 Differences in execution

  • Selling directly: requires a derivatives-enabled account and, in many markets, prerequisite training or simulated trading, plus hands-on margin management, expiry handling and rolling. In return you choose your own strike and expiry and pay no management fee.
  • Buying an ETF: purchasable in a standard brokerage account in small size, with the manager handling all execution. In return you pay a management fee and cannot choose the strike, expiry or the share of the portfolio written against.

Product design has fragmented quickly. Beyond the traditional monthly-option structure there are now weekly options, target-premium formats and variable write-ratio designs, with underlyings ranging from domestic indices to major overseas benchmarks. The result is that a fund name no longer tells you what is inside, which is itself a risk factor.

6. The NAV erosion debate around high-yield covered call ETFs

6.1 A distribution is not a return

Here is the most important sentence in this article. A distribution is a cash flow, not a return. Bank interest accrues on top of untouched principal, but an ETF distribution is money leaving the fund's net assets. Immediately after a distribution, net asset value falls by the amount paid. Receiving a distribution therefore tells you nothing about whether you made money. To know that, you must look at distributions plus the change in net asset value.

Research from the Korea Capital Market Institute notes that covered call ETF distributions can include not only option premiums but also dividends, realized gains and return of capital, and that the basis for calculating an advertised yield varies by product. Crucially, if net asset value falls further than the amount distributed, total return is negative. That is the precise definition of the erosion problem: receiving your own capital back while it feels like income.

6.2 Why erosion is structural rather than accidental

Erosion is closer to a mathematical consequence of the strategy than a management failure. In sequence:

  1. In rising months the short calls mean the fund fails to keep up with the gain.
  2. In falling months it absorbs nearly the full decline, cushioned only by the premium.
  3. Repeated cycles therefore push net asset value step by step below the underlying index.
  4. If a target distribution rate is held fixed while assets shrink, the shortfall must be funded out of capital.
  5. Smaller assets generate smaller premiums next month. The loop reinforces itself.

Overseas products with long track records have made this visible. Analyses of a well-known fund that writes at-the-money calls on 100 percent of a Nasdaq 100 portfolio every month have repeatedly found that net asset value declined by several percent a year on average since inception, even while it paid double-digit distributions. Funds that write against a smaller share of the portfolio and blend in other income sources have generally defended net asset value better. In short, two funds can both be covered call ETFs and end up very far apart depending on how much upside each sold.

6.3 The cost of missing a bull market

Major covered call ETFs have repeatedly trailed their indices badly in strong markets. In 2023, when the S&P 500 rose sharply, the total return of a leading premium income fund came in at well under half the index, a comparison that circulated widely. Analyses of the at-the-money Nasdaq 100 writer also show a substantial gap against the underlying index on a five year annualized basis, based on overseas data through the first half of 2026. That gap is not a failure of execution. It is a designed cost incurred because the upside was sold in the first place.

The implication matters. Covered call ETF performance should be compared against simply holding the same index, not against deposit rates or bond coupons. Saying the fund beats a savings account places two products with entirely different risk profiles side by side.

6.4 What the regulator flagged

Korea's Financial Supervisory Service issued a consumer alert on 28 July 2024 regarding covered call ETFs. Its main points were as follows.

  • A distribution rate printed in a fund name is a target set by the manager, not a contractually fixed payout.
  • The payoff is asymmetric: gains are capped when the underlying rises while losses pass through when it falls.
  • If the underlying falls far enough, principal loss is possible.
  • The word premium in a fund name refers to the option premium, not to a premium quality product.

Following this, numeric target-premium figures in fund names were revised because they could be mistaken for guaranteed returns, and target-style naming replaced them. A name change, however, does not change the structure. A target remains a target.

The practically decisive row is whether income counts toward comprehensive financial income taxation. Once annual financial income, meaning interest plus dividends, exceeds 20 million won, it is aggregated with other income and taxed at progressive rates. Covered call ETFs generate distributions every month by design, so financial income accumulates quickly. A Korea-listed fund holding overseas assets also books trading gains as dividend income, making the threshold easier to breach. Conversely, capital gains on overseas-listed ETFs are taxed separately and stay out of the aggregation, which can help investors already in higher brackets.

A few additional points are worth knowing.

  • Foreign dividends are withheld at source first, and double taxation is relieved through a foreign tax credit. The procedure for fund-related foreign tax credits recently shifted toward taxpayers filing the claim themselves, so check your broker's guidance.
  • Part of a US-listed ETF's distribution may be classified as return of capital. That is an accounting classification under US rules, and its treatment under Korean tax law needs separate confirmation.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts such as ISA, pension savings and IRP can defer taxation or lower the rate, subject to limits on eligible products and withdrawal conditions.
  • Without comparing after-tax outcomes, the illusion that a higher yield is simply better survives intact. The benchmark must be after-tax total return, not headline distribution yield.

8. Who should not use this strategy

Covered calls are a reasonable tool under specific conditions. If any of the following describes you, reconsider.

  • Investors targeting long term capital growth: repeatedly selling upside keeps clipping the top of compounding. Over twenty years or more that cost accumulates.
  • Investors who never want to part with the underlying: assignment means the shares go at the strike, with repurchase costs and taxes to follow.
  • Investors who mistake distributions for interest: there is no protected principal here. Treating it like a deposit delays reaction when losses arrive.
  • Investors funding all living expenses from distributions: payouts are not guaranteed and can be cut. Essential spending should rest on certain income.
  • Investors using money needed soon: housing deposits, tuition, or anything required within one to two years does not belong in a product that can lose principal.
  • Investors holding the underlying on margin or leverage: forced liquidation risk stacking on option exposure becomes very hard to control.
  • Investors unfamiliar with assignment, margin and rolling: if selling directly, understand expiry handling and margin requirements before starting.
  • Investors applying this to extremely volatile single stocks: premiums are large, but the risk of a collapse in the underlying overwhelms them.
  • Investors concentrating most of their assets in one product: an income strategy only makes sense as one slice of a diversified whole.

9. A checklist before you act

Whether selling calls directly or buying an ETF, you are ready to proceed only when you can answer all of the following.

  1. Can you write down the maximum profit, maximum loss and breakeven as numbers?
  2. Could you absorb the loss if the underlying fell 30 percent?
  3. Could you accept the forgone gain if the underlying rose 40 percent?
  4. Do you know exactly what the underlying is, index or single stock, domestic or overseas?
  5. Have you checked whether the write ratio is 100 percent or partial?
  6. Have you checked whether the option cycle is monthly, weekly or daily?
  7. Have you checked whether disclosed distribution sources include return of capital?
  8. Have you looked at one and three year total return rather than the distribution yield?
  9. Have you compared it with simply holding the underlying index over the same period?
  10. Have you subtracted management fees and other costs from that total return?
  11. Is your annual financial income likely to exceed the aggregation threshold?
  12. Does the decision still hold once recalculated after tax?
  13. Have you considered tax-advantaged accounts where available?
  14. Do you understand the effect of currency moves and whether the fund hedges?
  15. Have you set a maximum weight for this strategy within your total assets?
  16. Does your plan survive if the distribution is cut in half?
  17. Have you defined when you would exit in a losing phase?

10. Common misconceptions

  • Covered calls are a safe strategy - no. Downside exposure is nearly identical to holding the asset outright. The cushion is one premium deep.
  • A higher distribution yield means a better product - no. It signals that more upside was sold, and it is separate from total return.
  • Monthly distributions mean monthly profits - no. Distributions leave the fund's assets and net asset value falls accordingly.
  • Premium in the name means a premium product - no. This is the exact misunderstanding the regulator called out. It is an options term.
  • A target distribution rate is a promised return - no. It is a target, and it may not be met.
  • It falls less than the index, so it is defensive - only partly. The cushion is limited in a crash and it lags badly in the rebound.
  • Holding it in a retirement account is always better - it depends on account type and withdrawal timing. Check eligibility and rules first.

Conclusion: Ask whether it fits your purpose, not whether it is good

A covered call is neither a scam nor magic. It is an explicit trade exchanging upside for cash premium, with clearly favourable regimes, sideways and mildly declining markets, and clearly unfavourable ones, strong rallies and crash-then-rebound sequences. The problem is not the strategy but the habit of describing that exchange as a product that pays interest while leaving principal untouched. Remembering that a larger yield means more upside sold dissolves most of the confusion.

So the decision rule compresses into two lines. First, judge on total return, not distribution yield. Second, compare against simply holding the same underlying, not against a savings account. If the case still stands after both tests, size it within a share of your assets you can afford to lose, verify the underlying, the write ratio and the expiry structure, and recompute after tax. Markets hand nobody free cash flow. The money arriving each month from a covered call is payment for future gains you already sold. Deciding whether that price is fair is the investor's job.
