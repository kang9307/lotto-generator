This article is based on the figures in a Green Mountain Power (GMP) press release and in the coverage that repeats them: Electrek (2026-07-28) and the Concord Monitor (2026-09-29). Numbers absent from those sources are not invented, and anything computed from two source values is labeled as a calculation. Savings and closure effects are estimates by the operator, so each is given with its attribution.

The published numbers — 110 MW and 53 MW

In July 2026 Green Mountain Power (GMP), a utility in the US state of Vermont, said its virtual power plant (VPP) had become the largest single power resource in Vermont, at 110 MW of capacity. A virtual power plant is not a plant built in one place. It is a way of operating batteries, electric vehicle chargers, hydroelectric assets, commercial buildings and other equipment scattered across many sites as if they were a single plant, coordinated by software. The largest block in that bundle is the battery mounted on the wall of a home. Electrek reports that home batteries account for 53 MW, and that more than 5,000 customers have enrolled more than 10,000 batteries.

Because the numbers overlap across several sources, they are collected in one table first. The third column is the rule of this whole article: every figure carries the name of whoever said it, and the fourth column carries what that figure does not tell the reader.

What stands out in this case is the structure, more than the scale. A home battery is normally installed to protect a house during an outage. The utility draws on the spare capacity of that battery during peak hours, and the customer keeps a reserve for outages. One piece of equipment carries two purposes, and software arbitrates between them. The sections below read this structure through four questions. On what basis is it the largest? How do 110 and 53 fit together? What actually discharges at peak? And who saves money?

The basis of the largest resource claim — capacity, not generation

The sources describe the virtual power plant as the largest single power resource in Vermont by capacity. The qualifier matters. Power equipment can be compared on two main bases, and they answer different questions.

  • Capacity (MW). How much power can be delivered at one moment. It measures the ability to fill the shortfall when the grid is stretched at peak. The 110 MW figure is on this basis.
  • Energy generated (MWh). How much electricity was produced over a period. Ranked by annual generation, equipment that runs continuously, such as hydro or nuclear, comes first. The sources do not claim a ranking on this basis.
  • The nature of a battery. A battery does not produce electricity. It releases electricity stored earlier. Two batteries with the same MW differ in how many hours they can sustain it, and the sources do not state the discharge duration.
  • The condition on largest. It is the sum when bundled as one resource. No single battery is large; the total of thousands of households reaches 110 MW.

So the sentence that home batteries replaced a power plant has to be read precisely. What the sources say is that, in output capacity at peak hours, this bundle is larger than any other single resource in Vermont. It does not mean batteries substitute for the baseload power that supplies the grid around the clock. This distinction is the first to blur when the case is carried over to another grid, so section 8 returns to it.

The relation between capacity and stored energy is easiest to see with an illustrative assumption. If a battery bundle can deliver 100 MW for four hours, its stored energy is 400 MWh, and if the same bundle can deliver for one hour only, it is 100 MWh. The output capacity in MW is the same, yet the time the grid is supported differs by a factor of four. These numbers are assumptions for understanding and are not GMP actual values. Because the sources give no discharge duration, how many hours of the peak the 110 MW can fill cannot be calculated from them. The phrase largest resource therefore speaks to the size of the output and does not guarantee how long that output lasts.

110 versus 53 — where more than half and the arithmetic disagree

There is a place where the figures in the sources collide. Electrek writes that of the 110 MW total, 53 MW, described as more than half, comes from residential battery systems. The summary of the GMP release says residential storage is over 50% of the virtual power plant and gives the installed home battery capacity as 53 MW. But 53 divided by 110 is about 48.2%. That is short of half.

The cause of the mismatch is not in the sources. Several explanations are possible. The 110 MW total and the 53 MW home figure may come from tallies at different dates, the category of home storage might include home resources other than batteries, or the wording may simply have been loose. None of these can be settled from the sources. This article therefore keeps two rules. The 53 MW and 110 MW are quoted as written and attributed, and more than half is identified as the sources wording. The 48% is marked as this article calculation. No new figure is created by correcting either side.

Such a mismatch is not unusual in practice. A press release is a promotional summary, the coverage repeats that summary, and the denominator can change along the way. A reader can do three things: see how many sources give the same figure independently, divide the numerator by the denominator when a ratio appears, and, when they disagree, record the disagreement itself without declaring either side right.

What discharges at peak

The operation of a virtual power plant can be put in three steps. First, the battery in a customer home sits charged according to the customer own purpose. Second, as grid demand approaches its peak, the utility software instructs the enrolled batteries to discharge together. Third, the combined output enters the grid and reduces the power that would otherwise have had to be purchased or generated at that hour. The sources summarize the flow as the grid drawing on stored power in customer batteries when needed, while leaving the customer a reserve for outages.

  • What discharges. Batteries installed in customer homes. According to the sources, more than 5,000 customers enrolled more than 10,000 of them.
  • The discharge command. The utility control software, described as coordinating batteries, solar, electric vehicles and demand response together.
  • Preserving the customer share. The sources explain that the reserve for outages stays with the customer. The size and basis of that reserve are not given.
  • Result. About 90 MW was discharged in the early July heat wave, a scale GMP describes as similar in effect to taking 50,000 homes off the grid. That conversion is GMP wording.
  • Side uses. The Concord Monitor notes that home batteries also serve as backup power and move daytime solar output into the evening.

A design principle shows here. This model does not build new equipment; it uses the idle hours of equipment already installed. A home battery in a house with no outage on most days does little but hold stored electricity. Putting that slack to work in the few most expensive hours of the grid is the core idea. The weakness of the model sits in the same place. Heat wave peaks can coincide with periods when outage risk is higher, and the balance between what the utility draws and what the customer reserves then becomes a question of trust. The sources do not publish figures on how that balance is run. For the customer, the quality of this model shows itself not on ordinary days but on the day the reserve is needed.

Who saves — attribution of the $6 million

GMP said the virtual power plant cut about $6 million from all customers peak electricity costs during the early July heat wave. The Concord Monitor quotes it as cutting an estimated $6 million from all customers peak electricity costs. Three words decide the structure of that sentence.

  • Estimated. This is not a figure totaled from actual bills. GMP calculated the reduction in peak electricity costs. The summary of GMP material describes the method only as an estimate of reduced peak energy costs during the heat event.
  • All customers. The explanation is that the benefit is not limited to households with batteries but applies to the electricity costs of all GMP customers in Vermont. That the benefit of the virtual power plant is shared beyond participants is the outward logic of this program.
  • Peak. It is the cost in the hours when heat drove demand up, not across all hours of the year. The $6 million cannot be read as an annual saving. The previous year $11 million is reported separately, and whether it uses the same method is not stated.
  • No comparison case. It is a counterfactual, how much more would have been paid without the virtual power plant, so it is hard to verify independently. The baseline sits inside GMP.

In general, a utility has to buy more expensive power or run reserve equipment in hours when demand is concentrated. That general principle is widely used to explain power markets, but the sources do not say which items the GMP $6 million came from, whether market purchases, capacity charges or transmission related costs. This article therefore does not guess at the items. What the sources say stops at the point that it is an estimate and that it accrues to all customers.

One more point concerns how gains and costs are distributed. The customer with a battery pays a lease and gets a reserve for outages, while all customers get the indirect benefit of lower peak costs. How the cost of the lease business is reflected in rates, and whether participating customers receive separate compensation, are not in the sources. Benefit to everyone is GMP wording, and the details of the distribution are not closed by the published material.

A path to verifying the estimate can also be stated. If the baseline price for each peak hour, the hourly discharge of the enrolled batteries and the purchase avoided as a result were published, a third party could recompute the figure. Without those three, the $6 million remains a number to be read with its attribution. A claim of savings becomes stronger not because it is larger but because the reader can rebuild it from disclosed inputs.

Two peaker plants — what is closed and what is planned

A peaker plant is a plant kept to run only in the short hours when demand is highest. Because its hours are few but it must ramp quickly, a battery bundle becomes a competitor if it can take over the same role as output capacity. The facts about closures that can be confirmed in the sources are as follows.

  • Confirmed. GMP permanently closed two peaker plants, in Vergennes and Rutland. The release says only that this happened in the last few years.
  • Planned. There is wording about plans to close more peaker plants. Which plants and when are not in the sources.
  • Boundary of interpretation. The sentence that credits the closures to the virtual power plant is GMP explanation, and whether other reasons such as equipment age or economics were also involved cannot be settled from the sources. The summary of the BBC Future article on GeekNews says equipment at the end of its life is being closed in turn, which suggests an age factor in the closures.

This article therefore does not write the causal sentence that home batteries made power plants disappear. The sentence that can be written stops at this: GMP closed two plants and presents the virtual power plant as a resource that takes over this role. Any further claim is outside the sources.

It is also worth noting which records would be needed to separate the causes of a closure: the construction and maintenance history of the closed equipment, the demand forecast and the capacity of replacement resources at the time of the decision, and the operating record of peak hours before and after the closure. Only when these three are published can equipment age be separated from the effect of the replacement resource. The sources contain none of these records, so the closure is written as a fact and the cause is left open. The same standard can be used to read any later news of further closures.

Lease prices can change with time and program, and this article introduces the terms without recommending any purchase or lease. What the table is for is the structure, not the amounts. The customer hands part of the right to use the home battery to the utility and in return gets the equipment and a reserve. One can read the exchange as holding because the utility calculates that bundling batteries already installed is cheaper than building new generation, but that cost comparison itself is not in the sources.

The GeekNews discussion mixes in practitioner opinions. Comments raising fairness of cost allocation, responsibility that follows the utility control and privacy sit next to comments from participants who are satisfied. Comments are personal claims and not verified fact, so this article does not use them as evidence. Still, the fact that such issues are raised comes from the structure. When ownership and control of equipment are separated, who is responsible for what has to be fixed in a contract document. The reader can therefore treat the list of unpublished items in the table above as the list of questions a contract or a tariff document would need to answer.

Criteria for reading the case elsewhere

Three criteria are needed when carrying the Vermont case into the story of another grid. First, the problem this model answers is the output shortfall of a few peak hours, not the whole annual power supply. Second, the scale figures are evidence of structure and do not guarantee results in another region. Third, the figures are operator announcements, so they are read without stripping the attribution.

  • Ask for capacity and duration together. MW alone does not say how many hours can be sustained. The sources do not publish the duration.
  • Ask for the basis of the reserve. The rule for the situation where the customer share and the utility share compete decides trust.
  • Ask for the formula of the savings. An estimate varies greatly with the baseline, so whether the baseline and items are published matters.
  • Separate the cause of closures. Causation cannot be written without separating equipment age, economics and the effect of the replacement resource.
  • Check the premise of the institution. This case is a structure in which a Vermont utility runs a battery lease business itself. The same structure may not hold under other systems.
  • Comparison with the Korean system is outside this article. Korean power market and virtual power plant rules should be covered only as confirmed from official sources such as the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy, KEPCO and the Korea Power Exchange, and this article did not make that check.

The broad energy transition is covered in the post wind and solar first overtake gas, the general cost structure of putting solar on a home in the home solar installation guide, and how a bill is computed from peak and tiered structure in the electricity bill saving guide. This article does not explain those axes again and reads only one structure: bundling batteries into a grid resource.

What could not be verified

  • The cause of the gap between the more than half wording for 53 MW of 110 MW and the calculated 48%. The tally date and the definition of the denominator are not in the sources.
  • The formula and items behind the $6 million savings. GMP calls it only an estimate, and there is no independent verification material.
  • Discharge duration, annual number of discharges and the customer reserve share. None of the three reports relied on here states these values.
  • Whether participating customers receive compensation other than the lease, and how the cost of the lease business is reflected in rates.
  • The exact timing of the peaker closures, the targets of further closures, and the share of the virtual power plant among the causes of closure.
  • The BBC Future original. The GeekNews page lists it as the source, but this article could not open it to verify.
  • The current state of the Korean power market and virtual power plant rules. No official source was checked, so no comparison is made.
