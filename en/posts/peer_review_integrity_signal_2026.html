What Peer Review Was Never Built to Catch - What Reviewed by Peers Does and Does Not Guarantee

Review was not designed as a fraud detector. So what should a reader look at instead?

2026-08-02 Tech & IT

Introduction: before one paper decides something

In a technology selection meeting someone once proposed adopting a particular approach. The evidence was a single paper, and the presenter summarised it in one sentence. «It was accepted at a conference.» Nobody objected. I did not object either.

Months later, when the approach did not behave the way we expected in our environment, I opened the paper again and learned several things. The reported figures came from hardware of a different order of magnitude. The comparison target was an older approach we no longer used. The link to the reproduction code was dead. None of this had been hidden. It was all printed in the paper. The problem was that I had read the phrase «it was accepted» and stopped reading there.

That is where this article starts. What exactly does the sentence «it went through peer review» guarantee? And what does it not guarantee? The question is more urgent for people outside academia who use papers as evidence than for those inside it. We never see the inside of the review process, so it is easy to read the signal «accepted» as far more than it is.

The stance of this article. This is not a piece criticising academia, and it does not single out any conference or any researcher. The purpose is one thing only. To understand precisely what the review system was built to do. Blaming an institution for failing at something it was never designed to do produces a wrong diagnosis, and a wrong diagnosis produces the wrong response from the reader too.

1. What I confirmed and what I could not

This section comes first. If I do not say up front what this article rests on, the article becomes exactly the kind of writing it warns against.

The starting point was a matter known through reporting. It concerned a public write-up by someone who had served as a reviewer, and it was described as involving a manuscript that disclosed in its own body text that its author list contained names of people who do not exist, and that was nonetheless accepted.

I could not open that original write-up. Access was blocked and I could not read the text. So this article follows these rules.

  • No counts, no percentages, no venue names, no authors, no reviewers are stated as fact here. The rule on this blog is that the contents of a document I could not open are never quoted, and I am not making an exception this time
  • The matter is referred to only as «a topic known through reporting», and the body of the article is built as structural analysis grounded in documents that can be verified
  • You therefore should not read this article as a judgement about the review standards of any particular venue. There is no basis for such a judgement here

What I did open and read directly is the following. All of them are official guidance published without a login.

  • The authorship definition published by the international committee of medical journal editors, covering the four requirements and who bears responsibility for judging them
  • A large conference's published reviewer guidelines, covering what reviewers are asked to evaluate and on what schedule
  • The same conference's call for papers, covering author list changes, the status of code and data submission, and duplicate submission
  • The same conference's policy on large language model use, covering the scope of author responsibility and the constraints on reviewers
  • The same conference's code of ethics, covering the categories of concern that can be raised in review
  • An open science organisation's explanation of registered reports, covering the two-stage structure and the problem it targets
  • A science editors body's authorship responsibility guidance, covering the definitions of ghost, gift and guest authorship

I also state what I could not open. A publication ethics organisation's guidelines for peer reviewers, a large computing society's definitions of its reproducibility badges, and the starting write-up mentioned above. Access to all three was blocked and I could not read the text, so this article does not describe the contents of those three documents. Section 12 collects this again.

Why one conference's documents are cited so often. Below I quote one conference's policy text repeatedly. This is not because that conference is the problem. Quite the opposite. Not many venues publish their review rules, their authorship rules and their language model policy in full without a login, and to quote something you have to actually be able to open it. Publishing it so that it can be quoted is itself good practice.

2. What review was designed to verify

One misconception has to go first. Peer review is not a fraud detector. Not because the institution failed at that, but because it was never designed for it.

Open published reviewer guidelines and the axes a reviewer is asked to judge are listed plainly. Technical soundness, clarity of the writing, significance to the field, and originality. The same document notes that originality does not necessarily require introducing an entirely new method, and that providing novel insight through evaluation of existing approaches also counts.

Compress the four axes into one line and you get this. «Does the claim the authors made hold, on the evidence the authors supplied, to the degree the authors said?» That question presumes a cooperative document. It weighs the quality of an argument on the assumption that the authors did not lie.

2.1 What is absent from the guidelines

No matter how carefully you read those guidelines, the following items do not appear. Their absence is the core of this article.

ItemWhat review was designed to doWhat it was not designed to doBasis I checked
ArgumentJudge the fit between claim and evidence—The four evaluation axes in the reviewer guidelines
WritingJudge clarity and organisation—The four evaluation axes in the reviewer guidelines
ContributionJudge significance and originality—The four evaluation axes in the reviewer guidelines
Author existence—No identity check existsThe authorship document assigns responsibility to authors
Reference existence—No duty to confirmThe language model policy puts the duty on authors
Reproduction—No duty to reproduceCode submission is encouraged, running it is a hazard notice
Data existence—No raw data cross-checkAbsent from the guidelines entirely
Duplicate submissionDesk rejection on violationDetection depends on reports and luckThe duplicate submission clause in the call for papers

Read the right-hand column again. Almost everything needed to catch fraud sits on the right. No document asks reviewers to do that work, and neither the authority nor the resources for it are allocated.

The treatment of code compresses this character perfectly. The guidelines warn that just like any other untrusted code, any submitted code may contain security vulnerabilities, and recommend handling it in a sandboxed environment. The implication is unmistakable. Running the code is treated as a risk the reviewer absorbs, not as a verification step. A verification step does not come with that sentence attached.

3. Nobody verifies the author list

The author list is the most visible piece of information on a paper and simultaneously the least verified. That is not a guess, it is written in the guidance.

The authorship document from the international committee of medical journal editors requires all four conditions to be met. Substantial contribution to conception or design, or to acquisition, analysis or interpretation of data. Drafting or critically revising the work for important intellectual content. Final approval of the version to be published. And agreement to be accountable for all aspects, ensuring that questions about accuracy or integrity are appropriately investigated and resolved.

The same document then nails down who decides. In its own words, it is the collective responsibility of the authors, not the journal to which the work is submitted, to determine that all people named as authors meet all four criteria. And further, it is not the role of journal editors to determine who qualifies or does not qualify for authorship or to arbitrate authorship conflicts.

Put those two sentences side by side and the structure appears. The criteria exist, and no external party is charged with confirming compliance. The author list is not something checked, it is something declared. It has the shape of a tax filing, with one difference. Tax filings have an auditing body behind them, and author lists have no standing equivalent.

3.1 Ghost, gift and guest

A science editors body's authorship guidance names three distortions. All three are problems independent of whether the name is real, which makes them harder to detect.

  • Guest authorship. A name added on the expectation that it will improve the chances of publication, with no actual contribution
  • Gift or honorary authorship. A name granted on a tenuous affiliation with the study, such as leadership of a department
  • Ghost authorship. Someone who did participate in the research, the analysis or the writing but is neither named nor disclosed
  • The same guidance says editors should require authors and those acknowledged to identify their contributions, and should be aware of changes made in the author byline during review. That is monitoring, not verification

Conference rules have the same character. One call for papers states that after the abstract deadline, adding or removing authors is not allowed, and listed authors can potentially be reordered before the submission deadline. The purpose of that clause is not to confirm the list, it is to freeze it. Freezing makes later manipulation harder, but it never asks whether the first list was true.

  • Affiliation is self-reported. No standard step queries the institution
  • An email address is confirmed only by deliverability. Delivery proves the address is live, not that the person is who they say
  • Even where a contribution statement exists, there is no way to compare the statement against reality
  • Under anonymous review the reviewer cannot see the authors at all, so author verification sits structurally outside the review stage

4. What it means when a self-report gets through

Here I treat only the structural meaning of the matter that prompted this article. Since I could not confirm the event, what follows should be read in the conditional.

Suppose a manuscript stated in its own body text that its author list contained names of people who do not exist, and was accepted anyway. What would we learn?

It is easier to start with what we would not learn.

  • We would not learn that a reviewer was dishonest. Missing a self-report and turning a blind eye to fraud are different events
  • We would learn nothing about the research standards of the field. The sample is effectively nil
  • We would not learn that the review system has collapsed. One case is one case

There is one thing we would learn. A reviewer's attention is not distributed evenly across a manuscript. This is not surprising, and in fact it is true of all document review. Attention concentrates on particular places.

  • Abstract and conclusion. First establish what is being claimed
  • Tables and figures. Check whether the numbers support the claim
  • The baseline. What it was compared against is half the evaluation
  • The method section. Check whether it is written at a reproducible level of detail
  • Related work. Check whether anything from one's own corner is missing

What is not on that list is the blind spot. Acknowledgements, funding statements, affiliations, the tail of the appendix, and any unexpected sentence in the middle of the body. Finding fraud is the work of noticing the thing you were not looking for, whereas review is the work of evaluating the thing that was claimed. Those two tasks require different training and a different way of reading.

So the lesson is not «the reviewer was lazy» but «reviewing is a different kind of task from fraud detection». Only the second reading produces a response. The first reading leads only to pressing people harder, and as the next section shows, there is almost no slack left to press.

5. The reviewer's conditions - unpaid, deadlined, uneven

Before blaming reviewers it is worth seeing the conditions they work under. Only what is verifiable in public documents is listed here.

ConditionRealityWhat fraud detection needsThe gap
CompensationUnpaid volunteering is the normPayment for additional investigation timeThere is nobody to bill
WindowRoughly five weeks in one published scheduleIterative work for citation checks and reproductionIt overlaps with a day job
LoadOne person handles several papersDeep reading per paperDepth trades against count
ExpertiseAssigned by keywords and bidding, so coverage variesDetailed knowledge of the exact subfieldAdjacent-field assignment is common
AuthoritySees only the manuscript and appendixInstitutional queries, raw data requests, identity checksThe authority does not exist
ToolingConfidentiality rules restrict outside toolsAutomated checks for bulk cross-referencingThe rule and the need conflict
SanctionsLateness or low quality can restrict access to reviews of one's own papersA reward for accuracySlowness is punished, depth is not rewarded

The last row matters most. One set of reviewer guidelines states in effect that if your reviews are late or not of sufficient quality, you could lose access to the reviews on your own co-authored papers. A mechanism like that is effective at getting reviews submitted, but it does not push in the direction of digging deeper. Digging deeper only increases deadline risk.

Not blaming a structure on people. Not one row of that table is a gap an individual can close through conscientiousness. Expecting investigator work from an unpaid person is a hope, not a design. Any serious talk of improvement has to actually change at least one of compensation, authority or tooling.

6. The pressure AI created - only within what policy text confirms

I write this section with particular care. I could not verify any figures on how generative tooling has affected submission quality. So I state no numbers. Instead I look only at what policy documents have started to write down. Rules usually follow problems that already occurred, so the existence of a rule is a weak signal.

The language model policy I read contains the following.

  • Authors are responsible for the content of the paper. The text states that authors are responsible for the entire content of the paper, including all text, figures and references
  • Listed as a violation is using references generated by a language model without conducting the due diligence to verify correctness, existence and appropriateness. That existence appears as a check item is telling
  • If a language model was used in implementing the method and it is an important, original or non-standard component, it should be described in the experimental setup section
  • By contrast, spell checkers, grammar suggestions and programming aid for editing purposes do not need to be documented. The policy does not ban the tools, it fixes where responsibility sits

The constraints on reviewers run the other way.

  • Reviewers must keep all aspects of the review process confidential, and must not share, discuss or disclose information about submissions with anyone or any language model
  • Code submitted for reviewing cannot be distributed to anyone, including any language model, nor used for any purpose other than review
  • General concept understanding is allowed, but the moment confidential material goes in, it is prohibited

An asymmetry follows. The side producing manuscripts may use automated tooling freely, with limited duty to record it. The side examining manuscripts may not put manuscripts into automated tooling at all, because of confidentiality. Generation and examination do not gain equally from the same tools.

That asymmetry is not a flaw in the rules. Confidentiality is foundational to review and cannot be traded away. But it is worth recognising that the examining side's throughput has a harder time keeping pace with the generating side. Beyond that I have no verified basis, so I say no more.

What I do not say because I could not verify it. The growth in submission counts over recent years, the share of manuscripts containing generated text, and any resulting change in review quality. These three are widely quoted, but this time I could not open the primary statistics. That is why there is not a single figure of that kind in this article.

7. So how should a paper be read?

Reduce everything so far to one sentence. «Did it pass review» is a very low-information signal. The signal that replaces it is «what was disclosed».

First separate what acceptance does guarantee from what it does not.

  • It guarantees that formal requirements were met. Length, format, anonymisation, not a duplicate submission, and other mechanical gates
  • It guarantees that at least two peers read the manuscript and judged that the claim holds on the evidence given
  • It guarantees that obvious logical defects and omissions of well-known prior work were probably filtered
  • It does not guarantee that the result comes out again
  • It does not guarantee that the data and the authors exist
  • It does not guarantee the absence of conflicts of interest
  • It does not guarantee that it will not be refuted later
  • It does not guarantee the same effect in your environment

The table below is what to look at instead of the pass or fail signal. Read left to right.

What to checkStrong signalWeak signalNo signal
CodePublic repository plus a script that produces the paper's numbersRepository exists but settings differ from the paperOnly an available on request line
DataPublic data with the preprocessing steps includedAccessible after an application processInternal data, cannot be released
ConditionsHardware, scale and distribution stated in a tableScattered through the proseNo description of conditions
BaselineCompared against current methods with tuning budgets statedComparison present but tuning unclearCompared only against its own earlier version
Failure reportingThe conditions where it fails are in the bodyA limitations section exists pro formaNo mention of limitations
ConflictsFunding and relationships statedOnly affiliations listedNothing stated
VersionDifferences between preprint and final version notedOnly a version labelUnclear which version this is
Follow-upAn independent group reports reproductionHeavily cited but never reproducedNeither cited nor reproduced

Using the table is simple. If several marks land in the «no signal» column, that paper cannot be the sole basis for a decision. It can be a reference, not a basis. Conversely, several strong signals make even an unreviewed preprint a checkable document.

At what layer to read a claim itself is a separate problem. How to pin down what a word like «broken» actually covers in a particular field is handled separately in how to verify a claim that AI broke a cipher. This article looks at what the institution checked, that one at what the claim means.

8. For engineers - when a paper backs a decision

When you read a paper for a decision rather than out of academic interest, the checklist changes. Decisions cost money to reverse.

  • Actually click the reproduction code link. Dead links are startlingly common, and clicking takes three seconds
  • Compare the repository defaults against the paper's tables. If the values differ, the paper's numbers will not reproduce
  • Look at the last commit date. A repository untouched since the paper has no maintenance plan
  • Check the data licence. Results produced on research-only data do not transfer to a commercial environment unchanged
  • Write the scale difference as a number. «Bigger than ours» is not a judgement, «eight times ours» is
  • Write down the distribution difference. Different language, domain or length distribution usually shrinks the relative gain
  • Check whether the baseline is what you actually run. A comparison against something you already abandoned means nothing to you
  • Look at funding and affiliation. If the comparison target is a competing product, scrutinise the symmetry of tuning budgets harder
  • Skim the follow-up citations once. Rebuttals and failed reproduction reports usually surface there
  • Most important of all. Run it once at the smallest possible scale in your own environment. That single run beats twenty papers

8.1 What to write in the decision record

If a decision document says only «based on paper X», then when the decision turns out wrong you cannot find which part was wrong. Writing it in the shape below makes reversal easy.

[ENGINEERING DECISION RECORD]
Source        : title / authors / year / form (preprint · conference · journal)
Access        : address / date checked / version (v1 · v2 · final)
Figure used   : P99 latency from Table 3   ← from the table, not the prose
Its conditions: 8 accelerators · batch 64 · dataset A · 12 hours total
Our conditions: 2 accelerators · batch 8  · dataset B
Difference    : 4x compute gap · different data domain
Repro code    : yes / no — if yes, commit identifier
Ran it        : yes / no — if yes, one-line result summary
Conflicts     : funding source · whether baseline is a competitor
Reversal cost : work required to roll back if this is wrong

The value of this record shows up later. Six months on, when something breaks, this table alone lets you separate «the conditions differed» from «the paper was wrong» from «we ported it badly». The response in those three cases is entirely different.

9. Attempts at improvement - confirmed and unconfirmed

Several efforts try to change the system. Here I describe only those whose primary documents I opened, and for the rest I give the name and say nothing about effect.

Registered reports I confirmed from a primary document. The structure is this. Instead of the usual order in which a finished study is submitted with its results, the research plan is reviewed before data collection. At stage one the introduction, methods and any pilot data are submitted, reviewers evaluate the importance of the question and the rigour of the method, and a passing protocol receives in-principle acceptance. In the source's own words, that means the journal virtually guarantees publication if the authors conduct the experiment in accordance with their approved protocol. At stage two the results and discussion are submitted, and whatever the outcome, publication follows if the protocol was kept. The explanatory document states that over 300 journals currently offer the format.

What the format targets is also stated in that document. Low statistical power, selective reporting of results, and publication bias. Because the analysis is fixed before the results are seen, changing hypotheses afterwards or cherry-picking favourable analyses becomes difficult.

An important limit. Registered reports are not a device aimed at author existence or fabricated citations. They target something else. For the kind of problem that prompted this article, registered reports are not the answer, and presenting them as one would be inaccurate. A good mechanism is not the answer to every problem, and that deserves saying plainly here.

What an individual researcher's disposition means inside this structure is covered in zen and the art of machine learning research. If this article looks at the institution, that one looks at the person.

10. Applying this to our own blog and documents

This discussion does not stop at the edge of academia. Anyone writing a technical blog or an internal document cites sources every day. There is a discipline for that.

First separate sources by tier. A tier is not a ranking of trustworthiness, it is a label for what kind of scrutiny happened.

FormGates passedSpeedPhrase to put in the sentence
PreprintNone (author posted)Fastest«an unreviewed preprint»
Workshop paperShort review · work in progress allowedFast«a workshop-stage report»
Conference main trackOne full review round · fixed deadlineMedium«a paper accepted at the main track that year»
Journal articleMultiple revision rounds possibleSlow«a journal article»
Standards documentBody process · consensus basedSlowest«a clause in the specification»
Vendor engineering blogInternal review onlyFast«the vendor's own announcement»
Secondary reportingEditorial reviewFast«a matter known through reporting»

Simply naming the tier hands the reader a great deal of information. The phrase «research shows» flattens all seven rows into one.

10.1 Citation sentence templates

GOOD : «a paper accepted at that year's main track reports in Table 2 that …»
GOOD : «this is a preprint-stage report and has not been externally reviewed»
GOOD : «these are the vendor's own figures and I found no independent check»
GOOD : «I could not open the original, so its contents are not described here»

BAD  : «research shows that …»                the tier disappears
BAD  : «science has proven»                   turns one paper into consensus
BAD  : «it was revealed in a paper»           no who, what or under what conditions
BAD  : «according to reports, the paper says»  a secondary source used as primary
BAD  : «recent work suggests …»               neither the date nor the count is pinned

A few more rules belong here. None is hard to follow, and following them lengthens the useful life of what you write.

  • Never cite a document you could not open. Do not read a summary or a news item and write as though you quoted the original. Sections 1 and 12 of this article are that rule applied
  • Record the date you checked. Documents change quietly, and without a date there is nothing to compare against later
  • When you carry over the original wording, say that you did. Mixing summary and quotation erases where the original ends
  • Take figures from tables. A summarising sentence in the prose already has one layer of the author's interpretation in it
  • Do not inflate one paper into a consensus. «Researchers believe» is for when you checked several
  • Handle negative results by the same standard. Exempting only the papers that favour your argument is itself selective reporting
  • Link to the original. Routing through a summary site puts the reader one step further from checking it

11. The review path on one page

Reduce everything above to a single diagram and it looks like this.

submission
 │
 ├─ format check : length · template · anonymisation · duplicate submission
 ├─ assignment   : reviewer matching by keywords and bidding
 ├─ review       : soundness · clarity · significance · originality
 ├─ discussion   : author rebuttal and reviewer reconciliation
 └─ decision     : accept · reject

nowhere on this path is there a step for
   author existence · affiliation lookup · reference existence · reproduction · raw data

so what «accepted» means is
   the five steps above were cleared   (and that is all)

What you should read in that diagram is the boxes that are absent. The institution performs only the boxes that are there. Expecting the absent ones is a reading problem, not an institutional one.

Here I collect what this article cannot support. Anyone citing this article should read this list along with it.

  • The body text of the personal blog post that prompted this. Access was blocked and I could not open it. That is why no counts, percentages or venue names appear here
  • A publication ethics organisation's guidelines for peer reviewers. Access was blocked. It would have been the best source on what reviewers are explicitly not asked to do, and I could not use it this time
  • A large computing society's reproducibility badge definitions. Both addresses were blocked, so I did not describe what each badge level certifies and what it does not
  • Primary material on the effect of open review. Not confirmed

  • Actual papers assigned per reviewer. Not stated in the guidelines, so I wrote nothing beyond «several»
  • Variation across fields. The documents cited here lean on a medical authorship standard and one computing conference's policy. There is no basis for generalising them to other fields
  • The relationship between acceptance and later reproduction success. Not confirmed

13. Closing: not whether it passed, but what was disclosed

The conclusion here is not cynicism about academia. It is the opposite. Understanding the institution accurately stops you expecting what it does not contain, and lets you fill that space with your own checking.

Peer review has done enormously useful work over the past century. It filtered obvious errors, it forced arguments to be sharpened, it built shared standards within fields. All of that was the work of weighing the quality of an argument on the assumption that the authors are honest, and the institution was designed for exactly that. Failing at something outside its design is not the same as failing.

But when that assumption comes under strain, compensation has to happen on the reader's side too, not only inside the institution. And what a reader can do is more concrete than it sounds.

  • Ask «what was disclosed» instead of «did it pass». Code, data, conditions, conflicts
  • When citing, put the tier of the source into the sentence
  • Never cite a document you could not open
  • In decision documents, write which table, under which conditions, and how it differs from yours
  • And where possible, run it once at the smallest scale

Go back to that meeting. What was needed then was not a rebuttal to «it was accepted at a conference». It was one question. «Which table gives the number we would use, what were the conditions of that table, and where do they differ from ours?» That question blames nobody, requires no expertise, and takes thirty seconds to ask.

# Paper Triage Checklist — not «did it pass» but «what was disclosed»

> Source: braindetox.kr — what peer review was never built to catch
> Based on: 1 authorship standard, 4 published conference policies, 1 open science document, 1 editors body guidance (all opened directly)
> Paste this into an AI and say «check the paper below against these criteria».
> ⚠ This document does not rate any conference or researcher. It maps the design scope of an institution.

## 0. Premise — what acceptance means

| Category | Content |
|---|---|
| **Guaranteed** | Formal requirements cleared · peers judged the argument holds |
| **Not guaranteed** | Reproduction · data exists · authors exist · no conflicts |
| **Designed for** | Judging argument quality (assuming honest authors) |
| **Outside the design** | Fraud detection · identity checks · raw data cross-checks |

**Acceptance is not a quality guarantee, it is a minimum gate.**

## 1. Source tiers — always put this in the sentence

| Form | Gates passed | Phrase to use |
|---|---|---|
| Preprint | None (author posted) | «an unreviewed preprint» |
| Workshop | Short review · work in progress | «a workshop-stage report» |
| Conference main track | One full review round | «accepted at the main track that year» |
| Journal | Multiple revision rounds | «a journal article» |
| Standards document | Body process · consensus | «a clause in the specification» |
| Vendor blog | Internal review only | «the vendor's own announcement» |
| Secondary reporting | Editorial review | «known through reporting» |

## 2. Disclosure signals — 8 boxes

- [ ] **Code** repository is public and the link is alive
- [ ] **Code** repository defaults match the paper's tables
- [ ] **Data** is public or the access process is stated
- [ ] **Data** licence permits your use
- [ ] **Conditions** hardware, scale and distribution are in a table
- [ ] **Baseline** is current and tuning budgets are symmetric
- [ ] **Limits** the failing conditions are stated in the body
- [ ] **Conflicts** funding and relationships are stated

**Three or more «no signal» boxes and it cannot be the sole basis for a decision.**

## 3. Engineer's pre-decision checks

- [ ] I **actually clicked** the reproduction code link
- [ ] I checked the repository's **last commit date**
- [ ] I wrote the condition gap **as a number**
- [ ] I confirmed the baseline is **what we actually run**
- [ ] I skimmed follow-up citations for **rebuttals or failed reproductions**
- [ ] I **ran it once at the smallest scale**

## 4. Decision record format

```
Source        : title / authors / year / form
Access        : address / date checked / version
Figure used   : table number and item (from the table, not the prose)
Its conditions: hardware · scale · data · runtime
Our conditions: same items
Difference    : multiples and domain gap
Repro code    : yes (commit id) / no
Ran it        : yes (one-line result) / no
Conflicts     : funding · baseline identity
Reversal cost : work to roll back if wrong
```

## 5. Citation discipline — 7 lines

- [ ] **Never cite a document you could not open**
- [ ] Record the **date** you checked
- [ ] Say when you are **carrying over original wording**
- [ ] Take figures **from tables**
- [ ] Do not **inflate one paper into a consensus**
- [ ] Apply the **same standard to material against you**
- [ ] Link to **the original**

## 6. Do not

- ❌ Flatten it into **«research shows»** — the tier disappears
- ❌ **«Science has proven»** — turns one paper into consensus
- ❌ Read **acceptance as fact-checking**
- ❌ **Blame individual reviewers** — unpaid, deadlined, powerless is the structure
- ❌ **Single out and mock** a conference or a researcher
- ❌ Conclude that **academia is rotten** — a wrong diagnosis gives a wrong response
- ❌ Use **secondary reporting as a primary citation**
- ❌ Describe the contents of **a page you could not open**
