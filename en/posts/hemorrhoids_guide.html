Hemorrhoids Complete Guide — Symptoms, Causes, Treatment & Prevention

From rectal bleeding to prolapse — everything you need to know about hemorrhoids, based on 2026 medical information

2026-06-30 Health

Introduction: The Condition Nobody Talks About — But Almost Everyone Gets

Hemorrhoids are among the most common medical conditions in the world. Studies estimate that up to 75% of adults will experience hemorrhoids at some point in their lifetime. Despite their prevalence, many people delay seeking treatment out of embarrassment, allowing symptoms to worsen unnecessarily.

This guide is based on information from the NIH National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, and peer-reviewed medical literature, updated as of June 2026. It is a general health information resource — not a substitute for professional medical diagnosis or treatment. If you have symptoms, please consult a colorectal surgeon or gastroenterologist.

1. What Are Hemorrhoids — Internal vs. External

Hemorrhoids are swollen, enlarged veins in the lower rectum and anus. The anal canal naturally contains vascular cushions that help control bowel movements. When these cushions become excessively enlarged or displaced, they cause symptoms we call hemorrhoids.

1.1 Internal Hemorrhoids

Internal hemorrhoids develop above the dentate line (the boundary between the rectum and the anal canal). Because this area lacks somatic pain receptors, internal hemorrhoids are typically painless. The hallmark symptom is bright red rectal bleeding during or after bowel movements — blood on toilet paper or dripping into the toilet bowl. In advanced cases, internal hemorrhoids may prolapse (protrude through the anus).

1.2 External Hemorrhoids

External hemorrhoids develop below the dentate line, under the sensitive anal skin. This area is richly innervated, making external hemorrhoids painful, particularly when a blood clot (thrombus) forms — a condition called thrombosed external hemorrhoid. Symptoms include a painful lump near the anus, swelling, itching, and discomfort when sitting.

1.3 Anal Fissures

Although not hemorrhoids, anal fissures are often confused with them. An anal fissure is a small tear in the anal lining, typically caused by passing hard stools. It causes sharp pain during and after bowel movements, along with bright red bleeding. Chronic fissures may involve internal anal sphincter spasm.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

Hemorrhoids develop when pressure in the anal and rectal veins rises persistently. Key contributing factors include:

  • Chronic constipation and straining: Straining to pass hard stools dramatically raises anal venous pressure. Sitting on the toilet too long compounds this effect.
  • Prolonged sitting: Desk workers, drivers, and others who sit for extended periods experience reduced blood flow to the anal region.
  • Pregnancy: The growing uterus compresses rectal veins, and progesterone relaxes vein walls, increasing hemorrhoid risk. The straining of childbirth is also a trigger.
  • Low-fiber diet: Insufficient intake of vegetables, fruits, and whole grains leads to harder stools and more straining.
  • Obesity: Excess abdominal weight increases intra-abdominal pressure on rectal veins.
  • Aging: Supporting tissues in the anal canal weaken with age, making hemorrhoids more likely after 45-50.
  • Heavy lifting: Holding breath while lifting heavy objects (Valsalva maneuver) spikes abdominal pressure.

3. Symptoms to Watch For

  • Rectal bleeding: Bright red blood on toilet paper or in the toilet bowl — the most common symptom of internal hemorrhoids.
  • Prolapse: Tissue bulging out of the anus during or between bowel movements, ranging from temporary to permanent protrusion.
  • Pain and discomfort: Primarily with external or thrombosed hemorrhoids; sitting can become very painful.
  • Itching and mucus discharge: Enlarged hemorrhoids may secrete mucus, causing perianal moisture and itching.
  • Feeling of incomplete evacuation: A persistent sensation of fullness even after defecation.
See a Doctor Immediately If:
Rectal bleeding lasts more than 2-3 weeks; blood is dark red or maroon; bleeding is accompanied by weight loss, narrowing stools, persistent abdominal pain, or diarrhea. These signs may indicate colorectal polyps, inflammatory bowel disease, or colorectal cancer — not hemorrhoids. Anyone over 50 with rectal bleeding should have a colonoscopy.

4. Hemorrhoid Grades — The Goligher Classification

Internal hemorrhoids are graded I through IV based on the degree of prolapse. This grading guides treatment decisions.

Grade IEnlarged but do not prolapse; painless bleeding onlyLifestyle changes, high-fiber diet, sitz baths

Grade IIIProlapse and require manual reductionRubber band ligation, hemorrhoidectomy

5. Conservative Treatment — Lifestyle and Medications

Grade I and II hemorrhoids can usually be managed without procedures. Even Grade III often responds to conservative care initially.

5.1 Sitz Baths

Soaking the anal area in warm water (38-40°C / 100-104°F) for 10-15 minutes, 2-3 times daily (especially after bowel movements) is the cornerstone of conservative hemorrhoid management. Warm water relaxes the internal anal sphincter, improves local circulation, reduces inflammation, and promotes healing. Plain water is effective — no need for salts or soaps unless directed by a physician.

5.2 Dietary Fiber and Hydration

Consuming 25-35 grams of dietary fiber per day and drinking 1.5-2 liters of water daily softens stool and reduces straining — addressing the root cause of most hemorrhoids. High-fiber foods include whole grains (brown rice, oats, whole wheat bread), vegetables (broccoli, carrots, spinach), fruits (apples, pears, berries), and legumes (lentils, beans). Soluble fiber supplements such as psyllium husk (Metamucil) are effective additions if dietary intake is insufficient.

5.3 Topical Treatments

Over-the-counter creams and suppositories containing ingredients like witch hazel, hydrocortisone, or lidocaine can temporarily relieve itching and discomfort. Hydrocortisone-containing products should not be used for more than 1-2 weeks, as prolonged use may cause skin thinning. Stool softeners (e.g., docusate sodium) help reduce straining during bowel movements.

5.4 Bowel Habit Modification

Do not sit on the toilet for more than 5 minutes; avoid reading or using a smartphone during defecation. Respond promptly to the urge to defecate — delaying allows stools to dry and harden. Using a footstool to elevate your feet while sitting (simulating a squatting posture) reduces the anorectal angle and makes defecation easier.

6. Procedures and Surgery

6.1 Rubber Band Ligation (RBL)

The most widely used office-based procedure for Grade II-III internal hemorrhoids. A small rubber band is placed around the base of the hemorrhoid, cutting off its blood supply. The tissue necrotizes and sloughs off within 1-2 weeks, leaving a small scar that prevents re-prolapse. The procedure takes 5-10 minutes without anesthesia. Mild discomfort, a sensation of pressure, and minor bleeding may occur for a few days afterward. Studies confirm RBL is superior to sclerotherapy and infrared coagulation for most internal hemorrhoids.

6.2 Sclerotherapy

A chemical solution (sclerosant) is injected into hemorrhoid tissue, causing it to shrink and scar. Primarily effective for Grade I-II hemorrhoids with bleeding as the main complaint. The procedure is simple with low complication rates but has lower long-term success rates than RBL.

6.3 Infrared Coagulation (IRC)

Infrared energy is applied to coagulate the blood supply of small Grade I-II internal hemorrhoids. Quick and relatively painless, but less effective for larger hemorrhoids.

6.4 Hemorrhoidectomy

Surgical excision of hemorrhoid tissue — the most definitive treatment with the lowest recurrence rates. Indicated for Grade III-IV hemorrhoids, large external hemorrhoids, or combined internal-external hemorrhoids that fail non-surgical treatment. Post-operative pain can be significant for 1-2 weeks, and complete healing takes approximately 6-8 weeks. Stapled hemorrhoidopexy (procedure for prolapse and hemorrhoids, PPH) uses a circular stapling device to resect prolapsed tissue with less post-operative pain but slightly higher recurrence rates than conventional hemorrhoidectomy.

6.5 Thrombosed External Hemorrhoid Excision

If performed within 72 hours of onset, incision and evacuation of the blood clot under local anesthesia provides immediate and dramatic pain relief. After 72 hours, the clot begins reabsorbing naturally, and conservative management (sitz baths, NSAIDs) is preferred.

7. Prevention — Lifestyle Habits That Work

  • High-fiber diet: Target 25-35 g/day through whole grains, vegetables, fruits, and legumes.
  • Adequate hydration: 1.5-2 liters of water daily. Limit alcohol and excessive caffeine, which can dehydrate and worsen constipation.
  • Regular exercise: At least 30 minutes of aerobic activity (walking, cycling, swimming) daily to stimulate bowel motility and control weight.
  • Don't delay defecation: Respond to the urge promptly. Stool becomes harder the longer it remains in the colon.
  • Limit toilet time: Get off the toilet within 5 minutes. Leave your phone outside the bathroom.
  • Take breaks from sitting: Stand and walk for 5 minutes every 1-2 hours if you have a desk job.
  • Lift safely: Exhale when exerting force; never hold your breath during heavy lifting.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Do hemorrhoids go away on their own?

Small Grade I and some Grade II hemorrhoids can resolve with conservative treatment — increased fiber, water, sitz baths, and avoiding straining. Thrombosed external hemorrhoids typically reabsorb within 2-4 weeks. However, Grade III-IV hemorrhoids and large external hemorrhoids usually require procedures or surgery.

Q. Can I exercise with hemorrhoids?

Light to moderate aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, cycling) is beneficial — it stimulates bowel movements and reduces constipation. However, heavy weightlifting, intense straining exercises, or cycling on narrow seats can aggravate hemorrhoids. Avoid any exercise that causes pain or pressure in the anal region.

Q. Will hemorrhoids come back after treatment?

Recurrence rates vary by treatment. Conventional hemorrhoidectomy has a long-term recurrence rate of about 5-10%. Rubber band ligation has higher recurrence (30-50% over 5 years), but repeat banding is safe and effective. Regardless of treatment, maintaining a high-fiber diet and healthy bowel habits is essential to prevent recurrence.
