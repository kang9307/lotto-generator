Adopting AI and Getting Faster Are Not the Same Thing - What Organizations That Actually Sped Up Changed

Approval layers, review capacity, the new job of specs - and what to measure

Aug 4, 2026 IT/Tech

Introduction: every tool is installed and the calendar has not moved

The accounts are provisioned. A coding assistant is attached, meeting notes summarize themselves, and a first-draft spec takes thirty minutes instead of a day. And yet the dates on this quarter's roadmap look almost identical to last year's. Releases still go out every two weeks. Approvals still take three days.

The usual diagnosis is that adoption has not settled yet, or that people are not using the tools well. That diagnosis is usually wrong. The tools are being used. The problem is that the place where the tool was plugged in was never the bottleneck.

Break down the interval from commit to production and you find that the stretches where someone is actively working are shorter than the stretches spent waiting for the next person. Waiting for review, waiting for approval, waiting for a release window, waiting for another team. AI shortens the first kind of stretch. Turning thirty minutes of coding into ten does nothing to the three-day approval queue behind it, so lead time stays put. That part is arithmetic.

Organizations that actually got faster knew this arithmetic, and instead of adding another tool they changed the structure that produces the waiting. This article is about what those structural changes concretely were, and about what you have to measure before you have earned the right to say you got faster. The diagnostic side - why adding tools leaves the work unchanged - is covered in The Productivity Mirage - More Tools, Same Amount of Work, so it is not repeated here. This piece looks only at what works.

1. Adoption versus redesign - a tool only plugs into one cell of the flow

The difference compresses into one sentence. Adoption makes people do the same work faster. Redesign removes the work or reorders it. That is where two organizations spending the same budget end up in different places.

StageOrganization that only adoptedOrganization that changed the flowEffect on lead time
DraftingAI writes the draftAI writes the draftIdentical. This was never the bottleneck
ReviewSame human reviewers, longer queueBatches split small, automated checks moved earlierLarge. Touches the waiting directly
ApprovalApproval chain untouchedReversible decisions removed from the chainLargest. Day-scale waits disappear
SpecsDocuments humans readAcceptance criteria and prohibitions in machine-readable formModerate. Shows up as rework rate
MeasurementCounts seats and hours savedCounts lead time and rework rateThe measurement itself decides the next call
Incident responseAdds an approval stepAdds an automated checkThe first slows down permanently, the second does not

The last row is quietly the most important. An organization that adds an approver after an incident and one that adds a check end up with completely different speeds a year later. Approval spends human waiting time; a check spends machine execution time. Once AI has raised output, choosing the first means the queue grows in proportion to output.

2. The amplifier claim - why the same tool produces opposite results

The central sentence of the 2025 DORA report is that AI does not fix a team; it amplifies what is already there. That phrasing is not rhetoric, it is a summary of data drawn from roughly 5,000 technology practitioners and more than 100 hours of interviews.

  • 90 percent of respondents said they use AI at work. Whether you have adopted it is no longer a question that separates anyone
  • More than 80 percent said AI increased their productivity. That is self-report, not measurement
  • At the same time 30 percent said they have little or no trust in AI-generated code. Using something while not believing it now shows up in the statistics
  • AI adoption showed a positive relationship with software delivery throughput and product performance. That is a change from the prior year
  • But it still shows a negative relationship with delivery stability. More change failures, more rework, longer time to resolve problems
  • 90 percent of organizations have at least one internal platform. Whether you have one stopped being the differentiator; how good it is became the differentiator

Throughput up and stability down sounds contradictory the first time you hear it. In practice it is an extremely common shape. You get exactly this pattern when upstream production speeds up while downstream review, testing and release capacity stays flat. Pouring water faster does not widen the neck of the bottle.

2.1 The seven things DORA points at

The AI capabilities model from the same research names seven conditions that amplify AI's benefits. What stands out in the list is that six of them have nothing to do with AI itself. They are fundamentals.

  • A clear and communicated AI stance - does a written answer exist for what is allowed and what is not
  • Healthy data ecosystems - is it settled where data lives and who owns it
  • AI-accessible internal data - is knowledge locked in forms only humans can open
  • Strong version control practices - you can only move fast if you can move back
  • Working in small batches - inspection can only keep up if the handoff is small
  • User-centric focus - building the wrong thing faster is a loss, not a gain
  • Quality internal platforms - if the standard path is pleasant, people stop routing around it

The practical takeaway is blunt. Layering tools onto a team with poor version control and huge batches amplifies instability and nothing else. There is an order here, and organizations that skipped it are the ones asking why adoption produced no speed. The separate question of redesigning organizational structure itself is covered in What Is an AI-Native Organization?.

3. Decision layers - not the number of approvals but the sum of waiting

Advice to cut approval layers is common, and executing it directly usually runs into resistance. Approvals exist for reasons. Each one remembers a specific incident.

So rather than reducing the count, redefining the scope is what actually lands. There is one criterion. Is it reversible?

  • Reversible decisions come out of the approval path. A deployment behind a feature flag, an internal tool change, an experimental page - anything you can undo in minutes has no business carrying a three-day approval
  • Irreversible decisions get stricter approval instead. Bulk customer messaging, data deletion, billing changes, external announcements belong here
  • Treat silence as consent. If no objection arrives inside a fixed window, it passes. In exchange, require objections to come with a stated reason
  • Assign approvers by accountability, not by grade. The approver is whoever lives with the outcome. Copying the seniority ladder into the workflow only adds waiting
  • Put an expiry on waiting. If nothing happens in three days, withdraw the request and return it to the owner. An approval that hangs indefinitely is the most expensive kind
  • Turn approval history into a metric. Pull the average days per stage every month and the arguments get shorter

The effect of this work is large but quiet. On a lead time chart, the upper percentiles fall before the median does. If you are watching averages only, you will miss the improvement entirely.

4. When review becomes the bottleneck - production triples, inspection does not

This is the most predictable failure after AI adoption. Individual output rises visibly while the people who must inspect that output stay the same. Review requests pile up, the pile gets cleared in a hurry, hurried review gets shallow, and a change that passed a shallow review breaks in production. A significant share of the stability degradation DORA observes is explained by this path.

The answer is not more reviewers. Headcount does not grow that fast. Reducing the load per review is the realistic move.

  • Force changes to be split. An 800-line diff produces ceremonial review. Set a ceiling and require anything above it to be broken up
  • Push everything a machine can catch ahead of review. Formatting, static analysis, test status and vulnerability scanning are not places for human attention
  • Flag changes with a high share of generated code and review them against different criteria. Hand-written and generated code fail in different ways
  • Measure review waiting time. Time from request to first comment tells you immediately whether the bottleneck is a person or a rule
  • Automate reviewer assignment and rotate it. The moment work concentrates on one person, that person becomes the ceiling of the organization
  • Make the submitter fill in acceptance evidence first. A submission with no statement of what was verified and how doubles the reviewer's time

How to measure it today. Take 20 changes merged in the last four weeks and write down submission time, first review comment time, merge time. Looking at which of the two gaps is longer is enough to pick where to work. A long gap before the first comment is an assignment problem; a long gap after it is a change-size problem.

5. The new job of specs and docs - from read by people to consumed by models

AI changed what internal documents are for. A document used to be an artifact humans read, and the organization survived when nobody read it. A document is now an input to a model. When it is missing, the model fills the gap with a plausible guess, and if that guess clears review it ships.

That is why AI-accessible internal data sits on the DORA list. An internal wiki that exists but is bound to human-only permissions, or one that records what was decided without recording why, effectively does not exist from a model's point of view.

5.1 What is worth writing down

  • Acceptance criteria - what makes it done. Write it in a form you could transcribe into a test wherever possible
  • Decision records - what you rejected and why is worth more than what you chose. Without the discarded options, the model will keep proposing them again
  • Prohibitions and counterexamples - one sentence like this API is never called from a batch job prevents one incident
  • A glossary - internal abbreviations and domain terms. Without it, generated output uses different words every time and searches stop matching
  • An owner and an expiry date - a stale document is worse than no document. Output built on an expired reference is wrong and convincing at the same time

One common misreading is worth naming. This is not a call to write more documents. Volume degrades retrieval quality and raises the cost of expiry management. What needs to increase is not the number of documents but the density of reasons recorded per decision.

6. What you must measure to claim you got faster

This is the most operational section of the article. AI transformations that run without measurement end up, without exception, at a usage report. How many queries per month and how many people logged in is activity, not outcome.

The five metrics DORA publishes work as a starting point. The definitions are public, and the fact that they deliberately separate throughput from instability is what makes them useful.

GroupMetricDefinitionTypical movement after AI adoption
ThroughputChange lead timeTime for a change committed to version control to reach productionImproves slightly as hands-on time drops. Barely moves if waiting is untouched
Deployment frequencyNumber of deployments in a period, or time between deploymentsRises first. Which is exactly why it misleads
Failed deployment recovery timeTime to recover from a deployment failure requiring immediate interventionGrows longer as code nobody has the context for accumulates
InstabilityChange fail rateShare of deployments that required immediate interventionRises whenever inspection capacity stays flat
Deployment rework rateShare of deployments that were unplanned and caused by a production incidentThe most honest metric. If this rises, you did not get faster

Reading only the three throughput metrics guarantees you will be fooled. If deployment frequency rises and lead time falls while rework rate rises alongside them, that is not speed, it is debt. Simply putting both groups on the same screen settles half the internal arguments.

6.1 Add exactly two more

  • Share of lead time spent waiting - the fraction of elapsed time during which nobody touched the work. If this is above 60 percent and you are increasing the tool budget, the direction is wrong
  • First-pass rate - the fraction of submissions that clear without a change request. The quality of your specs and acceptance criteria lands directly here

The cost dimension is outside this article's scope but belongs on the same dashboard. Designing token budgets and attribution at the organization level is covered in Governing AI Token Spend at the Org Level.

7. Where perception and measurement come apart

The reason to insist on measurement is that perception is actually wrong. This is not speculation; it comes from a trial with a control condition.

The METR randomized controlled trial ran under these conditions. Sixteen open-source developers with roughly five years of average experience completed 246 real tasks in mature repositories they themselves maintain. In the AI-allowed condition they mostly used Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 or 3.7 Sonnet, across chat, agent mode and autocomplete.

  • Tasks in the AI-allowed condition took 19 percent longer to complete
  • The same developers, asked afterward, estimated on average that they had been 20 percent faster
  • The gap between perception and measurement is roughly 39 percentage points

How you use this number matters. Reading it as AI makes people slower is a misreading. The conditions are highly specific. Experts who already know the codebase intimately, working in mature repositories they built, with early-2025 tooling. Context transfer cost more than the assistance was worth in that setting, and the direction can differ in unfamiliar codebases or unfamiliar languages. METR itself announced in February 2026 that it was changing the experiment design.

One thing does come out of it cleanly. Self-report surveys cannot serve as evidence of productivity. DORA's finding that more than 80 percent report a productivity increase and METR's 19 percent slowdown do not contradict each other. One asked how it felt; the other measured elapsed time. If your organization is justifying budget with satisfaction scores from an internal AI survey, it is worth asking what that number is actually measuring.

The same design problem appears on the hiring side, where assessment has to assume AI is present. That discussion lives in Tech Interviews in the AI Era.

8. Four failure shapes - where things stall

Stalling has types. Different symptoms need different treatment.

TypeVisible signalActual causeWhere to work
Pilot stallExcellent demo, still a pilot six months onProduction requirements were out of scope from day oneNail down transition criteria at kickoff
Shadow ITInternal usage is low but everyone says they use AI a lotThe internal path is slow or blockedNot another ban - path quality
Quality debtSpeed is up but incident response time grewInspection capacity cannot keep up with outputSmaller batches, checks moved earlier
No evaluationEveryone says it is going well, nobody can produce a numberNo written quality bar for outputAcceptance criteria and a regression suite

Of the four, shadow IT only yields an answer when treated as a product problem rather than a control problem. People use personal accounts not because they missed the policy but because the internal route is slow or closed. One more circular pushes the usage further underground, and the result is that nobody knows where company data is going. Making the internal route genuinely usable is the only control that works.

No evaluation is also common and hard to spot. The observation that if AI produces ten times more output and your quality bar does not scale with it you have automated your own decline names this exactly. The more output there is, the more deciding what counts as passing becomes the bottleneck. The structure of failing to move from proof of concept to production is laid out separately in Why AI Proofs of Concept Succeed but Never Reach Production.

9. Judgment by scale - ten people and a thousand fix different things first

Applying the same advice at different scales loses money, because the bottleneck sits in a different place.

ScaleMain bottleneckDo firstDo not do yet
Up to 10Context exists only in people's headsDecision records, acceptance criteria, glossaryApproval process work. There is nothing to remove
10-50Handoffs between teamsReduce handoff count, shrink batchesA company-wide standard platform. Too early
50-300Review and approval waitingExempt reversible decisions, automate review checksReorgs. Measure the flow first, then decide
300+Standard path quality and routing around itInternal platform quality, lead time instrumentationTeam-by-team tool rollout campaigns. The total does not move

The last row is where large organizations waste the most budget. Making individual teams 10 percent faster barely changes total lead time. When work passes through several teams, the slowest segment decides the whole. In the other direction, a small team setting out to overhaul its approval process is equally wasteful. There is nothing to remove.

One thing holds at every scale. If inspection capacity does not grow with output, more output only lengthens the queue. That single sentence runs through the entire table above.

10. What specifically gets in the way in Korean organizations

10.1 Approval chains do not distinguish reversible from irreversible

Approval routes in Korean organizations are generally the seniority ladder transcribed into a workflow. In that structure, a deployment wrapped in a feature flag and a bulk message to customers travel the same path. The risk differs by two orders of magnitude and the procedure is identical.

Proposing to cut the number of approvers rarely passes. Proposing to redraw what requires approval based on how long it takes to undo has a much better chance, because it is not an argument for removing control. It is an argument for moving control to where the risk is.

10.2 In separated networks the shape of adoption itself is different

In finance, public sector and similar environments where outbound API calls are restricted, vendor services cannot simply be attached. What frequently happens is that a nominal internal channel is opened while real usage flows through personal accounts. That is the shadow IT above. When the path is slow, people route around it.

There are two options. Reach genuinely usable quality with a model running inside the perimeter, or clearly delimit which data may leave and formally allow external services within that boundary. Doing neither and issuing a prohibition is the worst combination: usage stays the same and only visibility disappears.

10.3 Privacy rules are cheaper to align with at adoption time

The guideline on personal data processing for the development and use of generative AI, released by Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission in August 2025, divides the generative AI lifecycle into four stages: purpose setting, strategy, AI training and development, and system deployment and management. It presents the privacy issues, legal standards and safeguards to consider at each stage, along with an AI privacy governance framework spanning all of them.

The practical value of the document is in sequence. Settling the legal basis for processing at the purpose-setting stage removes a later fight over the scope of training data. Retrofitting a basis after a pipeline is already running costs far more to unwind. Running your plan through this frame while adoption is still being evaluated is the cheapest moment to do it.

10.4 Performance systems punish flow improvement

The least visible constraint comes last. Performance review built around individual output gives no credit for reducing waiting time. Reviewing quickly, cleaning up approval rules, recording the reasoning behind decisions - all of it reduces someone else's lead time. None of it appears on a personal scorecard.

So flow improvement usually runs on one manager's goodwill and reverts when that person moves. Unless team-level lead time appears in the evaluation criteria, every prescription in this section is a one-off. Improvement plans that offer technical fixes while ignoring incentives rarely survive six months.

11. What to touch in the first 90 days

Doing everything at once accomplishes nothing. In this order, each step produces the evidence for the next.

  1. Days 0-30. Measure first. Collect submission, first review, merge and deploy timestamps for 20 to 50 changes from the last four weeks. Touch no tools. The single purpose of this window is to fix the waiting share as a number
  2. Days 30-60. Fix exactly one longest wait. If it is reviewer assignment, automate and rotate it. If it is approval, exempt reversible decisions. Touching two at once makes the result uninterpretable
  3. Days 30-60, in parallel. Set a batch ceiling. A change-size limit and moving automated checks earlier. This does not interfere with the other work, so it can run alongside
  4. Days 60-90. Fill in the spec side. An acceptance criteria template, decision records, prohibitions. Start with the areas that produced the most rework in the previous steps
  5. At day 90. Pull the five metrics again. Put the three throughput numbers and the two instability numbers on one screen. If rework rate rose, stop the speed work and grow inspection capacity first
  6. After that. Now you have grounds to decide whether to expand the tool budget. Reverse the order and you are spending before you have evidence

The point of this sequence is that step one contains no tool adoption. The tools are already installed. What is missing is a number describing where the flow is blocked.

12. What could not be verified

The following were not stated as figures in the body because a verifiable primary source could not be secured.

  • Effect size of each of the seven capabilities. The names and direction of the seven items in the DORA AI capabilities model are public, but per-item coefficients or relative contribution require the report body and are not visible in the summary pages
  • Full names of DORA's seven team profiles. Two of them, the foundational challenges group and the harmonious high achievers, appear in public summaries; the names and distribution of the remaining five could not be confirmed
  • Published lead time measurements from Korean organizations. No public domestic dataset measuring before and after AI adoption on a consistent basis was found. The Korea-specific material here rests on regulatory documents and structural analysis, not on domestic measurements
  • Reproducibility of the METR result with current tooling. The figure came from early-2025 tools, and METR announced a change to its experiment design in February 2026. Whether the direction holds with the current generation is not established by any public result found
  • Sample composition behind satisfaction surveys of tech workers. High percentages reporting that AI helped at work circulate widely, but only figures published with the surveying body and sample composition were cited here. The rest were left out

13. Conclusion: adding another tool is usually not the answer

The difference between organizations that actually got faster and those that merely adopted is observable. You do not need vague words like maturity or culture. The waiting share of lead time, the rework rate, and whether an incident produced an approval or a check. Those three almost always tell you which kind you are looking at.

To summarize.

  • AI shortens hands-on time. But most of lead time is waiting time, and tools do not reach it
  • If output rises while inspection capacity stays flat, instability rises. Watching throughput metrics alone will make you read that as improvement
  • Redefine the scope of approval, not the count. The criterion is reversibility
  • Documents changed from artifacts humans read into inputs a model consumes. What has to grow is the density of recorded reasoning, not the page count
  • Perception is genuinely wrong. A satisfaction survey is not evidence of outcomes
  • The bottleneck sits somewhere different at each scale. Small teams start with documents, large organizations with standard path quality

One last common misconception. We already adopted everything is not a status. Adoption rate no longer differentiates anyone: 90 percent of DORA respondents are already using it. Only one question remains. How did change lead time and deployment rework rate move compared with last quarter? If you cannot produce those two numbers right now, the next thing to buy is not a tool. It is instrumentation.
