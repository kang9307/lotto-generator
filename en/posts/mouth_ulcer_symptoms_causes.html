Mouth Ulcer Symptoms and Causes - The Three Types of Aphthous Ulcer and What Changes at Two Weeks

A condition that usually heals on its own, and how to spot the one that has left that course behind

Aug 18, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before anything else. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, see a healthcare professional. Nothing below should be used to diagnose yourself or to postpone getting checked.

The inside of your cheek has gone white in a small crater with a red rim. It catches every time you chew, and cold water stings it. The pain peaks after a few days, and somewhere around day ten it is simply gone. That is where most mouth ulcers end.

So this subject needs two stories told at once. The first is knowing the ordinary course precisely. Aphthous stomatitis is described as affecting up to 25% of the world population, and most episodes resolve without any treatment at all. The second is noticing the ulcer that has left that course behind. For an ulcer still present after two to three weeks, public health guidance in Korea and the UK points toward separating out other causes, malignancy included. Which drug to use and when belongs to the person doing the prescribing, so this article does not go there.

The ulcer that has outlasted two weeks - why this comes first

There is a reason for putting this ahead of everything else. Read the description of the common condition first and your judgement tilts toward mine is probably that one too. On the odds that is correct. But the item with the highest cost of being wrong sits outside those odds.

Signs worth not postponing — if any of the following applies, the ulcer has stepped outside the ordinary aphthous course. Do not settle it yourself; have it looked at by a dentist or doctor.
  • The ulcer has not healed after two to three weeks
  • It is not shrinking but getting steadily larger
  • It hurts little or not at all yet feels hard and firm to the touch
  • There is a red or white patch in the mouth that will not rub away
  • A lump can be felt in the mouth, on the lip, or in the neck
  • Swallowing is difficult, or a hoarse voice does not come back
  • You are losing weight without trying
  • Genital ulcers, or eye redness, pain or changes in vision, appear alongside
  • Fever keeps recurring, or crops of ulcers keep overlapping
  • A child is in enough pain that they cannot eat or drink
This list is not a sign of cancer. UK public health information states plainly that these symptoms are very common, can be caused by other conditions, and having them does not mean you have mouth cancer - but they do need checking.

Why two weeks in particular. Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency health information describes the minor aphthous ulcer, the most common form, as painful but healing within 10 to 14 days without scarring. UK public health information likewise says mouth ulcers should clear up on their own within a week or two. The two statements overlap at two weeks. Two weeks, then, is not a threshold of danger. It is the boundary of the ordinary course.

What happens once that boundary is crossed is also spelled out. The Korean guidance states that when an ulcerative lesion fails to respond to two to three weeks of treatment, a biopsy must be performed to rule out malignancy. UK guidance sends a mouth ulcer lasting more than three weeks to a GP or dentist, and puts the same item at the top of its list of mouth cancer symptoms.

Not healing and being cancer are entirely different statements. The large majority of long-lasting ulcers are not cancer. There is simply no way to confirm that by looking. A biopsy is what does that job, and a biopsy is not a diagnosis so much as a process of exclusion.

What aphthous stomatitis actually is

Stomatitis is not the name of a single disease but an umbrella for inflammation of the oral mucosa. Korean health information splits it into infectious forms - herpetic gingivostomatitis, hand foot and mouth disease, oral candidiasis - and non-infectious ones, including aphthous stomatitis, burning mouth syndrome and radiation mucositis. This article is mostly about the non-infectious side.

Its share is overwhelming. The Korean source describes aphthous stomatitis as the most common oral mucosal disorder at roughly 20% of cases, with a higher frequency in East Asian populations. Clinical reference material from the US National Library of Medicine puts the figure at up to 25% of the worldwide population, usually first appearing in childhood or adolescence. A family history is present in 24% to 46% of patients.

The mechanism is not infection. Aphthous stomatitis is explained as a T-cell mediated immunological reaction on a base of genetic predisposition. Lymphocytes infiltrate the oral epithelium, oedema develops, keratinocytes undergo vacuolisation, and vasculitis follows. Tumour necrosis factor alpha is described as driving neutrophil chemotaxis and generating an acute inflammatory response.

Where the ulcers appear follows a rule. Aphthous ulcers form on non-keratinised mucosa - the inside of the cheeks and lips, under the tongue, the floor of the mouth. They are described as less common on keratinised surfaces such as the hard palate and gingiva. That single rule becomes the axis of the differential further down.

One observation is routinely misread. Clinical reference material notes that aphthous stomatitis is less common in keratinised areas of the oral mucosa, including those of tobacco smokers. That is not a reason to smoke. Tobacco is named repeatedly as a leading risk factor for oral cancer.

Three types - minor, major and herpetiform

Aphthous ulcers of the same family run quite different courses. The practical value of knowing the types is not in labelling but in judging whether your ulcer is still inside the expected range.

Two things are worth reading off that table. First, duration alone cannot settle the type. Major ulcers run 5 to 10 weeks, so passing the three-week mark does not automatically make an ulcer something else. But that call belongs to clinical assessment, which is precisely why the two-to-three-week mark is used as the biopsy threshold: there is no way to separate a major aphthous ulcer from a malignant lesion by appearance alone.

Second, the name herpetiform does not mean herpes. It describes the clustered appearance, not a viral cause; aphthous stomatitis itself is not described as an infection. One more point: clinical reference material notes that major aphthous ulcers are the most frequent type in patients with AIDS. That is why repeated large, slow ulcers prompt a look at immune status.

What gets blamed, and how thick the evidence is

Aphthous stomatitis is defined in clinical reference material as a chronic oral mucosa inflammatory disorder with an uncertain etiology. So the items below are less causes than triggers acting on a predisposition - and the evidence behind each is of very different thickness.

The most important row in that table is stress. In popular accounts stress is treated as almost the only cause, yet by evidential thickness it sits below genetic predisposition and nutritional deficiency. Being listed as a predisposing factor and being demonstrated as a cause are different tiers. Reducing stress is worthwhile for other reasons, but blaming it while skipping the things that can actually be checked is the real loss.

SLS is a surfactant common in toothpaste. The evidence splits. A meta-analysis of four randomised trials found SLS-free toothpaste significantly reduced ulcer number, duration, number of episodes and pain, while one larger individual trial found no difference in ulcer number or episodes. All the included trials were crossover designs and the total participant count is in the low hundreds. Not conclusive, but changing toothpaste carries essentially no risk, so there is no reason to talk anyone out of trying - that is the honest summary.

Aphthous ulcers favour non-keratinised mucosa such as the inner cheek and lip, while recurrent herpes labialis tends toward keratinised surfaces such as the vermilion border and hard palate. That rule adjusts the odds; it is not a verdict. An ulcer that persists is not something site and appearance can make safe.

When it signals something systemic - Behcet disease as the anchor

Clinical reference material notes that aphthous-like ulcers can be a sign of bowel disease, including Crohn disease, gluten-sensitive enteropathy and ulcerative colitis. HIV infection, cyclic neutropenia, and a childhood syndrome combining periodic fever, pharyngitis, aphthae and cervical adenitis are also listed as associated conditions. Of these, the one tied most directly to mouth ulcers is Behcet disease.

  • Oral ulcers occur in 97% to 99% of patients with Behcet disease, often as the first clinical feature
  • More than 90% of those oral ulcers heal without scarring - meaning the mouth ulcer alone is indistinguishable from an ordinary aphthous one
  • Genital lesions are seen in more than 80% of patients, and more than 70% of them heal with scarring
  • Eye involvement is reported in more than 50%, and hypopyon uveitis or retinal involvement carries a high risk of blindness
  • A positive pathergy reaction, papules or pustules forming 24 to 48 hours after a needle prick, is seen in 60% to 70% of patients from Turkey and Japan but rarely in patients from Northern Europe or the United States
  • Prevalence varies sharply by region: 420 per 100,000 in Turkey against 5.2 per 100,000 in the United States

The classification criteria mirror that structure. The long-standing international study group criteria require at least three episodes of oral aphthous or herpetiform ulceration within twelve months, plus two of the following: recurrent genital ulcers that heal with scarring, ocular lesions, or skin lesions. The international criteria published in 2014 use points instead: 2 points each for oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis and ocular lesions, 1 point each for skin lesions, central nervous system involvement and vascular manifestations, 1 point for a positive pathergy test, and classification at 4 points or more. Sensitivity was reported at 94.8% and specificity at 90.5%, trading a little specificity against the earlier criteria (85.0% and 96.0%) for a large gain in sensitivity.

One conclusion follows. A mouth ulcer on its own does not make Behcet disease. A quarter of the population gets aphthous ulcers; Behcet disease is incomparably rarer. The axis is what accompanies it - genital ulcers, eye symptoms, skin lesions, joint symptoms - and that judgement happens in clinic.

Relief - what has evidence and what does not

The evidence for topical drugs is thinner than expected

Clinical reference material places topical corticosteroids as first-line treatment, alongside chlorhexidine mouthwash and anti-inflammatory drugs. For severe cases a short course of systemic steroid is described, with explicit limits on dose and duration. What to use, whether you can use it and for how long are all decisions for a doctor or pharmacist, so this article only names them.

The evidential landscape has not settled. One network meta-analysis of topical interventions pooled 72 trials, 5,272 participants and 29 topical interventions, and reported the following.

  • Better than placebo for healing - honey, insulin liposome gel, laser, amlexanox, glycyrrhiza and triamcinolone
  • Significant for reducing lesion size - triamcinolone, probiotics, glycyrrhiza and amlexanox
  • Significant for symptom reduction - triamcinolone, laser, glycyrrhiza, amlexanox and aloe
  • Prolonged the interval between ulcers - probiotics and chlorhexidine
  • High risk of adverse events - doxycycline and penicillin

Yet another network meta-analysis on the same question failed to show any statistically significant difference, either between topical treatments or against placebo. It is more accurate to leave that disagreement visible than to hide it. This field is not at a point where any one agent can be called clearly effective. And most of the list above concerns short-term symptom control, not prevention of recurrence.

Reducing irritation is far simpler

The items UK public health information lists are less treatment than protection from re-injury. Given how many aphthous ulcers begin with local trauma, that is a practical axis.

  • Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and drink cool drinks through a straw
  • Choose softer foods and keep a balanced diet
  • Have regular dental check-ups
  • Do not eat very spicy, salty or acidic food
  • Avoid rough, crunchy food such as toast or crisps, and hot or acidic drinks
  • Avoid toothpaste containing sodium lauryl sulphate

Salt water and strongly acidic topicals

Two widely used remedies deserve separating out, because being widely used and being evidence-backed are different things.

Salt water rinsing. UK public health information lists a salt mouthwash among the things a pharmacist can suggest without prescription. But no clinical evidence confirming that it speeds the healing of aphthous ulcers was found, and the literature notes that no recommended salt concentration safe from tissue damage has been established. It is a low-harm habit rather than a verified treatment.

Strongly acidic topicals. Policresulen topical solution, long used for mouth ulcers in Korea and elsewhere, is a strong acid with a pH of roughly 0.6. Case reports describe coagulative necrosis of the epithelium with a white sloughing pseudo-membrane over the resulting ulcer, and erosion of tooth enamel from the high acidity, when it is applied incorrectly. The sense that stinging means working is where the accidents come from. Pain intensity is not a measure of healing. Whether to use it, and how, is best left to a doctor or pharmacist.

If they keep coming back

Clinical reference material calls aphthous stomatitis a clinical diagnosis while also stating that excluding other causes such as Behcet disease, nutritional deficiencies and inflammatory bowel disease is necessary. The tests proposed for that purpose are specific.

  • Complete blood count
  • Red cell folate
  • Ferritin
  • Serum vitamin B12

Why those four. Haematinic deficiencies are described as occurring twice as often in people with recurrent aphthous stomatitis. The mechanism runs like this: insufficient haematinics lead to anaemia, a reduced capacity of the blood to oxygenate the oral mucosa, and consequent atrophy of the oral epithelium. Zinc deficiency is also found in some patients.

A common misreading follows here. Saying that correction helps people who are deficient is not saying everyone should take supplements. Taking them untested yields nothing if you are not deficient and, more importantly, can mask a separate cause of the deficiency. Testing comes first.

Beyond the nutritional markers, other things get looked at alongside.

  • Chronic diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss or anaemia - Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis and gluten-sensitive enteropathy are listed as associated
  • Genital ulcers or eye symptoms - the central axis of the Behcet classification criteria
  • Recurrent fever and infection - cyclic neutropenia is described with severe gingivitis and aggressive periodontitis
  • In a child, periodic fever with pharyngitis and cervical adenitis together
  • Repeated large ulcers - grounds for considering immune status
  • Every medicine and supplement being taken - ulceration is reported with nicorandil, methotrexate and some anti-inflammatory drugs. Rapid healing after withdrawal of the responsible drug is characteristic, but whether to stop anything is the prescriber's decision

And the cheapest thing that is most useful in clinic is a record. Note when, how many, where, how many days to heal, and what it coincided with, for a few months, and the recurrence interval and triggers start to become visible. It beats a history reconstructed from memory.

What to bring to the appointment

Consultations are short. Having the following ready makes that time go much further.

  • The date it appeared and how many days it has been
  • Whether it is shrinking or growing, plus the number and exact location
  • Whether it hurts - painlessness is information too
  • How often it recurs, at what interval, and which episode this is
  • Accompanying symptoms - genital ulcers, eyes, skin, joints, fever, digestive symptoms, weight change
  • Every medicine and supplement, plus smoking and alcohol
  • Any repeating irritant such as recent dental work, an appliance or a broken tooth
  • What you have already tried and how it responded

Where to go depends on the mix of symptoms. UK public health information directs an ulcer lasting more than three weeks to a GP or a dentist. Dentists look inside mouths more often than anyone else, so unusual lesions are frequently picked up there first. If genital ulcers or eye symptoms are part of the picture, say so in the first sentence. That combination changes the direction of the consultation.

It also helps to know a biopsy may come up. Korean guidance states explicitly that when an ulcer fails to respond to two to three weeks of treatment, a biopsy must be performed to rule out malignancy. Being offered a biopsy is not a notification that cancer is suspected; it is the step that removes the possibility. The cost of postponing that step outruns the burden of taking it.

What this article could not confirm

Collected here so that unverified things are not written as verified.

  • Prevalence figures for aphthous stomatitis in Korea - no official national statistic could be pinned down, so only the descriptive statements from the Korean source were carried over, without a separate prevalence number
  • The quantitative relationship between stress and onset - it is listed as a predisposing factor, but no source giving an effect size was found
  • A list of trigger foods - individual variation is reported, but no generalisable list was confirmed
  • Recurrence prevention by probiotics and honey - one network meta-analysis reported significance, another found no difference against placebo. This article does not adjudicate between them
  • The approved product information for policresulen preparations - it could not be opened and is not cited. Only the pH and injury pattern described in case reports are used here; the scarring behaviour of herpetiform ulcers was also absent from the sources, so that cell is left blank

Not confirmed does not mean safe, and it does not mean ineffective.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a mouth ulcer normally take to heal?

The most common minor aphthous ulcer is described in Korean public health information as healing within 10 to 14 days without scarring, and UK public health information says mouth ulcers generally clear on their own within a week or two. Major ulcers over 10mm run 5 to 10 weeks and often leave scarring, so duration alone cannot settle the type. What is clear is that an ulcer past two to three weeks has left the ordinary course behind, and at that point other causes need separating out.

Are mouth ulcers contagious?

Stomatitis contains both contagious and non-contagious conditions. Aphthous stomatitis is described as a T-cell mediated immunological reaction rather than an infection, while herpetic gingivostomatitis and hand, foot and mouth disease are classified as infectious. Location gives a clue: aphthous ulcers favour non-keratinised mucosa such as the inner cheek and lip, while recurrent herpes labialis tends toward keratinised surfaces such as the vermilion border and hard palate. Confirming that distinction is a clinical task, not a mirror test.

Will taking iron or vitamins stop mouth ulcers coming back?

There is a difference between people who are deficient and everybody else. Clinical reference material notes that iron, folate, vitamin B12 and zinc deficiencies are found in some patients, and that such deficiencies occur twice as often in people with recurrent aphthous stomatitis. That is why the response to frequent recurrence is a blood test - complete blood count, red cell folate, ferritin and serum vitamin B12 - rather than a supplement. Supplementing without knowing your levels has no established benefit in the absence of deficiency, and what to correct is for a clinician who has seen the results.

What does it mean if genital ulcers or eye symptoms appear as well?

That combination is the central axis of the classification criteria for Behcet disease. Oral ulcers occur in 97% to 99% of patients and are often the first feature; genital lesions appear in more than 80%, and more than 70% of those heal with scarring. Eye involvement is reported in more than 50%, with a high risk of blindness in some forms. The 2014 international criteria give 2 points each to oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis and ocular lesions and classify at 4 points or more. A mouth ulcer on its own does not make up that combination, and the judgement belongs to clinical assessment and testing.

Does a stinging antiseptic make an ulcer heal faster?

Pain intensity is not a measure of healing. Policresulen topical solution, long used for mouth ulcers in Korea and elsewhere, is a strong acid with a pH of roughly 0.6, and case reports describe coagulative necrosis of the epithelium with a white sloughing pseudo-membrane, plus erosion of tooth enamel, when it is applied incorrectly. Salt water rinsing is also widely recommended, yet no clinical evidence that it speeds healing was found, and no safe recommended concentration has been established. Whether to use either is best left to a doctor or pharmacist.

Conclusion

Understanding mouth ulcers takes two gauges rather than a list of disease names. The first is time. The commonest form heals within 10 to 14 days without scarring, and an ulcer still there past two to three weeks sits outside that course. The second is what comes with it. Genital ulcers, eye symptoms, recurring fever, digestive symptoms or weight loss raise the possibility that this is not only a mouth problem.

On causes, expectations are worth adjusting. The condition is defined as having an uncertain etiology, and is explained as triggers such as trauma, nutritional deficiency and hormonal change acting on a genetic predisposition. Stress, the most widely blamed of them, is among the thinnest by evidence. If ulcers keep returning, a blood test, the full medication list and a few months of records will tell you more.

If one thing survives from this article, it is this. An ulcer that has not healed after two to three weeks, one that keeps growing, one that is firm but painless, and a red or white patch that will not rub away are not things appearance can make safe. A biopsy makes that call. And in most cases the result is reassurance. Reassurance costs far less than what postponement risks.

To repeat. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, see a healthcare professional. Where your own symptoms fall, which tests you need and which medicines to use are decisions for your treating clinician. Nothing here should be used to adjust prescribed medication or to postpone being seen.
