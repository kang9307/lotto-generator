Where Don't Build Your Own Holds and Where It Breaks - A Judgment Drawn Through the Text Editor

The same code gets a different verdict depending on where you put it. And an editor gets hard in places you cannot see from the outside

Sep 3, 2026 Dev & Infra

Do not build your own is one of the most quoted pieces of advice in this trade, and it is mostly right. The trouble is that the sentence travels without ever saying how far it is right. The result is that the rule gets ignored exactly where it matters most, and gets used to shut down other people's learning where it carries no weight at all.

This piece is not an attempt to argue against the rule. It is an attempt to decide where the line goes. Drawn in the abstract that line is useless to everybody, so it gets drawn here through a single example, a text editor, followed all the way down. An editor is a thing people are routinely told not to build, and also a thing that leaves something behind only for the people who have built one.

This piece does not recommend or rate any particular editor or project. It passes no judgment on the author of the post that prompted it and reports only what that post states. The claims about data structures and Unicode are limited to what the standards documents and the original papers confirm, and everything that could not be verified is gathered into a single section near the end.

The prompt - fine, I'll build my own text editor

On 1 September 2026 the British developer David Bushell posted an account of building a text editor that runs in the browser, and the following day it was picked up and discussed on a Korean developer community. The starting point is plain enough. An earlier discussion, to the effect that nobody makes editors the way they used to, had struck a chord, and the author went from there to why can't I build my own. The post also notes that an attempt previously blocked by performance had become worth revisiting after moving to a new Mac.

One phrase from the discussion thread stands out. An exercise like this sits somewhere between reinventing the wheel and making soap at home. The comparison is exact, because the two halves demand different judgments. Rebuilding the wheel is waste, full stop. Whether making soap is waste depends entirely on why you are making it. The line this piece is trying to draw sits precisely between them.

What the original post confirms - three routes and what each cost

The post is a record of trying three different ways to build an editing surface in a browser, one after another. Only what the post states is reproduced here.

  • Drawing directly onto a canvas. The cursor, arrow key movement, current line highlighting and character input all got built. The author wrote that canvas gives nothing for free, and listed as missing: text selection, undo and redo history, multi-line paste, and overflow scrolling. He concluded that the approach was entirely inaccessible
  • Using the editable attribute. A plaintext-only editable region brings selection and undo history along from the browser. Attributes such as spellcheck had to be disabled to avoid input latency spikes, and past a certain character count performance became unpredictable. Chromium browsers performed worse than WebKit or Firefox, in the author's observation
  • Using a plain text input area. Far more performant for longer text. In exchange, syntax highlighting had to be layered on separately, applied only to the visible lines. He noted that too many highlights become a performance bottleneck in their own right
  • What was left undone. Tab indentation was handled provisionally by hijacking the tab key to insert two spaces, and virtual scrolling was left as an area for improvement. He also flagged that JavaScript strings and text ranges work in UTF-16 code units, which makes it easy to introduce bugs naively
  • The verdict and the ending. The author described the state of things as looking like 90 percent of a text editor with 1 percent of the features, judged the remaining work straightforward but tedious, and closed by saying he was filing the project away for a rainy day. He also disclosed that the demos still contain what look like UTF-16 related bugs

The most useful thing in that record is the line putting 90 percent and 1 percent side by side. It means that an editor is the kind of program whose surface finishes first and whose substance collapses later.

Where the rule holds - failures that stay quiet and bills that do not

The domains where do not build your own holds hardest share a single property. When they go wrong, they do not go wrong loudly. The screen looks normal, the tests pass, and the result is wrong anyway. By the time the error surfaces, it has usually already spread.

Cryptography is the standard case. The Cryptographic Module Validation Program, run jointly by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, states it flatly. If information is to be cryptographically protected, then the cryptography must be validated, and non-validated cryptography is regarded as providing no protection at all. The fact that your own crypto code runs guarantees nothing whatsoever. In this territory 「it runs」 is not a passing grade.

Dates and times fail for a different reason. Here the correct answer does not live inside the code; it keeps changing outside it. As of September 2026 the IANA Time Zone Database stands at release 2026c, published on 8 July 2026, and the project states that it is updated periodically to reflect changes made by political bodies to time zone boundaries, UTC offsets and daylight saving rules. A time zone calculator you write yourself starts going stale the moment you finish it.

Character encoding has the same shape. Unicode Annex 29, which defines the text segmentation rules, stands as of September 2026 at revision 47 for Unicode 17.0.0, dated 17 August 2025. The rules are refreshed with each version, and a boundary check you wrote by hand does not follow those refreshes. Browser engines and authentication carry both properties at once. The reasons not to rebuild browser-native features are set out in a separate piece, so they are not repeated here.

Authentication and sessionsTesting only the happy path passes. The problem is off the pathOther people's accounts open, and the victims never learn of it

Where the rule breaks - when the goal is understanding, not a product

Read the last column of that table and the nature of the rule becomes visible. It does not attach to code. It attaches to the bill. Which means the complete sentence is not do not build your own but something closer to do not put what you built on a path that other people's data travels through. That distinction is the centre of this piece.

A learning implementation is not on that path. The same file, the same algorithm, the same bug gets a different verdict. Three axes decide it.

  • Is the failure observed? In a learning build, getting it wrong immediately looks wrong on your own screen. In production, being wrong is quiet. The rule bites on the second case
  • Does the failure propagate? The blast radius of a learning failure is one person. A production failure reaches places you do not control
  • What does the failure leave behind? In learning, the failure itself is the output; in production it is pure loss. The same failure rate is an achievement on one side and an incident on the other

One common misreading is worth naming. The label learning project is not what makes code safe. Placement is what makes it safe. The moment a learning crypto implementation gets quietly slipped into an internal tool, it stops having the property that made it a learning project. Conversely, someone who has once implemented the innards of a library by hand uses that library better. The two sentences do not contradict each other. They describe different positions.

Why a text editor, of all things

There is a reason text editors keep turning up among the things people build to learn. As learning projects go, they have several good properties at once.

  • You set the scope yourself. There is no external standard for where an editor ends. Cursor, insert and delete, and from that point on it is an editor
  • It is something you use daily. The first user is you, so the entire requirements gathering step disappears
  • Feedback is immediate. Press a key and the screen changes. With a compiler or a database it takes a long while to learn whether you got it right; with an editor you find out on the spot
  • Intermediate output is useful on its own. Read a file and paint it on screen and you already have a viewer. Stopping partway still leaves something, which is rare

Those very properties then turn into the trap. Because you set the scope, you unconsciously push the hard parts outside it, and because feedback is immediate, you build what is visible first. Drawing a cursor, wiring the arrow keys and inserting characters takes about a day. That is where the sense of 90 percent comes from. The same structure shows up in the piece on what a screen actually does for file work: the visible part and the genuinely hard part do not overlap.

What to keep the text in

The first real obstacle, and not a matter of taste but a function of which operations you perform often. Charles Crowley's 1996 survey of data structures for text sequences, done at the University of New Mexico, lines up six representations for comparison: array, gap, list, line pointers, fixed size buffers and piece tables. Narrowed to the four that come up most often in editor discussions, they split like this.

  • Contiguous array. Holds the whole file in one block. Painting to screen and computing positions are fastest and the code is shortest. In exchange, every character inserted in the middle shifts everything behind it
  • Gap buffer. Also called the split buffer method. It keeps an empty span at the cursor so insertion and deletion there happen without shifting, and it is managed with three descriptors, one for each span and one for the gap. This matches exactly how people actually type, a run of input at one position. In exchange, a cursor jumping far away means moving the gap
  • Piece table. A buffer holding the original file contents is kept read only, newly typed text accumulates in an append-only buffer, and the current document is expressed as a list of pieces pointing into spans of the two. Never touching the original pays off directly for undo and for opening large files
  • Rope. Published by Boehm, Atkinson and Plass in 1995, this treats a string as a tree of concatenations. The authors wrote that traditional implementations of strings are not well suited to such general-purpose use and should be confined to applications with specific and unusual performance requirements. The advantage shows when large chunks of text are cut and joined often
RepresentationGood atBad atWhere it fits in an editor
Contiguous arrayRandom access reads, painting, simplicityInsertion and deletion in the middle. Everything shiftsRead-only viewers, small files
Gap bufferSustained editing around the cursorAlternating between two distant positionsOrdinary editing where a human is typing
Piece tablePreserving the original, undo, opening large filesPosition math gets heavier as pieces multiplyLarge files, editing with deep history
RopeConcatenating and slicing large chunksImplementation difficulty and overhead on small editsMachine-generated bulk edits, collaborative editing

What the learning yields here is concrete. Why existing editors each made different choices, and why some editors suddenly crawl on a large file while others do not, is explained by this one table. That understanding does not arrive from pulling in a library.

Unicode - what it means to move the cursor by one

This is where building an editor most reliably collapses. It collapses not because the algorithm is difficult but because the word one has a different definition at every layer.

There are four layers

The bytes stored in the file, the code units the programming language handles, the code points Unicode defines, and the grapheme cluster a person reads as a single character all produce different numbers. Unicode Annex 29 defines a grapheme cluster as a user-perceived character and notes that there are many cases where such a basic unit is made up of multiple Unicode code points. The same document says grapheme clusters can be treated as the default unit for text selection, arrow key movement and forward deletion, and in the same breath states the limit: grapheme clusters can only provide an approximation of where to put cursors. The standard itself is saying this is not a complete answer.

Hangul is one of the scripts where this surfaces most often. Annex 29 carries dedicated rules preventing a break between leading, vowel and trailing jamo, so several conjoining jamo bind into a single grapheme cluster. Emoji join several pictures with a zero width joining character, and there the standard states that each emoji sequence is a single grapheme cluster. UTF-16 then adds another layer. According to the official Unicode FAQ, characters outside the Basic Multilingual Plane are represented by a pair of surrogates, leading surrogates running from U+D800 to U+DBFF and trailing surrogates from U+DC00 to U+DFFF. That is why the length of a JavaScript string disagrees with intuition.

There is also the problem that the same character can be stored in more than one way. Unicode Annex 15 defines four normalization forms and describes canonical equivalence as a fundamental equivalency between characters or sequences of characters which represent the same abstract character. The purpose of normalization is to ensure that equivalent strings have a unique binary representation. Hangul decomposition and composition are handled algorithmically rather than by table, which the same document notes significantly reduces memory storage. For an editor this means that a string read from a file and a string arriving from the keyboard can look identical and still compare as different.

Delete one unit by the code unit measure and, as the output above shows, an orphaned surrogate is left behind, and that string is no longer well-formed text. The bugs the author of the prompting post disclosed in his demos are of this family.

Line endings - the dullest place and the one most often wrong

Line endings look dull enough to skim past in a learning project, and they are one of the places where real work most often goes wrong. Start with the definition. POSIX defines a line as a sequence of zero or more non-newline characters plus a terminating newline character. The newline is therefore not a separator between lines but a terminator of one. Characters left at the end of a file with no newline get their own name, incomplete line. Below is a check of what that definition changes, run on Windows 10 with Git Bash, GNU bash 5.2.37.

$ printf 'a\nb\n' > with_nl.txt      # final line has a newline
$ printf 'a\nb'   > without_nl.txt   # final line has none
$ printf 'a\r\nb\r\n' > crlf.txt     # CRLF

$ wc -l with_nl.txt without_nl.txt crlf.txt
 2 with_nl.txt
 1 without_nl.txt
 2 crlf.txt

$ wc -c with_nl.txt without_nl.txt crlf.txt
 4 with_nl.txt
 3 without_nl.txt
 6 crlf.txt

$ od -c crlf.txt
0000000   a  \r  \n   b  \r  \n

A file that reads as two lines to a person counts as one for want of a single newline. Build an editor and a decision has to be made right here. Do you add a newline to the last line on save, restore one the user deleted, or preserve whatever the original had? All three have a case, and if you do not choose, the behaviour is arbitrary.

  • If CRLF and LF are mixed in one file, what is the reference? A majority vote, the first line, or remembering each line separately
  • Should line endings in pasted text be converted to the document's convention or left alone
  • With soft wrapping on, up and down arrows have to follow either the file's lines or the screen's lines, and the two diverge
  • A single trailing newline generates noise in diffs. This is where the problem shows up most often in collaboration

Undo - what an undo stack actually demands

Undo looks like piling edit records onto one list and popping them off. Build it and the requirements multiply.

  • What counts as one unit? Undoing one character at a time is unusable. Runs of input have to be coalesced, and you must decide where a run breaks. Candidates: an arrow key press, a time gap, a different kind of edit arriving
  • The cursor and the selection have to be restored too. Restore only the text and leave the cursor and the user cannot find what changed back. Undo is not a text operation, it is state restoration
  • What happens to redo after you undo and then type? Most editors discard that branch. Keeping it as a tree is a valid choice, and then you inherit a new problem, showing that tree to the user
  • The relationship to saving. Undo past the save point and the document differs from what is on disk. How the modified indicator is computed gets decided here

The data structure discussion comes back at this point. In the piece table family, where the original is never modified and only the piece list is updated, undo becomes restoring an earlier state of the piece list. In a structure that mutates a contiguous array in place, you have to design separately what to record and how much. The prompting post's remark that using the browser's editable attribute brings undo history along belongs to the same theme. That is not a convenience; it is inheriting a design decision somebody else already made, whole.

Large files - designing for not loading it all

Handle only small files and this section is unnecessary. But start using an editor for real and something large, a log or a data dump, inevitably arrives. At that moment the premise that you load it all into memory falls apart.

  • Paint only the visible lines. A million line document still shows a few dozen lines. Not painting the rest is the first optimization. The prompting post left virtual scrolling as an area for improvement
  • Keep the original in the file and only point at it. The piece table's read-only original buffer fits here directly. You can express the editing state without copying the whole file
  • But knowing which line you are on requires an index. Computing the scrollbar position needs the total line count, and getting that needs one pass over the file. Opening instantly and showing accurate line numbers pull against each other
  • Search demands the whole thing again. It has to reach past the screen, so much of the benefit of lazy loading evaporates. With regular expressions, more so
  • Syntax highlighting demands context. To start painting from the middle you still have to look backwards to know whether that point is inside a string or a comment. Which is where designs that store per-line state come from

There is a reason this section is particularly valuable in a learning project. This is the first place where requirements conflict with each other. The earlier sections each had an answer; with large files, opening fast and displaying accurately cannot both hold. Why existing editors switch some features off on a large file becomes understandable here.

Terminal control - you must not receive input a line at a time

Build an editor that runs in a terminal and this is the first gate. A terminal in its default state does not suit an editor. POSIX splits input processing into two modes, and in canonical mode terminal input is processed in units of lines, a line being delimited by a newline, an end-of-file character or an end-of-line character. Which means the program receives nothing until the user presses enter.

So you switch to non-canonical mode. The same specification states that in non-canonical mode input bytes are not assembled into lines, and erase and kill processing shall not occur. How many bytes must accumulate before a read completes, and how long to wait, are set by two control values, the time value having a granularity of 0.1 seconds. What is commonly called raw mode is this non-canonical mode with several flags turned off alongside.

  • Turn off canonical processing. With it set, the erase and kill edit functions operate and input is assembled into lines. The editor has to do that work itself
  • Turn off echo. With it set, input characters are echoed back to the terminal. The editor decides what to draw and where, so it must not double up
  • Decide about signals. With the signal flag set, the interrupt, quit and suspend characters are each handled as signals. Using those key combinations as editor commands takes a deliberate decision
  • Put it all back when you exit. Skip this and the user's terminal is left in a strange state after the program dies. It is the first accident anybody has in a learning project

Drawing the screen is handled by control sequences. The standard representation of cursor movement and screen clearing is defined by ECMA-48, formally titled Control functions for coded character sets, currently in its 5th edition of June 1991 and corresponding internationally to ISO/IEC 6429. That a specification more than thirty years old is still current is itself part of the lesson. Then you meet the problem that repainting the entire screen on every keystroke flickers, and you end up inventing incremental redraw yourself. It is the same layer as the piece on keeping a terminal permanently open.

What to build and what to borrow

By this point the boundary can be drawn. Two criteria. First, will I notice if this is wrong? Second, does the right answer keep changing outside my code? If either answer is bad, borrow.

PartVerdictReason
Text storage structureBuild itErrors show on screen immediately. The rules do not change outside
Cursor movement and selectionBuild itThis is what gives the editor its character, and the learning payoff is largest
Undo stackBuild itThere is no single answer and every product differs. Good design practice
Grapheme cluster boundariesBorrowRules refresh with each Unicode version, and errors break quietly in specific languages only
NormalizationBorrowSame reason. Standard implementations cover the algorithmic parts too
Encoding detection and conversionBorrowEnglish tests pass. Data gets corrupted in somebody else's language
Screen control sequencesIt dependsThe spec is fixed but terminal implementations diverge. Build to learn, borrow to ship
Syntax highlighting rulesBorrowThey differ per language and keep changing. Not yours to maintain

What deserves attention in that table is that everything in the borrow column is data. Unicode rules, encoding tables, language grammars all change with time. Everything in the build column, by contrast, is design. The distinction transfers well beyond editors. Borrow what changes, build what you decide.

What is left when you are done

Honestly, no usable editor is left. The 90 percent and 1 percent verdict is the accurate summary. What is left is a person who knows why existing editors look the way they do. That is not vague consolation; it is a payoff you can itemize.

  • You know why an editor switches certain features off on a large file. Not laziness, but the conflict seen in the section above
  • When a program handles backspace strangely on emoji or conjoining characters, you can guess which unit it is counting wrong
  • You know why a single trailing newline puts noise in a diff, and which setting to go look at
  • You know why a screen is wrecked after a terminal program dies abnormally, and what has to be restored
  • Reading the docs of a text library, you get the habit of first checking whether a function counts code units, code points or grapheme clusters
  • When filing a feature request against somebody else's editor, you have a sense of how expensive the request is

Every one of those is the ability to use other people's code better. It does not conflict with the rule against building your own; it is what lets you follow that rule properly. Borrowing without knowing what you are borrowing and borrowing while knowing what is inside are not the same act.

A project you are allowed not to finish

If the goal is learning, completion is not a condition. It sounds like self-consolation and it is structurally correct. If the purpose is understanding, then the moment the understanding arrives is the completion point, and what follows is, in the prompting post's phrase, straightforward but tedious. That is a different kind of training. There is a difference, though, between simply dropping something and filing it away. Whether anything gets recovered is what separates them.

  • Write down how far you got. One paragraph on what works and what does not. Without it, reopening the thing months later means rereading everything from scratch
  • Write down why you stopped. Stopping for lack of time and stopping because the design was stuck lead to different decisions next time
  • Write what you learned outside the code. The code gets forgotten, but a note on why you did it that way carries into other projects
  • Deciding not to finish is also a decision. Separating postponed from shelved reduces the guilt later

What the condition of having no request does to the work itself is covered in a separate piece: the absence of a completion criterion and a judge is freedom and a bill at the same time.

What changes the moment someone else uses it

This is where the line actually falls. The same code has a different character sitting on your own machine and sitting in somebody else's hands. An editor is a kind of program where that shift is especially large, because it holds a person's only copy.

  • Data loss becomes immediate. An interrupted save can take both the original and the new contents with it. You need write-to-temp and atomic replace, which is barely thought about at the learning stage
  • Misjudge the encoding and the original breaks the moment you save. Reading it wrong can be fixed by rereading; saving under a wrong judgment cannot be undone
  • Line ending conversion rewrites somebody else's whole file. One line changed and the entire file showing as modified wrecks that person's collaboration
  • Bug reports start arriving. From that point the work has a requester, and the freedom that was the learning project's greatest asset is gone
  • A security surface appears. Handling file paths, attaching extensions and opening remote files are each an attack surface. The quietly-wrong domains from earlier reappear right here

None of this says letting other people use it is wrong. It says do it knowing the criteria change at that moment. Learning projects becoming products is a common path, and a project that made that transition deliberately ends differently from one that drifted into it.

Three questions to answer first

When you are weighing whether to build it yourself, three questions decide the answer. The order matters.

  • Will I notice if this is wrong? If you will notice, build it. If it is one of the quietly-wrong domains, do not. Cryptography and authentication get caught by this question
  • Is the goal a product or understanding? If you need a product, use what exists. If understanding is the goal, building is the fastest route. Answer this one honestly. Saying understanding is the goal while actually wanting the product is the most common way this fails
  • Will other people use it? If not, the first two answers are enough. If so, the bar rises regardless of them. Data loss, maintenance and security become yours from then on

Answer those three and do not build your own stops being a prohibition and becomes a conditional rule. Know the conditions and you can both keep the rule and break it legitimately.

Summary

Do not build your own is a rule that attaches to the bill, not the code. The domains where it holds hardest are the ones that fail quietly or whose right answer keeps changing outside your code, and cryptography, authentication, dates, character encoding and browser engines all live there. In that territory, the fact that something runs guarantees nothing. Conversely, the same rule breaks when the goal is understanding rather than a product, because the same code has a different failure character once it is off the path that other people's data travels.

Text editors get picked as learning projects because you set the scope, you are the first user and feedback is immediate, and it is exactly those properties that mean the surface finishes first and the hard parts are left behind it. Build the whole thing and no usable editor remains; what remains is a person who knows why existing editors look the way they do, and that person uses libraries better. Building it yourself and not building it yourself are not opposites. They are an order of operations, and the line is drawn not at completion but at the moment somebody else uses it.

What I could not verify

  • Crowley's data structures paper was confirmed only as far as its table of contents, abstract and portions of the gap method and piece table sections. The list of six representations, the gap method's alternative name of split buffer, and the fact that the piece table's first buffer holds the original file contents are what was confirmed. The quantitative performance comparisons could not be obtained and were not carried over
  • The ropes paper opened only to the abstract. The publisher and society full texts refused access. So no asymptotic cost figures for concatenation and slicing were used, only the authors' framing of the problem
  • The specification text stating that JavaScript strings operate in UTF-16 code units could not be retrieved, so direct measurement was used instead, with the environment stated in the body
  • The grapheme cluster counts here come from ICU 77.1 as bundled with Node.js v22.16.0, which is Unicode 16.0. The rules document cited is the Unicode 17.0.0 revision of Annex 29, so some boundary determinations may differ across versions
  • The per-browser performance differences with the editable attribute are the prompting author's observation and were not reproduced. This piece does not rate any browser on that basis
  • The claim that text editors are the most frequently chosen learning project is widespread but no supporting tally could be confirmed. So no claim about frequency is made here, only about the properties an editor has as a learning project
