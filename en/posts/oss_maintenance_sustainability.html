Who Keeps the Infrastructure Running - When the Spec Survives but the Implementation Stops

A protocol, its implementations and the organization that maintains them are separate layers. Any one of them stopping changes the arithmetic for everyone downstream

Aug 26, 2026 Dev & Infra

Specs live in documents. Implementations are kept alive by people

Picture the dependency list your service is carrying right now. For how many of those entries could you name the person currently fixing the code? For most of them you could not, and for years nothing bad comes of that. The trouble is that those years do not renew themselves automatically.

In August 2026, Interplanetary Shipyard, the engineering organization that had been carrying the major implementations and public infrastructure of the distributed storage protocol IPFS, announced that it would end its IPFS engineering, maintenance and infrastructure operations on September 30, 2026. The stated reason is that Protocol Labs informed Shipyard it would not be renewing its funding.

What makes this worth reading is not the future of IPFS. It is that the situation shows, unusually clearly, how a specification can be perfectly alive while using it becomes practically harder. What we import every day is not a spec but an implementation that somebody maintains, and those people are paid from somewhere. The moment that payment stops, the health of the spec is no comfort.

To be explicit: this article does not judge any organization's decision. Nothing has been published about the reasoning behind the funding call, and guessing at it is not this article's job. What is on the table here is the arithmetic for the side that depends. Which layer stopped, what actually pays for maintenance, and on what grounds to re-examine the dependencies you already carry.

What was announced in August 2026 - the confirmed scope

Start by narrowing the scope to what the notice actually says.

ItemWhat the notice states

The thing to notice in that table is that what ends is not the code but the act of caring for it. The repositories stay. The licenses stay. What goes away is the person accountable for new features, bug fixes and releases, plus the public services that generate a bill every month. Nowhere does it say the protocol is being retired.

That distinction is the starting point for everything below. “The protocol continues” and “the thing I was using continues” are not the same sentence.

Protocol, implementation and maintainer are three different layers

News like this usually gets misread in one of two directions. One camp reads it as “that technology is dead,” the other as “the spec is untouched, so nothing happened.” Both errors come from not separating the layers.

There are at least three, and each has its own lifespan and its own failure mode.

  • The specification — a document. If it is written well enough for a person to read and implement, it effectively never dies. But a document does nothing on its own. Saying the spec is alive is a statement about possibility: somebody could implement it again.
  • The implementation — code. You may keep using it for as long as the license allows. Yet code sitting still does not mean the environment is. Major language runtimes, security patches in dependencies, operating system ABIs, container base images and certificate practices all move. The code did not stop; the world walked past it.
  • The maintaining organization — people and a budget. When only this layer stops, the two above it depreciate over time, because updating the spec, keeping the implementation current and receiving vulnerability reports are all work done here.

In practice there is a fourth thing attached: public services somebody else operates. Gateways, bootstrap nodes, public registries, free API endpoints. That layer costs money every single day, so it stops first and without any grace period. The fact that domains and bootstrap nodes appear in that table right alongside the implementations is the proof.

Once the layers are separated, judgment gets simple. Only the spec left means re-implementation is possible. The implementation left means it runs today. No maintaining organization means the running state now has an expiry date. Without sorting out which one stopped, you can only overstate or understate the situation.

What actually pays for open source maintenance

To understand the maintaining layer you have to look at how money reaches it. Widely used open source generally runs on one of the following five arrangements or a mix of them, and each breaks at a different point.

Funding structureWhere the money comes fromStructural point of failureSignal visible from outside
Single corporate sponsorOne company's budget, or developers it employsOne strategy change ends everything. The decision sits outside the projectCommit authors clustered at one employer
Foundation or consortiumMember dues and donationsSupplies legal identity, trademark and governance, not salaries. Budget shrinks when members leaveMember roster, whether budgets are published
Public grantsGovernment or public agency budgetsAwarded in rounds, so continuity is never guaranteed. A policy shift means no next roundGrant round, stated end date
Commercial side businessHosting, support contracts, open core paid featuresTension between the revenue model and the open scope. License changes originate hereThe line between paid and free features moving
Individual sponsorshipSponsors, crowdfundingRarely reaches salary scale, and the amount swingsGoal and progress on the sponsor page

The numbers make the shape of it clearer. In a Tidelift survey of more than 400 maintainers conducted in mid-2024, 60% reported being unpaid. Of those, 16% described themselves as hobbyists who do not want to be paid, and 44% said they would appreciate being paid. Semi-professionals earning some income from maintenance made up 24%, and full professionals earning most or all of their income from it made up 12%. That 60% figure was identical to the previous year's survey.

On the other side there are attempts to put a price on maintenance itself. According to the European Commission's open source observatory case study, Germany's federally funded Sovereign Tech Fund grew from EUR 3.5 million in 2022 to a projected EUR 17 million in 2025, and by late 2024 had invested roughly EUR 23.5 million across more than 60 projects. Awards run from EUR 50,000 to EUR 1 million, and are explicitly earmarked for security, maintenance and resilience rather than innovation. That a separate instrument had to be created at all says something about who does not pay for this by default.

If you keep one thing from this section, keep this. One funder is one single point of failure. Funding structure never appears on an architecture diagram, but its effect on availability is the same in kind as putting everything in one region.

Why maintenance is harder than building - the recognition gap

Money explains only half of it. The other half is that maintenance work is structurally invisible.

New features land in release notes, go into conference decks and end up on resumes. That CI stayed green for three years is written down nowhere. Incidents that never happened cannot be counted, so doing maintenance well shows up only as nothing having occurred. Every method of counting output favors the side that builds.

Lay the actual work out and it becomes obvious why it burns people down.

  • Issue triage — filtering unreproducible reports, usage questions and duplicates. Not one line of code is added
  • Security intake — arrives over a private channel, has a deadline, and carries liability if the call is wrong. It is the worst possible shape of work for an unpaid volunteer
  • Release engineering — signing, distribution channels, multi-platform builds, regression checks. Automate it and a human still makes the final call
  • Compatibility decisions — deciding what may be broken. Get it right and nobody notices; get it wrong and thousands are angry at once
  • Documentation — the most requested and least contributed area there is
  • User support — the moment it is free, expectations match those for a paid product

What these share is that they are hard to stop and hard to delegate. Maintenance is the first thing budget cuts reach precisely because cutting it produces no visible effect that day. The effect arrives six months to two years later, by which point cause and effect are hard to connect. The same asymmetry explains why treating code review as a maintainability check is so hard to institutionalize.

The risk the consumer inherits - and what a fork actually costs

What really happens when one dependency stops

What follows a maintainer's departure is predictable if you lay it out on a timeline.

  • Months 0 to 3 — nothing happens. This stretch is the most dangerous. Risk is accumulating with no signal, so no one inside the organization has grounds to act
  • Months 3 to 12 — the surroundings start moving. Builds break on the new runtime, advisories appear on transitive dependencies, behavior shifts on a new distribution. Issues get filed and go unanswered
  • Beyond 12 months — a vulnerability is published and there is nobody to patch it. The era of workarounds begins: pinned versions, network isolation, defenses in front of a proxy. Workarounds accumulate and drift away from the original design
  • If you depend on a public endpoint — there is no runway. It stops on the date. Gateways, bootstrap nodes and free APIs live here

Two risks need separating here. Code dependency degrades slowly with time; service dependency falls off a cliff on a specific date. The latter usually hides in the default values of a config file, so it never shows up in the dependency list. That is exactly why it gets missed more often.

Forking is rarely the answer

“It is open source, we can just fork it” is true as a license clause and almost always false as an operating plan. A fork is not a copy of code but an assumption of responsibility, and the list you are assuming is longer than it looks.

  • The entire build, test and release pipeline, plus the machines to run it
  • Package signing keys and distribution channels. You have to rebuild a path users are willing to trust
  • A security intake channel, vulnerability numbering and a disclosure policy
  • The authority to decide whether compatibility may be broken, and somebody to explain that decision
  • Issue triage and user support. The questions arrive at your door the moment you fork
  • Trademarks. Sometimes you cannot keep the name, and once the name changes, search results, docs and tutorials all still point at the old one
  • Ecosystem adoption. Unless other projects make your fork their default, a fork is not meaningfully different from a private patch

Forks that actually worked share some conditions: at least one organization with a clear reason to pay for maintenance, some of the original maintainers coming along, and the capacity to stand up a name and a distribution channel from scratch. Missing any of the three, a fork adds one more repository and quietly stalls. The freedom to fork is a right, not an ability.

How to evaluate a dependency - the checklist

So you evaluate up front. Use the same items when adopting something new and when re-reading what you already run, once a quarter. Every item below is something you can confirm in a repository in about thirty seconds.

If eight is too many, fix an order. Funding concentration first, existence of an alternative implementation second. Putting maintainer count first is a common mistake, because three maintainers at one employer are, from a funding standpoint, one person. And when two or more implementations exist, an exit route survives even if every other item looks bad.

Treat this checklist as a question about exposure, not about trust. It does not ask whether this is a good project; it asks how many days we would need if this stopped. The habit of reading a dependency as a contract rather than an asset is developed further in the piece on code reuse and supply chain risk.

What makes distributed storage a hard subject in itself

That this landed in distributed storage of all fields is more than coincidence. Data permanence is an accounting problem, not a software problem.

Content addressing pins an object's identity to a hash. The same address always refers to the same bytes, so tampering is impossible. But that is a guarantee of integrity, not of existence. If not a single node actually holds those bytes on a disk, the address stays perfectly valid and returns nothing at all.

  • “Nobody's server” is a statement about ownership, not about physics. The data always sits on somebody's disk
  • The mechanism that keeps it there is pinning, and pinning is a recurring bill for power, disk and bandwidth
  • So a promise that something lasts forever carries exactly the same weight as a promise that somebody pays forever
  • The less popular the data, the fewer incidental replicas it accumulates, and the easier it disappears. The paradox is that the data most in need of preservation is the most fragile

Public gateways are the other face of the same structure. A gateway is the bridge from the ordinary HTTP world into the distributed network, and because it is convenient, most traffic funnels through a handful of them. The access path to a distributed protocol becomes centralized. The cost of keeping that bridge up is paid by whoever owns the bridge. Gateway domains appearing next to bootstrap nodes in the notice above is that structure showing its work.

So the question to ask about distributed storage is not how elegant the protocol is. It is how many nodes hold my data, how many of those I control, and who is paying for the rest. If you cannot answer, that data is not stored so much as briefly cached.

What the consuming side can do now

That is the diagnosis; what remains is what you can actually do. Listed cheapest first.

  • Keep one copy of the original under your own control — whatever the protocol, whatever the storage, one copy somewhere you hold the account. This single measure is cheaper and more certain than all the others combined
  • Do not leave someone else's endpoint as the default — find the gateway, bootstrap and mirror addresses in your config and check whether you could run your own. Knowing you can switch matters even if you do not switch today
  • Pin versions and make builds reproducible — if you can rebuild today's state after upstream stops, you have bought yourself time to negotiate
  • Snapshot upstream assets — repository, release archives, documentation, issue list. Doing this after the shutdown notice is sometimes too late
  • Sponsor regularly rather than generously — a small recurring payment from a company budget helps a maintainer plan more than a large one-off from an individual
  • Contribute triage, docs and reproductions before code — these are usually what actually reduces the maintenance load. One minimal reproducible example saves a maintainer hours
  • Name an internal owner — pick one person per critical dependency who knows it. Whether someone can make the call when upstream stops determines your response speed
  • Walk the alternative path once, in advance — writing “an alternative exists” in a document is not the same as having wired it up once. Half a day of experiment saves weeks later

Free convenience always has somebody's invoice attached to it. The premise that the invoice can change hands recurs in the same shape in the piece on who bears the cost.

The other side - not every project needs to be permanent

Read this far and shutting down starts to sound like a bad thing. That conclusion is not quite right. Software has a lifespan, and ending is not a synonym for failing. Projects that served their purpose, projects superseded by something better, projects that were experiments from the start being wound up is closer to a sign of a healthy ecosystem.

The genuinely dangerous state is not a shutdown but something unmaintained that looks maintained. The repository is open, the docs say nothing, and in reality nobody has looked at an issue in two years. An explicit end-of-life notice at least conveys information, and with information users can plan.

Seen that way, the form a good shutdown should take comes out roughly like this.

  • Fix the end date to a calendar date. “For the time being” makes planning impossible
  • Separate what stops from what remains. Code, services and spec work are three different stories
  • Name the owner of domains, repositories and signing keys, and who decides their future
  • State how long security handling remains valid. Without it, users learn about a vulnerability twice as late
  • If handover is welcome, leave a contact path open

The notice in question separated the end date, the scope and the ownership of the assets, and some questions remain undecided alongside it. Those are collected in the next section. In the end it comes back to a design principle. Assume by default that no dependency is permanent, and a shutdown notice becomes a scheduled event rather than an accident. The side that gets surprised is always the side that assumed forever.

What could not be confirmed

Everything this article declined to assert, in one place. Checked as of August 26, 2026.

  • Who receives security reports after September 30 — the notice does not name who triages and fixes vulnerabilities in Kubo, Boxo and Helia. The same question sits on the IPFS community forum, and no published answer was found
  • The fate of the public gateway domains and bootstrap nodes — only that the owner will determine it; whether they keep running was not announced
  • Why the funding was not renewed — no explanation has been published. This article does not speculate about it
  • The organization's budget size and headcount — not found in public sources, which is why no monetary or staffing figures appear in the body
  • Any successor support arrangement — some press reports exist, but none could be confirmed against an official document, so none of it was used
  • Real-world deployment scale per implementation — no verified count of how widely each implementation is deployed was found

Conclusion: how not to lean on what is not being maintained

Three sentences. First, spec, implementation and maintaining organization are separate layers, and what stopped here is the third one plus the public services it operated. Second, the maintaining layer stands on a funding structure, each structure breaks at a different point, and one funder is one single point of failure. Third, what the consuming side can do is not worry about upstream but know in advance how many days it would need if upstream stopped.

If you pick one thing to do, take your three most important dependencies and check just two lines of the checklist above. Where the money comes from, and whether an alternative implementation exists. Anything you cannot answer becomes the first line of next quarter's list. Maintenance is undervalued not because it is cheap but because it is invisible. Opening the sponsor page of a library you use daily is not a sentimental gesture; it is checking the funding structure of your own infrastructure.
