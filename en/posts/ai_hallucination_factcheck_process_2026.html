Filtering AI Hallucination With a Process, Not Personal Skill

Claim extraction, source demand, primary-source traceback, verdict - building fact-checking into the workflow

Jul 27, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Read carefully is not a control

Incidents caused by shipping AI-written text unchecked are no longer curiosities. In the legal field alone there is a public database devoted to collecting only those matters in which a court explicitly found reliance on fabricated citations, and its count keeps climbing. In October 2025 a major professional services firm partially refunded the Australian government after its commissioned report was found to contain non-existent academic papers and a fabricated quotation from a federal court judgment.

These incidents share one uncomfortable trait: the people involved were not especially lazy or ignorant. In the 2023 Mata v. Avianca matter before the Southern District of New York, the attorney did grow suspicious of the cases the chatbot had produced, and he did try to confirm them. He simply asked the same chatbot. It told him they were real. The outcome was a Rule 11 violation and sanctions. Attention was paid; it was just paid without structure.

That is the argument of this article. Hallucination cannot be stopped by individual care. It has to be stopped by procedure. Care fluctuates with fatigue, deadlines, seniority and mood, and above all it does not reproduce. There is no guarantee that the person who caught an error yesterday catches it today, and far less guarantee that a colleague catches what you caught. A procedure behaves differently. It makes everyone check the same things, and it records what could not be checked.

What this article is not about. How to design a checking pipeline for outputs in general - the syntax, runtime and semantic layers, false-positive management, regression tests, the statistical limits of sampling - is out of scope here. That subject is covered separately in Building a Verification Pipeline for AI Output. This article does exactly one thing: it examines how to confirm individual factual claims inside sentences. This is fact-checking practice, not inspection theory.

The litigation and sanction examples cited below are drawn from publicly reported facts and court records and are used illustratively. They are not legal advice; consult a qualified professional about any specific matter.

1. Hallucination is not one thing - typing it makes the check designable

Before you can build a control you have to decide what it catches. The research literature has settled on two axes. One is intrinsic versus extrinsic; the other is factuality versus faithfulness. Survey papers on hallucination in large language models use these distinctions consistently.

  • Intrinsic hallucination: output that directly contradicts the source text or context supplied to the model. The source says March, the summary says May. With the source in hand this is mechanically detectable.
  • Extrinsic hallucination: output whose truth cannot be judged from the supplied context at all. Background the model added that may or may not be correct. Only external material settles it, and in practice this is where all the real time goes.
  • Faithfulness problems: how tightly the output adheres to the given material. This is the governing metric for summarization, translation and extraction work.
  • Factuality problems: whether the output matches the world. If there is no source, or the source itself is wrong, output can be perfectly faithful and still false.

The distinction matters operationally because the two cost radically different amounts to check. Intrinsic hallucination resolves against the source document, so it automates well and is cheap. Extrinsic hallucination requires a human to go find external evidence, so it is expensive. A working process therefore puts cheap checks first and expensive checks last, and rations the expensive checks with a risk tier. Sections 4 and 5 are those two ideas respectively.

There is one more type practitioners must know: temporal error. A model does not know what changed after its training data ends, and it does not mark that ignorance in the sentence. Tax rates, fee schedules, statutory numbering and product specifications are all values that move, so a stale figure was correct at some point, which makes it pass review far more easily than an outright fabrication. For these items the question is not true or false but as of when.

2. Why models cannot say they do not know

If it does not know, why not just say so? That is the question practitioners ask most often, and the 2025 OpenAI paper Why Language Models Hallucinate answers it with incentives rather than capability. Its thesis is that hallucination is a product of how models are trained and graded. Most benchmarks score in binary: one point for correct, zero for wrong. But an admission of ignorance also scores zero. Under that rule the score-maximizing strategy is obvious - produce a plausible guess for everything. A model optimized to perform well on tests becomes a model that guesses well.

The same paper notes that generation is harder than classification. Even where a model can reliably judge whether a given statement is valid, producing a correct answer from scratch carries a higher error rate. This is why the intuition just have the model review itself is weaker than it sounds. Judgment does outperform generation, but if the judging happens in the same model and the same conversation, it gets dragged along by the answer already on the page.

Layered on top is the calibration problem. Verbalized confidence - the language a model uses to signal certainty - correlates poorly with actual accuracy, and there is a recurring finding that preference-based post-training widens that gap. Specific error magnitudes vary enough across studies and setups that only the direction is reported here, not a number. The operational takeaway is a single sentence: tone is not a reliability signal. A more decisive sentence is not a more accurate sentence.

3. The unit of verification is the claim, not the sentence

The first place fact-checking collapses in real work is the definition of the target. Saying we will review the whole document is the same as deciding nothing. The verifiable unit is neither the document nor the paragraph; it is the claim, the smallest statement whose truth can be judged.

One sentence routinely carries several. Take: introduced in 2024, the scheme covers 70 percent of eligible people and is updated annually by the relevant ministry. That contains at least four claims - the year of introduction, the coverage rate, the responsible ministry, and the update cycle - and each needs its own source. One being right says nothing about the others. Most real incidents come from a single wrong fragment inside a broadly correct sentence.

3.1 Scanning by five markers

Nobody has time to atomize every sentence. In practice you scan for markers first. Any sentence containing one of the following goes on the list without exception.

  • Numbers: amounts, percentages, counts, rankings. Treat the unit and base year as part of the number.
  • Dates and reference periods: effective dates, publication dates, amendment dates. Relative words such as recently and currently are temporal claims too.
  • Proper nouns: institutions, products, cases, people. Check both existence and exact spelling.
  • Statutory and standard identifiers: section numbers, standard numbers, case docket numbers. Identifiers are the easiest thing in the world to fabricate plausibly.
  • Quoted material: anything attributed to someone. Frequently the utterance does not exist, or exists with its context stripped.

3.2 Filtering out what is not a factual claim

Just as important is quickly removing sentences that are not fact-check targets. This approach is better and we recommend adoption are evaluations and recommendations - that is argument, not fact. Such sentences do not need a source hunt; they need a check that reasons are attached and the conclusion follows from them. Mixing the two inflates the list, and an inflated list is a list nobody finishes.

The easiest way to make this stick in a team is to mark it in the document. A rule as simple as factual claims carry a footnote marker and evaluations carry nothing tells the reviewer immediately where to look.

4. The four-step process: extract, demand a source, trace to the primary, judge

This is the core of the article. Fact-checking is fixed into four reproducible steps. It is essentially what the International Fact-Checking Network Code of Principles asks of signatories - use the best available primary rather than secondary sources, check key elements against more than one named source, cite in a way that lets a reader replicate the work, and publish a corrections policy - translated into a document workflow.

StepWhat happensOutputSignal that this step failed
1. Claim extraction Break sentences into the smallest truth-judgeable units. Scan the five markers first. A numbered claim list Entries read like this paragraph rather than a claim
2. Source demand Attach a source to each claim. If there is none, leave it blank. Do not fill it in. A claim-to-source table Source cells contain phrases like commonly known
3. Primary-source traceback Follow the source upward until you reach whoever originally produced the figure. Actually open the links. Primary URL, document title, publication date You stopped at a second- or third-hand citation
4. Verdict Label each claim: confirmed / partially confirmed / unconfirmed / erroneous. A labelled final list Not a single item is labelled unconfirmed

Re-read that last failure signal. A verification result with zero unconfirmed items usually means no verification happened. Honest traceback always leaves a few claims that never reach a primary source. Zero unconfirmed rarely means the check was flawless; far more often it means the bar was loose.

4.1 Why four labels instead of two

True and false are not enough, because the two most common real-world states are the source exists but the figure differs slightly and I simply cannot find it. Forcing those into a binary destroys information.

  • Confirmed: the same value or wording was found in a primary source. Record the URL and publication date alongside it.
  • Partially confirmed: the source is real but its scope, basis or period differs - a national figure cited where the source covers one region, for instance. Here you narrow the claim to match the source, not the other way round.
  • Unconfirmed: no primary source was reached. Delete it from the text, or if it stays, say on the surface that it is unconfirmed.
  • Erroneous: it contradicts the primary source. Fix immediately, and if already published, log a correction.

5. Risk tiers - deciding what gets full coverage and what gets sampled

Verify everything at the same intensity and the process dies within days. So intensity is allocated by claim type, on one criterion: what happens if this is wrong.

TierClaim typeVerification intensityIf it cannot be confirmed
A (full coverage) Numbers such as amounts, rates and counts; dates such as effective and amendment dates; statutory sections; case numbers; quoted material At least one primary source per claim; two cross-checked for headline figures Delete. It cannot stay
B (full coverage, existence) Proper nouns for institutions, products, people and cases; paper and report titles; DOIs and URLs Confirm existence and spelling. Links must actually be opened Replace the proper noun with a generic term, or cut the sentence
C (sampled) Background explanation, definitions, descriptions of general procedure One or two spot checks per section. Any hit promotes that section to full coverage Soften absolute wording and narrow the scope of the statement
D (not a target) Evaluations, recommendations, forecasts, opinion Argument review, not fact-checking. Only ask whether reasons are attached Make it visible in the sentence that this is opinion

The most valuable device in that table is the promotion rule on tier C. One error found in a sample promotes the whole section to tier A treatment, because errors cluster rather than scatter. When a model wanders into territory it knows poorly, several neighbouring sentences degrade together. Having found one, digging around it is statistically the right move.

One local note worth adding: in tier A, an as-of date always rides along. Rates, caps and section numbers are amended often enough that a stale value was frequently correct at some earlier date. So the verification record must carry a period marker such as as of July 2026, and that marker should survive into the published text. A number without a date is a half-finished check.

6. Primary versus secondary sources - why re-citation is dangerous

A primary source is the original document of whoever first produced the fact: the raw release from the surveying or publishing body for statistics, the statute text in the official repository for law, the manufacturer specification for products, the paper itself for research findings. A secondary source is everything that carries it onward - articles, summary reports, vendor blogs, slide decks, and language-model answers.

Secondary sources are dangerous not because they are wrong but because distortion accumulates. Each hop drops a qualifier. The metropolitan-area sample basis falls off, then the 2023 survey date, then the self-reported nature of the responses. After three hops all that survives is a bare number, and a bare number is unusable. Worse, language models have been trained on enormous volumes of secondary material. The more confidently a model states a figure, the more likely it is a figure that got repeated often enough to be stripped of its conditions.

6.1 If only a vendor blog has it, drop the number

One rule makes this judgment fast. If the only source for a figure is a party that benefits from the figure, do not use it. Typical examples are a verification-tool vendor quantifying how risky it is to skip verification, or a supplier publishing the impact of adopting its own product. This is not an accusation of falsehood; it means the figure has not been independently confirmed and therefore cannot serve as evidence. Section 12 applies this same rule to this very article.

6.2 Extra friction in non-English material

Working in a language other than English usually adds a layer to traceback. Articles derived from the same press release cite one another, and raw data often sits inside an appendix table in an attached PDF that search does not index. The practical countermeasure is to memorize the entry points. For law it is the official statute repository; for statistics it is the national statistical portal rather than a ministry press release; for administrative rules it is the official gazette or the ministry legal notices page. With the entry point known, traceback usually finishes within ten minutes - or it establishes within ten minutes that the source cannot be found. Both are useful outcomes.

7. Six checks for detecting fabricated sources

Non-existent papers, cases and reports cause more incidents than any other hallucination type, because they arrive with plausible author names, real-sounding journals and perfectly formatted identifiers. The upside is that this type has several mechanically checkable points.

CheckMethodSigns of fabrication or contamination
Identifier resolution Append the DOI to doi.org and actually open it 404, a redirect to a journal landing page only, or an entirely different paper opening
Metadata match Compare author, year, title, volume and issue of the opened document against the citation, character by character The identifier resolves but author or title differs - a citation assembled from parts of different works
Venue existence Confirm the journal, society or publishing body exists and that the issue was actually published Plausible name, no presence in the field indexes
Author check Verify the author against an institutional page or researcher profile A real person who has never published on that topic
Date coherence See whether the cited content references events postdating publication A 2019 paper discussing a 2024 policy
Retraction status Query the retraction database. Since September 2023 Crossref has held this data and made it openly available Real, but retracted or carrying an expression of concern

Of the six, the one that catches the most in practice is the second, metadata match. A wholly invented identifier dies at check one, but a composite citation that pairs a real identifier with a different title or author opens perfectly and slides past a casual look. The link opened is not a verification. Whether the opened document says what was cited is the verification.

Legal material adds one more: whether the docket number, case name, court and decision date all cohere. In the Mata v. Avianca matter mentioned earlier, the six submitted cases matched professional citation conventions precisely and even carried internal quotations from the supposed opinions. The paradox holds: the more perfect the form, the more the content needs checking.

8. When the source is real but says something else - the gap RAG leaves

There is a widespread expectation that bolting retrieval onto a model removes hallucination. What actually happens is that hallucination changes shape. The preregistered Stanford RegLab evaluation of commercial legal research tools, released in 2024 and subsequently published in a peer-reviewed journal, reported that retrieval-based products still produced inaccurate or misgrounded answers at a substantial rate. Adding retrieval does not by itself guarantee accuracy.

The failures split three ways.

  1. Retrieval failure: the relevant document is never surfaced. Rather than saying no material was found, the model fills the gap from prior knowledge - and because a citation is attached on the surface, the answer looks more trustworthy.
  2. Context misreading: the right document is retrieved but conditions, exceptions and scope limits get dropped in the reading. This concentrates in heavily qualified documents such as statutes, terms of service and technical specifications.
  3. Entity mismatch: the cited document is real and its content is accurate, but it is about something other than what was asked.

The third is the nastiest. A preprint released in July 2026 (not yet peer reviewed) names this phenomenon deceptive grounding in the context of clinical retrieval-augmented generation, reporting that systems present clinical evidence for a different drug than the one queried while passing standard hallucination, faithfulness and citation checks. Because the citation points at a genuine document, an automated checker sees nothing wrong.

What to do about it. Automated citation checkers get as far as is there a source and does the source exist. Is the source about this claim is a human question. That is why step 3 of the four-step process explicitly says open the links. Without opening them this failure type is never caught. Even restricting mandatory link opening to tier A and B claims delivers the best return of any single rule here.

9. Orphan statistics - numbers cited everywhere with no origin

Trace sources long enough and you will meet it: a figure quoted by dozens of places that, followed upstream, leads to nobody. A cites B, B cites C, and C cites A. It is not that the origin is missing; the citation loop itself is the origin.

This predates AI and has a name. It is circular reporting, and the variety that originates on Wikipedia carries the nickname citogenesis, coined by Randall Munroe in a 2011 xkcd strip. The most frequently cited real example is the 2007 obituary episode involving the British composer Ronnie Hazlehurst. An unsourced sentence added to his Wikipedia article was copied into obituaries at several major outlets, after which those obituaries were imported back into Wikipedia as the citation for the claim. Anyone following the source in good faith arrives at respectable press coverage.

Language models amplify this structure. The more often a figure appears in training data, the more confidently the model states it, and those answers become documents that thicken the loop further. Which means widely cited is not grounds for trust; it is grounds for suspicion.

The handling is simple.

  • Attempt traceback to three hops. If three hops do not reach an institutional original, stop.
  • If the loop closes on itself, label the figure unconfirmed. Do not promote it to confirmed on citation volume.
  • If it must be mentioned, drop the number and keep only the direction: several sources describe an upward trend, but no primary source was confirmed.
  • When the same figure keeps recurring, add it to an internal blocklist so the next person does not spend the same hour.

10. Writing unconfirmed beats making something up

Everything above converges on one sentence. The process only works if you can write down that you could not confirm something. Without that, every preceding step degenerates into paperwork. In an organization where unconfirmed cannot be written, reviewers push every ambiguous item into confirmed, and the labels stop carrying information.

What is striking is that this is precisely what a model structurally cannot do. As section 2 showed, models have been trained and graded so that admitting ignorance earns nothing. People and organizations feel the same pressure - a report full of unconfirmed looks like a report where less work was done. So the pressure has to be countered institutionally.

  • Treat the unconfirmed count as a deliverable. Make the unconfirmed list a required section of the verification report, and unconfirmed becomes a result rather than a failure.
  • Standardize the in-text wording. Fixed phrases such as unconfirmed, no primary source located, and as of July 2026 and subject to change save you from re-inventing language each time.
  • Make deletion the default. An unconfirmed tier A claim is removed, not retained. Keeping it requires a written reason.
  • Build the correction path in advance. If nobody has decided where and how a post-publication error gets flagged, errors get found and not fixed.

From the reader side it is clearer still. A sentence marked as unconfirmed gives the reader material to judge with. A sentence pretending to be confirmed removes the chance to judge at all. The first is incomplete information; the second is wrong information. They are not the same class of problem.

11. Embedding it in a team - who verifies what, and when

Working alone you can run the process in your head. In a team you cannot. Team fact-checking usually fails not on capability but on a gap in ownership. Somebody must have looked at it is the single most common cause of incidents.

RoleWhenScope of responsibilityOutput
Author Immediately after the draft Claim extraction and source attachment. Responsibility extends to flagging their own unconfirmed items Claim-to-source table, blanks included
Verifier After submission, before editing Full traceback of tiers A and B. Must be someone other than the author Labelled verdict list
Editor After verdicts Decides how unconfirmed and partially confirmed items are handled - delete, soften, or date-stamp Final text with reasons recorded
Publisher Immediately before release Opens every tier B link, confirms as-of markers are present Release checklist
Operations Periodically after publication Manages the recheck cycle for time-dependent items and maintains correction history Update and correction log

Exactly one cell in that table is non-negotiable: the verifier must not be the author. Everything else can be merged to fit the size of the organization. With two people, check each other. Alone, insert time. Reopen the draft in verification mode hours later and sentences that felt obvious while writing look different. The effect is not small.

Tooling helps, provided you do not confuse the layer tools catch with the layer humans catch. Link existence, identifier resolution, date format coherence and unit consistency automate well. Is this source about this claim and did a qualifying clause get dropped do not. How to stack automated checkers into layers and manage their false positives is covered separately in the verification pipeline article.

12. What actually got cut from this article

An article about a process is worthless if it does not follow the process. So here is what was genuinely removed or weakened while preparing this piece. The reasoning is often more useful than the rule.

  • Cut 1 - a specific percentage for how many model-generated references are fabricated. It was a striking figure, but the only source was the blog of a company selling citation-verification software. Section 6.1 applied to itself, so it went. The preregistered Stanford RegLab study took its place.
  • Cut 2 - registration counts and publisher totals for the scholarly identifier database. Same vendor blog, sole source. The method description - resolve the identifier to confirm existence - works fine without a scale figure, so it is described without numbers.
  • Weakened 1 - the calibration error figure. A specific value for confidence-calibration error was going to be cited, but it surfaced only through a summary path that could not be matched to the original paper, and conditions varied widely across studies. Section 2 therefore states only the direction and gives no number.
  • Date-stamped - the count of legal hallucination incidents. Direct access to the primary database was blocked, so the original could not be confirmed. As a result no specific count appears in the body; the introduction says only that it keeps climbing, and the reference list carries the database address so readers can check the current value themselves.
  • Qualified - the deceptive grounding research. The concept and the examples fit the argument exactly, but it is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed. It is cited, with that fact stated in the body of section 8.

Note that four of the five were decisions to remove a number. The actual output of a fact-checking process is usually not added evidence but deleted claims. The writing gets slightly flatter; in exchange you never have to write a correction notice.

13. A minimum setup you can start in thirty minutes

Trying to adopt the whole process at once generally fails. These four alone stop most incidents.

  1. Make one table. Four columns is enough: claim / source URL / primary or not / verdict. Attaching this table to each document is eighty percent of the process.
  2. Run full coverage on tier A only. Numbers, dates, statutory references, quotations, proper nouns. Leave the rest alone for now.
  3. Open the links. Actually click every source link in tiers A and B. That one rule catches fabricated citations and entity mismatch simultaneously.
  4. Write down the unconfirmed. Record what you could not find. Delete it or flag it, but do not pretend to know.

If you add a fifth thing, make it record-keeping. Keep the URLs you used rather than discarding them, and the next revision needs only a date check instead of a repeated traceback. The most expensive resource in fact-checking is not judgment; it is the time spent redoing a check you already did once.

Conclusion: hallucination is a gap in the process, not in your attention

AI tools will keep getting more fluent, and the more fluent they get, the less surface inspection catches. The era of spotting them by awkward phrasing is over. A perfectly formatted fake case, a link that resolves cleanly to the wrong evidence, a number cited everywhere with no origin - none of the three can be distinguished by reading carefully. Distinguishing them requires checking, and checking does not persist without procedure.

What this article proposes is not an elaborate methodology. Break claims apart, demand sources, climb to the primary, and record whether each was confirmed. Four steps. Add risk tiers to ration the effort, and a culture in which writing that you could not confirm something is permitted.

One last point: this process is not about distrusting AI. Quite the opposite. Once a document records what was verified and what was not, it stops mattering which tool produced it. Not needing to trust the tool is the only condition under which the tool can be used freely.
