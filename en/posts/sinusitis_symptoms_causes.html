Sinusitis Symptoms and Causes

How it differs from a cold, and when antibiotics actually earn their place

Aug 19, 2026 Health

Introduction: when day ten arrives and the nose has not moved

It started as a head cold. Ten days later the congestion is unchanged, and now there is a dull ache across the cheekbones. Bending forward makes the front of the face feel heavy and pressed. At this point most people do one of two things: wait it out a while longer, or reach for the antibiotic left over in a drawer. The second is not advisable, and the first depends entirely on which signs are present.

Sinusitis is common enough that the misconceptions around it have set like concrete. The deepest-rooted one is the equation yellow discharge means bacteria, and bacteria mean antibiotics. That equation appears in no international guideline. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guideline on acute bacterial rhinosinusitis estimates that 90 to 98 percent of acute rhinosinusitis is viral and 2 to 10 percent involves bacteria. Secondary bacterial infection after a viral upper respiratory infection is put at roughly 0.5 to 2 percent in adults and about 5 percent in children. Yet the same guideline cites data showing that 81 percent of adults with acute rhinosinusitis received an antibiotic. The American Academy of Otolaryngology (AAO-HNS) guideline notes that more than one in five antibiotics prescribed to adults is for sinusitis.

National figures tell the same story. In South Korea, the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service reported an antibiotic prescription rate of 41.42 percent for acute upper respiratory infections in 2023, up from 32.36 percent the previous year, and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency weekly report described the same upward trend.

So this article runs in a deliberate order: what the sinuses are and why they block, where sinusitis parts company with a cold and with allergic rhinitis, the duration thresholds that separate acute from chronic, the patterns that raise suspicion of bacteria, the real benefit and harm numbers behind antibiotics, what actually eases symptoms, and the warning signs that cannot be sat on. That last axis matters most. Sinusitis nearly always resolves on its own, but on rare occasions it spreads to the eye or the brain. Recognising that fork is the practical point of reading any of this.

Medical disclaimer
This article is provided as general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, see an ear, nose and throat clinician. Nothing here should be used as a reason to start, stop or change a medicine on your own. The choice, dose and duration of any medicine, including antibiotics and steroids, is a decision for the clinician and pharmacist who assess you. Recommendations differ for children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults, immunocompromised people and those with diabetes. If any of the red flags below are present, seek care without delay.

1. What the sinuses are, and why a blockage matters

The paranasal sinuses are air-filled spaces inside the facial bones, which is how the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency health portal describes them as well. They are named for where they sit.

  • Maxillary sinuses — the largest pair, behind the cheekbones above the upper jaw. Most facial pain people describe points here
  • Ethmoid sinuses — a honeycomb of small cells between the eyes, separated from the eye socket by an extremely thin plate of bone
  • Frontal sinuses — behind the forehead. These are behind the heavy pressure people feel when they lean forward
  • Sphenoid sinuses — deepest of all, just under the skull base. Symptoms here can present as pain at the back or top of the head

Each space connects to the nasal cavity through a small opening called the ostium. The lining constantly produces mucus, and cilia on its surface sweep that mucus toward the ostium. As long as that clearance keeps running, the sinuses stay quiet.

Trouble starts when the ostium narrows. A cold virus swells the nasal lining, an allergic reaction puffs it up, or the passage is structurally tight, and the drainage route closes. From there a chain follows. Ventilation drops, oxygen tension inside the sinus falls, mucus pools, ciliary movement slows, and the standing mucus becomes a reasonable growth medium for bacteria. This is exactly why sinusitis sits on the far end of an ordinary head cold: no new disease appears, an existing inflammation of the nasal lining simply extends into the next room and that room's door shuts.

It is also why the preferred medical term has shifted from sinusitis to rhinosinusitis. The nasal and sinus linings are continuous, so inflammation confined to the sinuses alone is effectively rare. The old lay term used in Korea, chuknongjeung, literally means an accumulation of pus, but in practice it covers plenty of inflammation with no pus at all.

The maxillary sinus carries an extra structural disadvantage. Its ostium sits high on the wall rather than at the floor, so pooled mucus cannot simply drain downward under gravity and depends entirely on ciliary transport. Swell the lining or thicken the mucus and clearance stalls immediately.

2. Cold, allergic rhinitis, sinusitis: where they part company

This is the question that comes up most in clinic. Appearances alone cannot settle it, but four axes — duration, fever, itch and facial symptoms — point the direction reasonably well. One row is deliberately missing: mucus colour. It is the single most misread signal, and the criteria guidelines use to identify bacterial disease do not list it as a standalone item. Thicker and darker discharge after a few days is common in plain viral infection. A change in colour signals that immune cells have arrived, not which organism brought them.

Allergic rhinitis and sinusitis overlap heavily in symptoms but they are different diseases. Rhinitis is IgE-mediated inflammation of the nasal lining; sinusitis is inflammation of the sinus cavities. Each does invite the other, though. Chronically swollen tissue from allergic rhinitis narrows the ostia, and that narrowing becomes the backdrop for sinusitis. If sneezing and itching dominate, fever is absent, and the pattern tracks a season or a situation, rhinitis deserves the first look. Testing and stepwise treatment for that condition are covered separately in the complete guide to allergic rhinitis. This article stays on the sinus side.

3. Acute, subacute, chronic: what four weeks and twelve weeks divide

Rhinosinusitis is classified by duration, and the classification matters because the direction of treatment changes with it. Acute disease is mostly a self-limiting infectious event. Chronic disease is not a question of curing an infection but of managing inflammation that will not switch off.

  • Acute — symptoms lasting less than four weeks (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency health portal)
  • Subacute — between four and twelve weeks
  • Chronic — symptoms that never fully resolve and persist twelve weeks or longer
  • Recurrent acute — four or more acute episodes in twelve months with no symptoms between them

The European guideline EPOS 2020 draws the same twelve-week line: resolution inside twelve weeks is acute rhinosinusitis, persistence beyond it is chronic. Diagnosis calls for two or more symptoms, at least one of which must be nasal blockage or nasal discharge, with facial pain or pressure and reduced sense of smell as the additional items. The AAO-HNS guideline adds that calling a case chronic requires not just the twelve weeks but objective evidence of inflammation on endoscopy or imaging. Long-standing symptoms alone do not make the label.

It is worth knowing that Korean sources differ in how they phrase this. The Korean Society of Otorhinolaryngology public-facing page still uses the older split of under three weeks for acute and three months or more for chronic, while the national health portal uses the four-week and twelve-week international thresholds. Three months and twelve weeks are effectively the same line, so the chronic boundary does not really move; only the ceiling on acute shifts between three and four weeks. Either way, the ten-day mark remains the line that drives the antibiotic decision.

4. Most of it is viral: the weight behind 90 to 98 percent

The figure appeared in the introduction, but it is the single most consequential number here, so it gets its own section. The IDSA guideline sets out the causes of acute rhinosinusitis as follows.

  • Viral cause in 90 to 98 percent of cases
  • Bacterial involvement in 2 to 10 percent
  • Progression from viral upper respiratory infection to secondary bacterial infection in 0.5 to 2 percent of adults and about 5 percent of children
  • Spontaneous improvement in roughly 70 percent of placebo-controlled trial participants

When bacteria are involved, the usual organisms are Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae, and Korean national health material names the same two. The hard part of this whole topic, though, is that there is no way to tell from the outside which case falls into that 2 to 10 percent. A swab from the nose grows the bacteria that live there anyway, so it cannot identify the responsible organism on its own, and a clean specimen requires puncturing the sinus directly. Routine practice does not do that.

So the guidelines gave up on finding the organism and gave the job to the shape of the clinical course instead. That is the next section.

5. The three patterns that raise suspicion of bacteria

The IDSA and AAO-HNS guidelines word it slightly differently but describe the same three pictures. If none of the three fits, there is little basis within the guideline framework for calling a case bacterial.

  • 1. Persistent — symptoms run ten days or more with no sign of improvement. A viral course normally turns downhill inside ten days, so departing from that curve is itself the signal
  • 2. Double worsening — a few days of improvement followed by deterioration within ten days. Fever climbs again, headache intensifies, or discharge increases once more. English-language literature calls this double sickening
  • 3. Severe onset — the illness starts with fever of 39C or higher plus purulent discharge or facial pain for three to four consecutive days. This is the threshold the IDSA guideline states explicitly

All three share the same underlying logic of duration and inflection. A viral course comes down once it peaks. If it never comes down, or comes down and turns back up, or starts far above where a viral peak belongs, something else may be happening.

The criteria differ somewhat for children

The American Academy of Pediatrics guideline for acute bacterial sinusitis in children aged 1 to 18 years sets three branches.

  • Persistent illness — nasal discharge of any quality, daytime cough, or both, lasting more than ten days without improvement
  • Worsening course — new or worsening discharge, daytime cough or fever after an initial improvement
  • Severe onset — fever of 39C or higher together with purulent discharge for at least three consecutive days

Prescribing advice splits by branch too. The same guideline recommends antibiotic therapy for severe onset or a worsening course, while for the persistent branch alone it allows either antibiotics or three additional days of outpatient observation. Children run fevers easily and catch colds often, which makes the judgement harder rather than easier. Rather than applying these criteria to a child yourself, have a paediatrician or ENT clinician confirm the picture.

6. Antibiotics: what guidelines advise against what actually gets written

Meeting one of the three patterns does not automatically produce a prescription either. This is where the field has moved most in recent years.

The 2018 Cochrane systematic review by Lemiengre and colleagues (CD006089) pooled trials of acute rhinosinusitis in adults and reported the following.

  • Without antibiotics, 46 percent recovered by one week and 64 percent by fourteen days
  • Antibiotics helped 5 to 11 more people per 100 recover faster
  • The number needed to treat for one additional cure was 19 with clinical diagnosis and 10 with imaging confirmation
  • Adverse events occurred in 27 percent of antibiotic groups against 15 percent of placebo or no-treatment groups, giving a number needed to harm of 8
  • The authors concluded that there is no place for antibiotics in uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis

Set those numbers side by side and the picture is stark. It takes 19 people treated to produce one extra recovery, and only 8 to produce one extra harm. Antibiotic resistance sits on top of that as a cost paid by everyone else.

Here is how that evidence has moved into guidance.

  • 2015 AAO-HNS adult sinusitis guideline — clinicians were to either offer watchful waiting or prescribe initial antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated acute bacterial rhinosinusitis. Watchful waiting assumed assured follow-up, with antibiotics started if there was no improvement within seven days or if symptoms worsened
  • 2025 update — the centre of gravity shifted. For uncomplicated cases the wording now recommends watchful waiting. Antibiotic duration was shortened from 5 to 10 days down to 5 to 7 days, and reassessment for treatment failure was brought forward from seven days to three to five days
  • First-line agent — amoxicillin, with or without clavulanate. The IDSA guideline prefers the clavulanate combination over amoxicillin alone for empiric therapy
  • Imaging — guidelines advise against radiographic imaging unless a complication or an alternative diagnosis is suspected. Where complications are suspected, contrast-enhanced CT is the first choice

In short: whether to use an antibiotic turns on the ten-day line and the shape of the course, and that judgement belongs to the clinician who examines you. Do not take leftover antibiotics from a previous illness. The same diagnostic label can involve different organisms and different resistance patterns, and an interrupted course leaves resistance behind without leaving benefit.

One more thing worth saying plainly: not receiving an antibiotic is not the same as being left alone. Watchful waiting comes bundled with pain control, symptom relief and a scheduled point of reassessment. Walking out without a prescription and doing nothing are different states.

What stands out in that table is the mismatch between what sells most and what the evidence supports most. Decongestant sprays get treated as a staple because relief is instant, yet guidelines do not recommend them as an adjunct in acute bacterial disease and a few days of continued use brings rebound congestion of its own. Saline irrigation, meanwhile, costs almost nothing and has a good safety profile, and hardly anyone keeps it up.

One caution on irrigation: the safety of the water matters. Because of microbial risk including amoebae, using untreated tap water directly is not advised, and the device needs cleaning every time. Follow the product instructions and the guidance of a pharmacist or clinician for solution strength and device care.

8. Red flags: symptoms that must not be filed under a nose problem

This section matters more than any other here. The ethmoid sinuses are separated from the eye socket by bone as thin as paper, and the sphenoid and frontal sinuses abut the skull base. Rarely, inflammation crosses those walls. Orbital complications described in the literature include orbital cellulitis and subperiosteal or intraorbital abscess; intracranial complications include epidural and subdural abscess, brain abscess, meningitis, encephalitis and venous sinus thrombosis.

One observation about timing
A review of complications of acute rhinosinusitis reported that orbital complications appeared within three days of symptom onset in some cases, whereas most intracranial complications presented from day eight onward. In other words, eye signs can appear early, so a wait-and-see plan does not apply to them. If any sign in the table above is present, go to emergency care rather than waiting for clinic hours.

A further note: people who are immunocompromised or living with poorly controlled diabetes need the same symptoms handled differently. Rapidly progressive forms of sinus infection are described in these settings, so facial pain or swelling that escalates quickly warrants immediate medical attention. The same applies to anyone on chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy.

9. What to re-examine when it turns chronic or keeps coming back

Past twelve weeks, or four or more episodes in a year, the question changes. It is no longer how do I end this infection but why does it not end. Several things come under review at that point.

Structural factors

  • Deviated septum — the central wall of the nose bent to one side, narrowing the passage. Korean ENT society material lists it among the background factors for chronic sinusitis
  • Nasal polyps — lining that has grown into soft swellings, physically blocking drainage. This is why chronic rhinosinusitis is split into forms with and without polyps
  • Other anatomical variants — tight structures around the ostia belong here. Endoscopy and CT are how they are identified

Coexisting conditions

  • Allergic rhinitis — keeps the lining chronically swollen and the drainage route narrow
  • Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease — polyps, asthma and aspirin sensitivity together, newly recognised as a modifying factor in the 2025 AAO-HNS update
  • Asthma — upper and lower airway inflammation are continuous, so control of one affects the other
  • Immune factors — unusually frequent recurrence can put these on the assessment list. That call belongs to the clinician

Dental causes: the axis most often missed

The maxillary sinus sits directly above the roots of the upper molars, which is how odontogenic maxillary sinusitis arises. Prevalence estimates vary widely. Reports before the 1970s put dental origin at roughly 10 to 12 percent of maxillary sinusitis, whereas a CT-based systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of about 50 percent per patient and 51 percent per maxillary sinus. Narrowed to one-sided maxillary disease, some reports find dental pathology in around 30 percent. The spread reflects differences in study populations and assessment methods.

The practical takeaway is single. If one side of the nose stays blocked, the odour is unusually foul, and it recurs despite antibiotics, a dental assessment alongside the ENT one is a reasonable step. Cases where the cause sat in a molar the whole time are not rare.

The treatment axis in chronic disease

Korean national health material places saline irrigation and intranasal corticosteroids at the centre of chronic rhinosinusitis care and notes that biologics such as dupilumab and omalizumab are used in refractory eosinophilic disease. The 2025 AAO-HNS update added three points: it advises against routine biologics in chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps, recommends patient education about biologics where polyps are present, and advises against routine antimicrobials outside acute exacerbations or as a prerequisite to surgery. Where medical therapy fails to control the disease, endoscopic sinus surgery is considered, and its purpose is less to remove inflammation than to open the drainage route so that topical treatment can reach the lining. That is why irrigation and topical therapy continue after an operation.

What could not be confirmed

  • The full text of the 2025 AAO-HNS update — the changes were confirmed through society material and guideline summary services; the complete original was not opened. Exact grading language and evidence profiles would require the original
  • Population prevalence of sinusitis in Korea — no recent national survey figure comparable to the ones available for allergic rhinitis was found, so no prevalence number is quoted here
  • Steam inhalation, humidifiers and fluid intake — widely used, but guideline-level evidence was not confirmed
  • Official national guidance on water treatment for nasal irrigation — no explicit Korean agency instruction was located, so only the principle is stated and specifics are deferred to product instructions and clinicians
  • Herbal remedies, supplements and nasal dilator devices — no guideline-level evidence was confirmed, so they are not covered
  • Why odontogenic prevalence estimates diverge — the coexistence of the 10 to 12 percent and roughly 50 percent figures could not be pinned to anything more specific than differences in populations and assessment methods

Frequently asked questions

Q. Does yellow or green mucus mean a bacterial infection?

Colour alone does not settle it. The criteria guidelines use to identify bacterial disease do not include mucus colour as a standalone item. Thicker, darker discharge after a few days is common in ordinary viral infection. The axes that matter are duration and the shape of the course.

Q. How can I tell a cold from sinusitis?

Duration and facial symptoms are the practical clues. A cold usually settles within seven to ten days. If day ten passes with no sign of improvement, if there is pressure across the cheekbones, between the eyes or over the forehead, or if the sense of smell is dulled alongside, the sinuses are more likely involved. Most acute sinusitis is the same cold virus reaching the sinus lining, so the two sit on a continuum.

Q. When are antibiotics actually needed?

The persistent, double-worsening and severe-onset patterns are the starting point, and even then the 2025 AAO-HNS update recommends watchful waiting for uncomplicated cases. On Cochrane figures the number needed to treat for benefit is 19 and the number needed to harm is 8. Whether to prescribe is a decision for the clinician who examines you.

Q. Is saline nasal irrigation supported by evidence?

In acute disease it is an adjunct with a weak recommendation; in chronic disease the Korean national health portal describes it as actively recommended. Safety is generally good, with reported problems limited to mild nasal irritation. Follow product instructions and pharmacist or clinician guidance on water safety and device care.

Q. Does sinusitis eventually require surgery?

Acute disease is largely unrelated to surgery. Even in chronic disease, medical therapy built on irrigation and intranasal steroids comes first, with endoscopic sinus surgery considered when that fails. Surgery mostly opens the drainage route so treatment can reach the lining, so care and follow-up continue afterwards. Whether and when to operate is decided through ENT assessment.

Conclusion: when to wait, and when to go in

The principle behind managing sinusitis is simpler than it looks. Most of it is viral, and time does most of the work. While that time passes you control pain, rinse the nose, and watch the shape of the course. Then, at defined lines, you change the decision.

  • Fine to wait — still inside a cold's course, under ten days, symptoms past their peak and coming down, no red flags
  • Have it assessed — day ten with no improvement, improvement followed by relapse, high fever with purulent discharge running past three days from the start, symptoms past four weeks, four or more episodes in a year, symptoms confined to one side
  • Go immediately or to emergency care — swelling or redness around the eye, a bulging eye, pain on eye movement, double or reduced vision, severe headache, stiff neck, altered consciousness, focal neurological signs, a fever that will not come down

Reduced to one sentence, the position of this article is this. Before asking when to use an antibiotic, ask whether this is still a waiting window at all. The line is not the colour of the discharge but duration, the shape of the course, and the presence or absence of red flags.

Medical disclaimer, once more
This article is for information purposes and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, see an ear, nose and throat clinician. The duration thresholds and differential items above reproduce the reasoning framework of clinical guidelines; they are not a tool for settling on a diagnosis yourself. Whether and how long to use any medicine, including antibiotics and steroids, and whether testing or surgery is warranted, are decisions for the clinician who examines you. Recommendations differ for children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults and immunocompromised people, so do not apply these thresholds to them directly.
