What a Machine Can and Cannot Verify in an LLM Answer

The boundary of verifiability, seen through a harness built by a mechanical design engineer

Jul 29, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: the phrase we automated verification hides a condition

Tools that claim to verify LLM output automatically keep appearing. Take the claim at face value and disappointment arrives quickly, because what actually gets adjudicated is not the whole answer but the fragments of it that happen to have a verifiable nature. Whether a calculation is wrong can be settled by recomputing it. Whether the assumption behind that calculation fits the situation cannot be settled by recomputing anything. Both questions sit side by side in the same answer, and their verifiability is opposite.

In July 2026 a case surfaced that draws this boundary unusually clearly. A single mechanical design engineer tidied up the set of verification rules he had been running in his own working environment and published it as a repository. It is organised as thirteen gates. Some gates stop the answer from leaving at all when a violation is found. Others do not block anything and instead hand the decision to a person. The interesting part is not the feature list but the split: where the author drew the line, and why the line ended up there.

One thing should be stated up front. This repository is a snapshot of one person's setup running in one person's environment, and its own documentation asks readers to treat it as a reference architecture rather than a finished product. This article does not evaluate whether the tool is good. No published performance or accuracy figures were confirmed, so no such numbers appear here. The interest is narrower: where does the boundary of verifiability lie, and why does it sit so far out in domains with physical constraints?

1. Verifiability is decided by the claim, not by the tool

When teams adopt automated verification they usually start by choosing a tool. The order is backwards. Whether an answer can be adjudicated by machine depends not on how capable the checker is but on where the answer gets its truth value from.

The test is simpler than expected. Does the correct value exist independently outside the answer, and can it be obtained without reading the answer? If yes, a machine can adjudicate. If no, no amount of checker sophistication removes the need for human eyes.

  • Truth lives outside the answer — the result of a multiplication, a converted unit, a clause number in a standard, a part number that exists in a catalogue. You can ignore the answer entirely, derive the value independently, and compare.
  • Truth is settled inside the answer — self-contradiction, where the text says A early and not-A later. No external reference is needed; putting the two statements side by side is enough.
  • Truth exists nowhere — is this design appropriate, does this tone suit our organisation, which of three alternatives should we take. Here the best a machine can do is present rather than adjudicate.

The third category is the one most often misread in practice. Attach a checker to a question that requires judgment and the checker produces another opinion, not a verdict. The moment an opinion is treated as a verdict, wrong passes and wrong blocks both increase at once.

2. Four bands for sorting claims

Two boxes labelled yes and no are not enough for real work. Automatability is continuous, and there is a wide middle where the machine starts the job and a person finishes it. Sorting each claim in an answer into one of four bands makes it obvious where rules belong.

BandBasis for adjudicationDegree of automationTypical items
ComputableRecompute and compareFully automaticArithmetic, unit conversion, totals, ratios, invertible formulas
ComparableLook up an external list or documentNearly automatic (lookup failure needs handling)Identifiers, standard designations, clause numbers, API names, link validity
ConsistencyCompare within the same document or sessionDetection automatic, resolution humanSelf-contradiction, mismatched figures, claim versus execution record
JudgmentDomain context and purposeNot automatable (assistive only)Validity of assumptions, choice among alternatives, risk tolerance, tone

Applying this table produces an interesting effect. All four bands frequently appear inside a single paragraph. Take a sentence like: the required thickness under these conditions is roughly twelve millimetres, and following the relevant clause of the applicable standard the safety factor leaves margin. That contains a computable item (the thickness), a comparable item (whether that clause exists), and a judgment item (the assessment that margin exists).

So the unit of verification turns out to be neither the answer nor the sentence but the claim. How to decompose text into claims is outside this article's scope; the hallucination fact-checking process covers it. Here the focus is what to do after decomposition: which band does each claim go into?

  • Trap in the computable band — a value can be right while the unit is wrong, and it still passes. Unless units are checked as a separate item, automation here is only half done.
  • Trap in the comparable band — if a failed lookup does not distinguish does not exist from could not confirm, valid items get blocked. The two outcomes need different handling.
  • Trap in the consistency band — finding a contradiction and deciding which side is correct are different problems. The machine only does the first.
  • Trap in the judgment band — put automated rules here and you end up managing a pass rate rather than quality.

3. Where the published harness drew its line

Overlaying the four bands on the actual case shows this is not merely theoretical. The thirteen gates in the published repository are documented with a split between what blocks automatically and what a person adjudicates. The documentation states, in effect, that humans own the gates that matter, with scanners warning and ledgers recording as the supporting roles.

Nature of the gateWho adjudicatesBand from section 2Why automation does or does not hold
Blocking invented identifiers and document numbersAutomatic (hard block)ComparableExistence is settled by an external list
Re-executing non-trivial arithmeticAutomatic plus humanComputableThe computation is automatic; intermediate values and unit sanity are human
Isolating instructions embedded in external inputAutomatic (demoted to data)ComparableThe pattern is formal enough to catch with rules
Comparing a claim of verification against the actual tool outputAutomaticConsistencyTwo sources already exist: the claim and the record
Detecting contradiction within a sessionAutomaticConsistencyEarlier statements are on record, so comparison holds
Assigning confidence tiersHumanJudgmentWhat counts as certain cannot be declared by the answerer
Surfacing and validating design assumptionsHumanJudgmentThe appropriateness of a premise has no external answer
Cross-model verification of safety-critical conclusionsInvoked by a humanJudgmentAgreement is a signal, not evidence of truth

Read the table vertically and a pattern appears. Every hard block sits in the computable, comparable, or consistency band, and not one sits in the judgment band. In the judgment band what the harness does is not blocking but making things visible. It forces a confidence tier to be attached, forces assumptions out into the open, and calls a different model to re-derive the conclusion. None of these decide pass or fail.

The three-tier confidence scheme rests on the same principle. Only material that comes directly from a tool run, a standard, or a real address earns the top tier; anything sourced from memory or industry habit is forcibly demoted to the middle tier, and speculation sits at the bottom. The important part is not the tiers themselves but the fact that the top tier cannot be earned from the model's own recall. The route by which a model declares its own certainty has been cut structurally.

4. Why mechanical design - physical constraints are a free verifier

More telling than the fact that a domain expert built this is which domain it was. Fields governed by physical constraints get several conditions favourable to LLM verification for free.

  • The numbers close — dimensions must add up, volume and mass connect through density, and unit conversion follows fixed coefficients. You can regenerate the value without reading the answer.
  • The identifier systems exist — part numbers, material specifications and standard designations have external lists. Inventing a plausible number that does not exist becomes an immediately adjudicable failure.
  • Mismatches surface physically — parts do not assemble, interfere, or fail to carry the load. Knowing that a verification failure will eventually be observed is a strong motive to write rules.
  • Failure costs are asymmetric — a wrong pass is expensive. That makes a principle like nothing ships before it passes inspection easy to accept.

These conditions are not exclusive to mechanical design. Accounting requires debits and credits to balance; tax work pins its reasoning to specific statutory clauses; code is adjudicated by compilation and tests. Machine learning has used the same distinction for years. Approaches built on verifiable rewards work well in domains where a machine can grade the answer, such as mathematics and programming, while open-ended or subjective tasks still need preference-based or model-based reward signals. That framing is widely documented.

Even so, physical constraints do not verify every answer in the domain. The arithmetic gets rechecked, but which load case to assume is still chosen by a person. What physics supplies for free is the computable and comparable bands, not the judgment band. Blur that distinction and you arrive at the false comfort of all the checks passed, so the design is correct.

5. The other side - domains where judgment decides the answer

The boundary sharpens when you set up a contrast: how much of the same harness survives when moved to another domain, expressed as band composition. The weightings below are not precise measurements but my own relative reading of the character of each domain's output.

DomainWhat can be adjudicated automaticallyWhat stays with peopleVerification difficulty
Mechanical and electrical designDimension and load calculations, part numbers, standard designations, unitsSetting assumptions, judging margin, choosing alternativesLow
Software implementationCompilation, tests, types, static analysis, execution resultsDesign suitability, maintainability, choosing edge casesLow
Accounting and tax computationTotal checks, existence of clause numbers, rate lookupCharacterising a transaction, choosing the applicable clauseMedium
Medical and legal adviceExistence of cited literature, terminology accuracy, numeric rangesWhether any of it applies to the individual caseHigh
Strategy and planning documentsFactual citations, numeric consistency at bestLogical validity, prioritisation, feasibilityVery high
Creative writing and editingFactual errors, notation consistencyNearly everythingNot a measurement target

The further down the table you go, the less a harness can do. And here a common misconception appears: that harder-to-verify domains need more sophisticated checkers. The opposite is true. In domains dominated by the judgment band, what is needed is not a cleverer checker but an arrangement that reduces what a person has to look at. Separating assumptions from confirmed facts, marking low-confidence passages, and surfacing sentences with no supporting evidence makes human review dramatically faster.

One more caution: bands are set by the nature of the question, not the domain. Within software, is this architecture appropriate belongs to the judgment band, while within creative writing, does this quotation actually exist belongs to the comparable band. Reasoning at domain granularity means missing things that could have been automated.

6. Three conditions for a hard block

Deciding which items deserve a hard block is not a matter of instinct. Given the cost of failure the conditions narrow to three, and an item should be promoted to blocking only when all three hold.

  1. Adjudication must be deterministic — the same input must give the same verdict. If probability enters the verdict, the item belongs at warning level, not blocking. Using a model as the adjudicator falls foul of this in particular.
  2. False positives must be rare — a block stops work. Frequent stoppages make people build detours, and a gate with a detour around it is effectively no gate.
  3. The consequence must be hard to reverse — reversible errors can be allowed through and fixed later. Block only what is hard to recall once it flows downstream, such as a nonexistent identifier reaching a drawing or a purchase order.

Apply the three conditions and the gate layout in the previous section makes sense. Inventing an identifier is deterministic (it is on the list or it is not), rarely misfires (the format is unambiguous), and has heavy downstream reach. Is this conclusion safe satisfies none of the three. That is why, inside the same harness, one is a block and the other is a human call.

A practical rule follows. Every new rule starts at log-only. Starting at blocking means obstructing the workflow before anyone knows the false positive rate. Observing that rate for a while, then promoting to warning and finally to blocking, is a sequence that is easy to reverse.

7. What a harness misses - right in form, wrong in fact

The biggest limit of automated gates is the case where every indicator is green and the answer is still wrong. This category comes from a structural limit rather than a design mistake. The recurring shapes are these.

  • Correct computation, wrong input — the arithmetic recheck passes. The value fed in was wrong to begin with, so the calculator says nothing. Unless the provenance of inputs is demanded alongside, this is never caught.
  • Real source, different content — the document exists when looked up, but it does not support the claim. A check that only tests existence cannot adjudicate this.
  • Right standard, wrong scope — the cited standard exists and the clause number is correct, but the standard does not apply to this situation. Scope applicability is a judgment item.
  • Errors of omission — every sentence written is true, and the conclusion is dangerous because of a missing condition. A checker cannot check a sentence that is not there.
  • Consistently wrong — contradiction checks only fire when statements disagree. Hold the same misunderstanding from beginning to end and the consistency gate stays quiet.
  • Agreed-upon error — several models producing the same answer is not evidence of truth. Models sharing training data fail together at the same points. That is exactly why different model families are used, but agreement remains a signal rather than a proof.

What these share is that formal checking cannot substitute for meaning. How to stack checking layers, and what each layer catches or lets through, is covered separately in the AI output verification workflow. This article is interested not in the composition of the layers but in what remains after the top layer, the residue that never descends into any checking layer at all.

8. How to decide what gets handed to a person

Important things get human review is not a criterion. Human time is finite, so deciding what to hand over is the same as deciding what not to look at. Asking four questions in order usually settles it.

QuestionIf yesIf no
Does this item have its answer outside the text?Push it down to automatic adjudicationPromote it as a candidate for human review
Is the error recoverable downstream?Reduce to sampled reviewReview every instance
Does the model get this item wrong often?Priority review itemPeriodic sampling is enough
Does the reviewer have the knowledge to adjudicate?Hand it overDo not hand it over; secure the knowledge first

The last question is the one most often skipped. Handing an item to someone who cannot adjudicate it is not verification but a transfer of liability. If the reviewer does not know the domain the outcome becomes it looks plausible, so it passes, and that is worse than an automatic pass, because a record now exists saying a person reviewed it, which suppresses later suspicion.

When you hand something over, hand over what to look at as well. Throwing the whole answer at someone and asking them to check it produces a formality. The forms that have worked in practice are these.

  • Pass only the low-confidence items, marked — instead of making someone read everything, direct them to the flagged passages first.
  • Extract assumptions into a list — pulling premises out of the prose turns review into item confirmation.
  • Include the evidence — without the origin of a number the reviewer has to go find it, and at that moment the review gets postponed.
  • Record the verdict — verdicts must accumulate or the same item gets reviewed again next time.
  • Have a path that confirms the handed item was handled — the most common failure is that something was handed over, nobody looked, and it shipped anyway.

9. When false positives pile up in a blocking gate

Alert fatigue is a well-known phenomenon. In blocking gates, though, the problem has a different character. A warning can be ignored; a block cannot. So when false positives accumulate, people do not ignore, they bypass. They disable the rule, rephrase to slip past the gate, or move to a path without the gate. This is far more dangerous, because the gate still looks alive on the dashboard.

The response starts not with deleting the rule but with lowering enforcement by one step. Defining the ladder in advance changes how fast this gets handled.

  1. Block — a violation prevents the output from leaving. Only items satisfying all three conditions from section 6.
  2. Confirmation required — a person must explicitly acknowledge before it passes. For deterministic items that still misfire.
  3. Warning — let it pass but mark it. For items with middling adjudication accuracy.
  4. Log — record only, with no intervention. For new rules and rules under observation.

When false positives are observed, step down one rung; when accuracy recovers, step back up. Deleting a rule is the last resort. Delete it and the genuine defects it used to catch disappear with it, a fact that surfaces only after an incident.

There is one more thing to confirm before stepping down. Is it genuinely a false positive? Once the habit forms of declaring something a false positive without opening the flagged passage, real defects start getting dismissed the same way. Opening the source every time a flag appears looks expensive, but saving that cost destroys trust in the whole gate system.

It is also worth deciding in advance what happens when a bypass is discovered. The published harness includes a separate item for turning a defect that occurred after a gate was skipped back into a rule. That idea travels well. Fixing each breach on the spot and moving on means the same class recurs; promoting the case into a prevention rule and a test item makes the gate as dense as the experience it has accumulated.

10. Why a domain expert has to build it

The most-discussed detail in this case is the author's background. But stopping at experts build better tools explains nothing. There are three structural reasons.

10.1 Only someone who has been burned knows what to block

Automated rules ultimately start from a list of errors that recur in this domain. That list is not written down anywhere. Why a plausible but nonexistent part number is dangerous is known only to someone who has placed an order using that numbering system. This is why checkers built from outside a domain drift toward syntax and formatting. From outside, you see malformed, not wrong.

10.2 How strict to be is a question of risk instinct

The same error costs different amounts in different domains. A single typo is trivial in prose and delivers the wrong part when it lands in an identifier. Whether to promote an item to blocking or leave it at warning comes out balanced when decided by someone who feels the size of the failure cost.

10.3 The verification criteria are themselves domain knowledge

Some calculations can be checked by inversion and some cannot. Some standards must be read in their latest revision and for others the revision history is irrelevant. These are things you have to know before writing rules, and no tool supplies them.

Let us not overstate the reverse, though. This does not mean a domain expert automatically produces a good tool. The relationship is closer to necessary than sufficient. Without domain knowledge you cannot even draft the list of what to block; with domain knowledge alone, false positive management, maintenance and portability remain unsolved. The published harness itself is tied to specific operating system features and was released with its domain-specific parts removed for licensing reasons.

11. What reading it as a reference architecture means

The most common mistake on meeting a repository like this is trying to copy it into place. A tidied-up personal setup is rarely transplantable. In this case the documentation itself says it is not a finished product, and mentions dependence on a particular operating system's scheduler and command options, the removal of personal infrastructure, and third-party technology omitted for licensing reasons.

So what can be taken? The structure, not the code. The transferable items are these.

  • The principle behind the automatic-versus-human split — which kinds of item are blocked and which are handed to a person.
  • The confidence tier system — how many tiers, and what evidence alone can earn the top one.
  • Placement across the lifecycle — checking before a tool call, after a tool call, at session start, at response end. When a check runs determines what it can catch.
  • The conditions for invoking cross-verification — not always, but conditionally, for safety-critical conclusions or suspected problems.
  • The path from defect back to rule — the loop that converts a breach into a prevention rule and a test item.

Lifecycle placement is especially reusable. Pre-call checks block dangerous input, post-call checks compare the result, and end-of-response checks act as the final gate before shipping. The same rule catches different things depending on which point it attaches to, and skipping any of the three leaves a blind spot.

12. A procedure for drawing your own boundary

To finish, here is the transferable form. Before choosing tools, the first job is measuring the band composition of your own output.

  1. Pick ten recent outputs — mix good ones with ones that caused incidents. Without incident cases the boundary gets drawn optimistically.
  2. Decompose into claims — split by smallest verifiable unit, not by sentence.
  3. Sort into the four bands from section 2 — computable, comparable, consistency, judgment. When unsure, place it in the lower band, toward judgment.
  4. Count each band — if computable and comparable exceed half, automation pays off. If judgment dominates, invest in arrangement and marking rather than checkers.
  5. Map past incidents onto the bands — mark which band each real incident came from. That mapping sets priority.
  6. Write rules for the band with the most incidents first — start with what hurt, not with what is easy to build.
  7. Attach at log level and observe — do not promote straight to blocking.
  8. Adjust enforcement based on the false positive rate — follow the ladder from section 9.
  9. Handle the judgment band with a review process — resist the urge to put rules there.
  10. Turn breaches back into rules — without this loop the gate stays as dense as it was on the day it was written.

The output of this procedure is not a tool but a single picture of a boundary. Once a team agrees on where the machine looks and where people take over, which tool to use becomes a secondary question. Skip the picture and adopt tools first, and you end up with more checks and nobody able to say what was actually verified.

Conclusion: what gets automated is the floor, not verification

Reduce what a verification harness does to one sentence and it is raising the floor. Answers with wrong arithmetic, answers citing identifiers that do not exist, answers that contradict themselves, answers claiming a verification that never ran. It keeps these from leaving. The floor looks low, but a large share of real incidents happens exactly there, so it is worth having.

Raising a floor and guaranteeing a ceiling are different things, though. An answer where every gate is green means it contains no error of the kind a machine can catch, not that it is correct. Blur that difference and the gates stop being a safety device and become a comfort device. A comfort device is more dangerous than nothing.

What is worth taking from this case is neither the number thirteen nor any particular tool layout. It is the sequence: the author first separated what is computable from what is judgment in his own domain, and only then wrote rules. A mechanical design background made that sequence easier to follow, but the sequence itself is the same in any domain. Draw the boundary first, and automate only what is inside it. Attempts to automate the outside usually fail not for lack of tooling but because there is no answer out there to adjudicate against.
