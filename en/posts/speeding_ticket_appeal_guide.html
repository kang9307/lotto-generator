Contesting a Speeding Ticket in South Korea - What Can Be Argued and What Cannot

Only one window ever moves the amount, and it is not the objection stage

Sep 1, 2026 Law & Legal

Open the envelope and the document inside carries one of three names: an advance notice, a notice of imposition for an administrative fine, or a notice to pay a criminal-track penalty. Even for the same speeding event, those three sheets rest on different statutes, and the doors that open behind them are different. One route ends with a written filing to an agency and a court decision. The other crosses into criminal procedure.

How an administrative fine and a criminal-track penalty differ in concept is already covered in Traffic Fines vs. Penalties. This piece starts after that. At which stage can you say what, and at which stage is it already too late? Everything below is based on South Korean law as it stands in September 2026.

This article describes procedure under the law of the Republic of Korea. It is general information, not legal advice. Outcomes in any individual case turn on the specific facts, so an actual response should be confirmed with an attorney or with the Korea Legal Aid Corporation. Nothing here concerns avoiding enforcement.

Two routes that split at a single sheet of paper

When unmanned equipment catches a vehicle, all that remains is a photograph and video. Who was in the driver's seat is never fixed. That is why Article 160(3) of the Road Traffic Act provides that where the violation is proven by video recording media and a criminal-track notice disposition cannot be issued under Article 163, an administrative fine of up to 200,000 won is imposed on the employer or equivalent person under Article 56(1) of the same Act. On this route the governing procedural statute is not the Road Traffic Act but the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order.

When an officer stops the vehicle at the roadside, the driver is fixed on the spot. A notice disposition under Article 163 then becomes possible and the case travels the criminal-track route, whose terminus is a summary judgment in court. Same speed, same road, same car, and yet the way it was caught decides both the governing statute and the way to contest it. The first thing to establish in front of a notice is not what the violation was but which route you are standing on.

Start by identifying what you received

On the administrative-fine route the paperwork arrives twice. Article 17(1) of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order requires the agency to impose the fine in writing only after completing the opinion-submission procedure, so an advance notice comes first and a notice of imposition comes later. Treat the two as the same thing and let the first go past, and one thing becomes unrecoverable: the voluntary-payment reduction.

Opinion submission - the only window where the amount moves

Article 16(1) of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order provides that before imposing an administrative fine the agency must notify the party in advance and set a period of at least 10 days for submitting an opinion. Under Article 16(2) the party may state an opinion or submit materials, and Article 3(2) of the Enforcement Decree opens that up to oral statement in addition to writing and electronic documents.

What makes this stage cheap is Article 16(3). If the submitted opinion has substantial grounds, the agency may decline to impose the fine or change what it notified. Nothing has been imposed yet, so there is no disposition to revoke, no court and no prosecutor involved, and no cost. Three reductions live here.

  • Voluntary-payment reduction — paying voluntarily within the opinion-submission deadline allows a reduction of up to 20 percent of the fine that would be imposed (Article 18(1); Article 5 of the Enforcement Decree). Pay the reduced amount inside that deadline and the imposition and collection procedure ends there (Article 18(2))
  • Individual-ground reduction — Article 2-2 of the Enforcement Decree provides for a reduction of up to 50 percent for recipients of national basic living security, protected single-parent families, persons with severe disabilities, persons of distinguished service with a disability rating of grade 3 or above, and minors, among others
  • Withdrawal of the imposition itself — the change of notified content under Article 16(3). If the violation or the target party was misidentified and that is sorted out here, none of the later procedure ever opens

What the advance notice itself must contain is also fixed, in Article 3(1) of the Enforcement Decree: the party's name and address; the facts giving rise to the fine, the amount and the applicable law; the name and address of the agency; the fact that an opinion may be submitted and by when; and the fact that a reduction is available on voluntary payment. A missing item is itself a procedural problem.

Objection - the disposition vanishes and the case goes to court

After the notice of imposition, the window is the objection. Article 20(1) provides that within 60 days of receiving the notice the party may file an objection with the issuing agency in writing. The form differs from the previous stage: an opinion may be given orally, an objection may not.

Article 20(2) is what fixes the character of the procedure. Once an objection is filed, the agency's imposition loses its effect. This is not a suit to have a disposition revoked; the disposition simply ceases to exist and the case becomes a matter for the court from the start. The opponent is no longer the agency either.

The agency must forward the case to the competent court within 14 days of receiving the objection, attaching its opinion and supporting documents (Article 21(1)). It does not forward the case where the party has withdrawn the objection, or where the objection is found to have merit and no fine is considered necessary. Either way the party is notified of that fact immediately (Article 21(3)).

  • An objection may be withdrawn in writing until that notification arrives (Article 20(3)). Withdrawal is treated as the imposition becoming final
  • An objection that is out of time, or filed by someone who is not the party, is not dismissed by the agency. It goes to the court and is disposed of by a dismissal decision
  • Because the imposition has lost its effect, the surcharge mechanism on that fine also stops running while the objection is alive

What happens once the court has it

Unless another statute provides otherwise, an administrative fine case falls to the district court, or its branch, for the party's place of address (Article 25). Jurisdiction is fixed as of the moment the agency forwarded the objection (Article 26).

In the ordinary procedure the court must open a hearing date and hear the party's statement (Article 31(1)), and must also seek the prosecutor's opinion (Article 31(2)). The ruling takes the form of a decision with reasons attached (Article 36(1)). Where the court considers it appropriate it may dispose of the matter by summary ruling without a hearing (Article 44), and an objection filed within 7 days of being notified of that ruling returns the case to the ordinary procedure (Article 45(1)). Those 7 days are a peremptory period (Article 45(3)).

Against the decision, the party and the prosecutor may file an immediate appeal, which has the effect of staying execution (Article 38(1)). The appeal period is one week from the day the ruling is notified (Article 40, applying the Civil Procedure Act). A final administrative fine ruling is executed on the prosecutor's order (Article 42(1)).

That is the skeleton of the procedure. This article neither recommends filing an objection or a formal trial request, nor says it is better to simply pay. Which is better depends on the facts and the evidence, and only someone who has seen the case can judge it.

Does objecting reduce the amount

This is the most misread part. There is no window at the objection stage for cutting the amount. The Ministry of Justice commentary on the Act explains why, as a matter of drafting history. For cases where review of an objection shows the imposition is sound but mitigating circumstances would justify a lower figure, a modification-decision mechanism was considered and not adopted — the reasoning being that a modification decision would itself need an avenue of challenge, allowing objections to repeat.

So the agency has exactly two options: uphold the imposition and forward it to the court, or conclude no fine is needed and not forward it. There is no middle value. Nor does the amount drop automatically at the court stage. Article 14 merely directs the agency and the court to consider the motive, purpose, method and result of the violation, the party's attitude and the circumstances afterwards, and the party's age, financial position and environment.

Supreme Court decision of 23 December 1998, 98Ma2866 put the structure this way. An administrative fine ruling by a court is not an administrative litigation procedure reviewing the propriety of the disposition imposed by the competent agency, so the court is not bound by the agency's internal imposition standards and may fix the amount at its discretion within the ceiling set by the relevant statute. For speeding, that ceiling is 200,000 won. The number the agency wrote is neither a floor nor a cap as far as the court is concerned.

Two further things attach. The voluntary-payment reduction exists only inside the opinion-submission deadline, so on this route it is already closed; and if a fine is pronounced, the costs of the proceeding fall on the person against whom it is pronounced (Article 41(1)). An objection, in other words, is not a place to haggle over the figure. It is a place to contest whether the imposition stands at all.

The criminal-track route - contesting means not paying

A notice disposition has no window called an objection. The chief of the police station gives notice to pay by a notice document stating the reasons (Article 163), and the recipient must pay within 10 days (Article 164(1)). Where a natural disaster or another unavoidable cause prevented payment, the period is 5 days from the day that cause ceased. Miss the deadline and 1.2 times the noticed amount must be paid within 20 days from the following day (Article 164(2)).

If it is still unpaid, the chief of the police station requests a summary judgment without delay (Article 165(1)). Non-payment is the expression of intent to contest, and the courtroom is where the contest happens. Two return paths remain open. Paying the noticed amount plus 50 percent before the summary judgment is requested means it will not be requested, and paying the same amount before pronouncement means the request is withdrawn.

If you disagree with the pronouncement, Article 14 of the Act on the Procedure for Summary Judgment allows a request for a formal trial within 7 days. Once a judgment in the formal trial is given, the summary judgment loses its effect (Article 15); and once the request period passes or the request is withdrawn or rejected, the summary judgment has the same effect as a final judgment (Article 16).

  • A person who has paid the penalty is not punished again for that act (Article 164(3)). That covers the act stated in the notice and acts recognised as identical to it, and does not blanket other criminal offences at the same time and place
  • A person whose name or address is uncertain, who is likely to flee, or who refuses to receive the notice is excluded from the criminal-track scheme from the outset and goes straight to summary judgment (Article 162(2))
  • The outcome of this route is a criminal one. The gap between it and the administrative-fine route is far wider than any difference in money
  • The two routes do not overlap. If the driver was punished or received a notice disposition for that violation, no administrative fine may be imposed on the owner (Article 160(4), item 2)

Grounds that are actually argued

Who was driving

This is the clearest channel on the administrative-fine route. Article 160(4), item 3 of the Road Traffic Act states plainly that no fine may be imposed where the driver who committed the violation has been identified as a result of opinion submission under Article 16(2) of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order, or an objection under Article 20(1) of the same Act. That the provision names both stages matters: identification at either one works. But pass through this channel and, in place of the fine, the driver receives the criminal-track penalty and demerit points. It does not disappear; it moves.

Whether the circumstances left no choice

On the criminal route, Article 22(1) of the Criminal Act — necessity — can come into play: an act to avert a present danger to one's own or another's legal interest is not punished where there are reasonable grounds. The threshold the case law sets for reasonableness is not low. The act must be the only means of protecting the endangered interest, must be the method causing the least harm, and the interest preserved must outweigh the interest infringed.

The administrative-fine route has no express counterpart to necessity. Instead Article 7 of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order provides that no fine is imposed for a violation committed without intent or negligence, and Article 10 deals with mental incapacity. Arguments about circumstances are built on those provisions. Theft is listed separately, as a ground barring imposition under Article 160(4), item 1.

Whether the signs or the equipment were defective

Attached Table 6-2 of the Enforcement Rule of the Road Traffic Act provides that when unmanned enforcement equipment is installed, a guide sign announcing the location of the enforcement point or enforcement section must be installed, and a supplementary sign marking the start and the end of the zone or section must be affixed with it. The signs are required, not merely desirable. There are rules on the equipment too: an acceptance inspection on first installation to confirm it meets the installation specification, and a regular inspection once every year to maintain reliability.

Grounds that go nowhere

The other side is worth stating just as plainly. Some arguments come up constantly and never find a foothold in the procedure. Usually for one reason: the element the argument aims at is not in the provision to begin with.

Where enforcement equipment leaves room to argue

The equipment is installed and managed under Article 4-2 of the Road Traffic Act, with detailed standards in Article 8-2 of the Enforcement Rule and Attached Table 6-2. What gets recorded varies by type, so the room to argue sits in different places.

Fixed installations

Mounted permanently at a set point, recording the speed at the moment of passing it. Because the location is fixed, the existence and placement of the guide sign is something that can be checked. Whether the guide sign required by Attached Table 6-2 was actually there, and whether the supplementary sign showed which zone or section it covers, are the checkable points.

Mobile units

Operated from shifting locations, so how the signage stood at that particular spot varies case by case. The management standards in Attached Table 6-2 focus not on location but on inspection of the equipment itself: an acceptance inspection, a regular inspection once every year, and the requirement that the inspecting body be the Korea Road Traffic Authority or an accredited institution recognised by the Korea Laboratory Accreditation Scheme (KOLAS).

Where the inspection standard actually lives

The structure of the rule is worth knowing precisely. Attached Table 6-2 says inspections must be carried out; it carries no figure such as a tolerance. The detailed standards needed for inspection are delegated: they are set by the head of the body operating the unmanned enforcement equipment, taking into account the type and function of the device. Which means that looking for a tolerance range in the text of the law will not produce one.

What section enforcement measures

In section enforcement the unit of the disposition is not a point but a section. Attached Table 6-2 lists the enforcement point and the enforcement section side by side and requires a supplementary sign marking the start and the end of the zone or section, which is what reveals the structure. A rule that requires the start and the end to be identified treats everything between them as a single object of observation.

That is why a common argument loses its force. Saying you were within the limit at some particular point is no answer unless the disposition rests on that point. To contest a section you have to aim at the section itself: whether the start and end signs were actually there, and whether the passage records belong to your vehicle.

Why demerit points do not attach to administrative fines

Demerit points are not a scheme on the same layer as fines or penalties. They are the basis for licence revocation and suspension, with the standards in Article 93 of the Road Traffic Act and Article 91(1) of the Enforcement Rule with Attached Table 28. The target of that measure is the driver. But in unmanned enforcement the driver is never identified, and the fine goes to the registered owner. A measure aimed at the driver cannot issue when no driver has been fixed. Administrative fines carry no points not out of leniency but because the target is different.

Conversely, identify the driver through Article 160(4), item 3 and the fine disappears while that driver receives the penalty and the points. On ordinary roads the demerit points for speeding are as follows.

  • The cumulative score is the sum of points from violations and accidents, minus offsets earned through periods free of violations and accidents
  • Whether from a single event or accumulation, once the points or disposition points reach 40 or more, a suspension is determined and carried out
  • School zones carry heavier standards. This article deals only with ordinary roads
  • Choosing between paying the fine and identifying the driver is not settled by comparing amounts. One side carries no points and the other does

Deadlines - what actually attaches when you miss one

What attaches when a deadline passes is written into the provisions as figures. Article 24(1) of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order provides that where the fine is not paid by the due date, a surcharge of 3 percent of the arrears is collected from the day after the deadline. Article 24(2) adds an aggravated surcharge of 1.2 percent of the arrears for each month that passes thereafter, for a period that may not exceed 60 months. Where no objection was filed and the surcharge is also unpaid, collection follows the procedure for national or local tax arrears (Article 24(3)).

Deadlines run the other way too. An agency may not impose a fine once five years have passed from the day the violation ended (Article 19(1)), and a final fine is extinguished by prescription if it is not collected or executed for five years (Article 15(1)).

When the registered owner is not the driver

The employer or equivalent person defined in Article 56(1) of the Road Traffic Act is a person employing the driver of the vehicle, a person directly managing the driver or the vehicle, or the user of the vehicle. The provision itself is a duty provision: such a person must at all times caution and supervise the driver to observe the Act and orders issued under it. The structure that sends the fine to the registered owner is built by Article 160(3) borrowing that duty provision.

Practice adds a layer. The registered owner on the vehicle register is treated as the employer or equivalent person, and the address entered on the advance notice is that person's address, not the vehicle's place of use. Whether a family member or an employee was driving, the notice reaches the registered owner.

  • Article 160(4) sets four exceptions to that structure: theft or another unavoidable cause; the driver having been punished or having received a notice disposition for that violation; the driver having been identified through opinion submission or an objection; and a rental or lease vehicle where it is clear that the vehicle alone was let out
  • Rentals and leases are listed separately because the split between owner and actual user is clear from the contract. A car lent between individuals does not fall under that item
  • For company-registered vehicles, Article 11 of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order also operates. Where an employee or the like violates a duty imposed on the corporation in the course of business, the fine is imposed on the corporation
  • Where two or more persons take part, each is deemed to have committed the violation (Article 12(1)). It is not a matter of splitting the burden

Naming the wrong driver

Because of the channel in Article 160(4), item 3, identifying the driver makes the owner's fine disappear. Which creates the temptation to write down someone other than the person who was actually there. That point is not a procedural matter but a criminal one.

Article 151(1) of the Criminal Act punishes a person who harbours or lets escape someone who has committed an offence punishable by a fine or heavier with imprisonment for up to 3 years or a fine of up to 5 million won. Falsely stating that you were driving in order to keep the actual violator from punishment is the textbook shape of an application of that provision. Article 151(2) provides a special exemption for relatives or cohabiting family members, but its reach is limited to that relationship.

The reverse direction exists too. Article 156 of the Criminal Act punishes a person who reports false facts to a public office or public official with the purpose of causing another person to be subjected to criminal punishment or disciplinary action, with imprisonment for up to 10 years or a fine of up to 15 million won. Naming as the driver someone who was not driving can reach that provision. Article 153 does provide that where the person confesses or surrenders before the trial or disciplinary decision becomes final, the punishment is mitigated or remitted.

  • The fine is capped at 200,000 won (Road Traffic Act, Article 160(3)). The size of the risk taken on to erase that amount is written into the provisions above
  • Identifying the driver is a normal channel the law has provided. The problem is writing something other than the facts
  • If you are not certain who was driving, saying you are not certain is the only answer that matches the facts
  • Under Articles 22 and 23 of the Act on Regulation of Violations of Public Order the agency may require the party's attendance and order the production of materials
Stated again: this is general procedural information under the law of the Republic of Korea, not a judgment on any individual case. Outcomes turn on the specific facts and evidence, and matters touching the criminal route in particular should go through an attorney or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation rather than your own judgment. Statutes are amended, so the provisions should be rechecked at the time you actually respond.

What I could not verify

  • The tolerance range for speed measuring equipment. Attached Table 6-2 carries no figure. The management standards delegate it — the detailed standards needed for inspection are set by the head of the body operating the equipment — so no tolerance is fixed at the level of the statutory text. I tried to consult the National Police Agency traffic enforcement guidelines, but the posting page would not open, so no figure from it appears here
  • How speed is derived in section enforcement. Attached Table 6-2 governs only the enforcement section and its start and end signs; it contains no formula. I could not locate a statutory basis for the method of calculating speed from passage records
  • The success rate of objections. I could not find official statistics on how often impositions are overturned in administrative fine proceedings. That is why this article makes no claim that objecting is advantageous or disadvantageous
  • Amount tables for school zones and other aggravated areas. That heavier standards apply is confirmed, but the per-zone amount tables are outside this article's scope and are not reproduced
  • An actual case where a court set a higher figure than the agency. The principle that the court fixes the amount at its discretion within the ceiling is confirmed by 98Ma2866, but I could not find a traffic case in which the amount was in fact increased
