AI 2040: Plan A - How to Read the Superintelligence Slowdown Scenario

What the AI Futures Project's long-term scenario actually proposes, and how to read this kind of future-forecasting piece

Jul 12, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: What Does It Mean to Forecast 2040?

The AI safety research group AI Futures Project has released a new scenario document, "AI 2040: Plan A". Its predecessor, "AI 2027," drew significant attention for its two branching endings - a catastrophic outcome from an unchecked race to superintelligence, and a coordinated slowdown that averts it - so a follow-up naturally draws interest. But this one is framed differently from the title down. It calls itself a "Plan," not a "forecast."

This piece focuses on what the project actually argues, and how readers should approach this kind of long-term future scenario. To state the conclusion up front: what this document offers is not a prophecy of "what will happen by 2040" but a policy proposal - "what the authors believe should happen." That distinction itself is the starting point for reading it critically. As of July 2026, this piece is based on research into the original document, its published critiques, and the wider discourse on long-term AI forecasting.

1. What Is "AI 2040: Plan A"? - The Facts, Sourced From the Original

To avoid simply relaying a press release, this section sticks to facts drawn from the original document and its authors' public statements. Interpretation comes later.

1.1 Who Wrote It, and For What

The document was written by Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt, and Daniel Kokotajlo under the AI Futures Project. Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher, is also the lead author of "AI 2027." The team says an unchecked race to superintelligence could end in "human extinction or an irreversible concentration of power," and that Plan A is their positive alternative vision to avoid that outcome.

1.2 What Plan A Actually Proposes

The core of the project's proposal is to deliberately delay superintelligence development to 2040 through international agreement. Specifically, it calls for the US and China to make all AI research fully transparent, put verifiable safeguards in place, allow dozens of companies across many countries - not just a handful - to catch up to the frontier together, and deliberately enter a mutual-verification regime aimed at data centers, modeled loosely on nuclear deterrence logic. The authors are explicit that this is a recommendation, not a prediction. The name itself signals "what should happen," not "what will happen."

1.3 The Timeline and Five Alternative Paths

The milestones the document lays out are: an AI transparency law passing in 2027, AI becoming a major election issue in 2028, a US-China agreement in 2029, a risky phase of automated AI R&D in 2030, a pause at human-level AI in 2035, and a staged scale-up to superintelligence in 2040. The authors compare this slowdown path against four others side by side: Plan D (letting the race continue), Plan C (giving up leadership), Plan B (confronting China head-on), Plan A (verified slowdown - their recommendation), and Plan S (a full stop). The authors themselves call this a "scenario planning" method, explaining that its purpose is not to predict the future but to stress-test a policy proposal through a concrete narrative.

2. What Long-Term AI Forecasting Attempts Mean - and Their Limits

2.1 The Lesson From "AI 2027"

The same authors' earlier "AI 2027" drew considerable attention on release, but its subsequent trajectory illustrates just how hard forecasting is. A physicist-critic writing as titotal pointed out that the model's key acceleration parameter carried no uncertainty analysis. Vitalik Buterin argued that assuming the leading lab's capability would explode while the rest of the world stood still contradicted the scenario's own claims elsewhere. Ali Farhadi of the Allen Institute for AI told the New York Times the forecast did not appear grounded in how the field was actually evolving. The authors themselves later pushed their expected breakthrough from 2027 to the early 2030s, and Kokotajlo's own probability for "AGI by end of 2027" was only about 40% at publication - not even his median estimate.

This history matters because "AI 2040," from the same author group, likely carries the same methodological limits. A scenario with specific years attached reads persuasively, but specificity is not the same thing as accuracy.

2.2 Even Experts Disagree Sharply on Timelines

Long-term AI forecasts vary enormously among experts to begin with. Anthropic's Dario Amodei has forecast AI broadly better than humans at almost everything by 2026-2027, while Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis puts the timeline at five to ten years. Researchers like Yann LeCun and Gary Marcus argue current architectures cannot reach AGI at all. The superforecaster group Samotsvety moved sharply from a 32% probability of AGI by roughly 2042 in 2022 to a 28% probability by 2030 as of January 2026, while the Metaculus community forecast, as of February 2026, sits at 25% by 2029 and 50% by 2033. One striking gap is between expert forecasters and the public: the superforecaster median for AI reaching 80% success on software tasks requiring 8+ hours of human expert effort is 2028, while the public median is a far more conservative 2037. Most skeptics are not arguing AGI is impossible outright, but rather that the current scaling approach is insufficient and fundamental research breakthroughs are still needed. This spread of opinion is itself the reason no single scenario's specific year should be taken as settled.

3. The Landscape of AI-Future Discourse - Acceleration, Alignment, Regulation, Jobs, Governance

3.1 The Acceleration-Slowdown and Alignment Axes

Discourse on AI's future tends to move along a few recurring axes: how fast development should go, how mature alignment and verification technology is at keeping systems within human intent, and what impact all this has on jobs and economic structure. Plan A sits firmly on the slowdown-and-verification axis, resting on the premise that verifiable transparency can prevent both alignment failures and covert racing. That premise is itself contested - whether verification technology is mature enough to underwrite trust between nations is a separate question that needs its own scrutiny.

3.2 The Jobs and Economic-Impact Debate

Separately from the superintelligence discourse, even today's AI is already fueling a labor-market debate. Economists are split on whether AI will act as a complement that lifts productivity and wages together, or a substitute that drives large-scale structural unemployment and widening inequality. Some analyses estimate it will take roughly a decade for AI adoption to become widespread, with a meaningful share of workers facing transition pressure along the way, while other analyses see a far larger share of tasks as automatable. Past technological transitions saw labor markets absorb the shock by creating jobs in new sectors, but whether this automation wave follows the same pattern - given its pace, breadth, and reach into cognitive work - remains an open question. This uncertainty affects far more people, far sooner, than any superintelligence scenario, which makes it no less important than the Plan A-style debate.

3.3 The Reality of Governance - A 2026 Snapshot

Policy reality is far more fragmented than any scenario. In 2026, the EU AI Act became fully enforceable on August 2, becoming the world's first comprehensive, binding AI regulation, while the OECD's AI Policy Observatory tracks over 1,000 AI policy initiatives across 69 countries. A UN-backed Global Dialogue on AI Governance and an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI have also launched. But approaches differ by country: the EU leans on rights- and risk-based regulation, the US favors voluntary standards, and China maintains state control while promoting what it calls inclusive cooperation. There is also a stark asymmetry of scale - global hyperscaler capital spending in 2026 is projected at roughly $527 billion, while the EU AI Act's enforcement budget sits around €1 billion. For a transparency-and-verification proposal like Plan A to become real, the institutional capacity to actually monitor and verify capital at that scale would need to exist first.

4. How to Read a Future-Scenario Piece Like This Critically

4.1 Prediction or Recommendation - How Format Breeds Misreading

One of the more interesting things about Plan A is that the AI Futures Project itself invited a critic to publish a rebuttal alongside the original. Richard Ngo, who worked with the team on the document, called it "selective optimism" and raised three points. First, that a hybrid format mixing prediction and recommendation risks being misread once it gets compressed into a single summary sentence. Second, that the US-China competition frame itself risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy, and that domestic political conflict within each country may end up shaping AI's pace more than international rivalry does. Third, that it assumes rapid change while underweighting the gap, observed over the past decade, between benchmark performance and real-world impact. The fact that this rebuttal was published alongside the original is itself a signal: when reading this genre, do not blur the line between "prediction" and "recommendation."

4.2 Who Is Trying to Persuade Whom

A scenario document always has an intended audience. Plan A is aimed at policymakers, national security experts, and AI policy leaders - it is less a prophecy for the general public than a persuasion document aimed at moving a specific group of decision-makers in a specific direction. When reading this kind of piece, it helps to check the authors' affiliations and prior public statements, and to ask who the document is actually trying to convince. Community reaction also raised doubts about forecast reliability, criticized the model for not fully accounting for economic variables, and flagged that the bottleneck of physical robotic automation gets short shrift. All of this shows that the very choice of which variables a scenario includes already reflects the authors' point of view.

4.3 Optimism and Pessimism Bias

Long-term AI scenarios are structurally vulnerable to bias in both directions. Emphasizing catastrophe draws attention and political momentum but invites charges of exaggeration; offering an optimistic fix runs into skepticism about feasibility. Plan A leans toward the latter. The narrative structure itself - warn of catastrophe, then offer a solution - leaves open the possibility that the authors decided what they wanted first and then built a path to match that conclusion. That is the weight behind the word "selective" in Ngo's critique.

5. What Individuals and Society Can Actually Prepare For

Regardless of whether any specific year plays out, preparing for trends already underway remains worthwhile. At the individual level, it is safer to invest in capabilities that outlast any specific tool - problem framing, judgment, and verification skills - rather than mastery of a particular AI product. At the organizational level, building verification and audit processes around AI adoption now is valuable regardless of whether superintelligence ever arrives, since it applies just as well to today's AI systems. At the societal and institutional level, developing transparency and verification techniques now has value independent of whether Plan A itself ever materializes, because any international agreement needs mature verification technology to be trustworthy. On the other hand, redesigning an individual's career or an organization's strategy wholesale around a scenario's specific year would likely be an overreaction - the uncertainty in these forecasts is already well documented across a wide range.

6. A Korean Reader's Perspective

Korea is not a bystander in this discourse. Since January 22, 2026, the Basic Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of Trust has been in effect, making Korea the second country in the world after the EU to have a comprehensive AI regulatory framework. A National AI Committee chaired by the President oversees policy, and "high-impact AI" across ten domains - hiring, lending, healthcare, education, transportation, and more - faces stricter oversight. The approach also leans toward promotion rather than pure regulation. In the US-China-centered picture Plan A paints, Korea belongs to neither bloc, but sits as a middle power ready to align with international verification and transparency standards if and when they actually materialize. Rather than betting on who wins the superintelligence race, building the institutional groundwork to align with whatever international standard eventually emerges looks like the more realistic strategy for Korea.

Conclusion: A Matter of Preparation, Not Prediction

"AI 2040: Plan A" is a document that states plainly it is a recommendation, not a prediction. Whether or not you agree with that recommendation, what this effort demonstrates are the basics of reading a long-term AI scenario responsibly: separate prediction from recommendation, check the authors and their intended audience, and look for what variables are missing. The most constructive response to this document is not to bet on whether 2040 actually arrives as described, but to separately evaluate the feasibility of the idea at its core - verifiable transparency. As of July 2026, this debate remains unresolved, and it will keep being updated.
