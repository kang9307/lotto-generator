Where Copyleft Compliance Breaks Down - The Duty That Starts at Distribution

It is not publish to the world, it is provide to whoever received it. Most failures begin at the point where that distinction gets skipped

Aug 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: What Begins the Moment You Ship

Think of one firmware image your team shipped last quarter. Can you produce the list of open source components inside it right now? Assume you can. Do the versions on that list match the versions actually inside the binary that went out? Fewer organizations can answer both questions confidently than you would expect.

That is why copyleft compliance gets treated as a question of good faith when it is really a question of tracking. However firm your intention to honor a license, you cannot honor it against an unidentified target. Turn that around and the picture improves: once you know what went in, the rest is mostly clerical.

This piece walks that structure. What copyleft actually requires, what the network era added to it, where practice slips, and what to reconcile before a release goes out. Assigning blame to a particular company is not the job here, and in any case that determination belongs to rights holders and courts.

This is a technical explanation of how these licenses are built and not legal advice. Outcomes in any specific matter depend on how components are combined, how they are distributed, and which jurisdiction applies, so decide your actual response with qualified counsel.

The Claim That Prompted This - And Where Confirmed Fact Ends

Start by cutting the trigger down to its real size. An article by Jake Edge published on LWN on August 26, 2026 reports that a software freedom organization used a conference talk to argue that a 3D printer manufacturer distributes slicer software in a way that does not satisfy the requirements of AGPL version 3. Three strands were described. That complete corresponding source was not provided, that some functionality sits in dynamically loaded libraries, and that some functionality lives in a server-side application run by the manufacturer.

One line matters here. What was raised is an allegation, and no determination of violation has been made. Whether license conditions are met turns on findings about how components combine and how they are distributed, and those findings emerge either from negotiation between the parties or from a legal process. A persuasive-sounding analysis and a legally settled conclusion are different layers. So this article does not name the company, and it does not use the matter as evidence for any conclusion below.

The shape of the claim is still worth studying. Each of the three strands lands exactly where compliance work tends to slip: completeness, boundary drawing, and what happens across a network. The rest of this piece unpacks those three in order.

What Copyleft Requires - Provide, Not Publish

Clear the most common misreading first. GPL-family licenses do not require you to publish source to the world. They require that whoever received the binary can get the source that corresponds to it, on the same terms. The counterparty is specified, and that is the whole point.

Two consequences follow. First, no distribution, no duty. The common understanding is that if you use software only inside your organization and no copy leaves it, the obligation to provide source does not arise. Second, once there is distribution, one recipient is enough. Not having posted anything on a public repository is not a defense.

The scope of corresponding source is written into the text as well. GPL version 2 defines complete source for an executable as all the source for all modules it contains, plus any associated interface definition files, plus the scripts used to control compilation and installation. GPL and AGPL version 3 generalize this to all the source needed to generate, install, run and modify the object code, including scripts to control those activities. Build scripts and configuration are named in the license itself.

The ways of providing it are enumerated rather than open-ended. The practical technique is to decide up front which option your distribution format relies on, and then honor that option all the way through.

RouteSubstance of the conditionWhere it snags in practice
Ship it alongsideCorresponding source travels on the same medium as the binaryPhysically awkward for firmware images and app packages
Written offerA written offer valid for at least three years. Version 3 extends it for as long as you offer spare parts or customer support for that modelYou must keep three years of archives and a working reply channel. Nothing survives reorganizations worse
Same place downloadEquivalent access from the same place the binary is offered, at no further chargeEquivalence breaks easily. Login walls, account restrictions and unanswered request forms are typical

Version 3 adds another layer. When object code ships inside a consumer product, the corresponding source must be accompanied by the information needed to actually install and run a modified version. The concern is that if signing keys or attestation prevent a user from loading firmware they have modified, the freedom to modify becomes hollow even when the source is in hand. This clause is decided at product design time, which makes it the hardest one to satisfy late.

The Line the AGPL Added - Users on the Far Side of a Network

The GPL trigger is the movement of a copy. So what happens when software runs on a server and users only ever see a screen? No copy reaches the user, so there is no distribution, and with no distribution there is no duty to provide source. This is the gap long known as the service provider loophole.

Section 13 of AGPL version 3 aims at that gap. If you modify the program, you must prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network an opportunity to receive the corresponding source. The text specifies from a network server, at no charge, through some standard or customary means.

Three things about this clause are easy to forget in practice.

  • The condition is a modified version. The wording assumes modification. Running the program untouched is a different case and should be treated as one
  • The manner of the offer is specified as prominent. A link existing somewhere and a link a user of the service can actually notice are not the same thing
  • The target is the version running right now. Not the source as of some release, but the corresponding source of the exact modified version people are interacting with. Continuous deployment breaks this more often than anything else
  • It does not ask about a contractual relationship. The text speaks of users interacting remotely and draws no line between paying customers and free users

This also explains why the trigger above drew attention. Moving functionality out of the client and onto a server visibly reduces how much you distribute. The AGPL contains a clause written with exactly that migration in mind. Whether any particular matter falls under section 13 depends on what the server-side component inherits, and that is a question of fact.

Where It Slips in Practice - Six Patterns

Compliance failure is rarely dramatic. Six patterns recur, and nearly all of them come from the gap between what you believe you did and what the recipient actually holds in their hands.

PatternHow it looks from outsideWhat is actually missing
Did not know it was in thereAbsent from the notice list entirelyAwareness that the component exists at all, having arrived inside a vendor board support package or a contractor image
It does not buildA source archive is availableBuild scripts, configuration files, toolchain specification. The license names these explicitly
Versions do not matchSource with the right name is postedThe same revision as the shipped binary. A few patches that never left the internal tree are the usual culprit
Cannot actually obtain itA download link existsEquivalent access. A 404, a login wall or an unanswered request form is typical
Cannot actually install itSource exists and it buildsFor consumer products, the information required to load a modified version onto the device
Boundary drawn wrongConcluded that in-house code is separateAny record of how the combination was assessed. Discussed just below

The sixth is the most contestable. There is no universally accepted answer to where one combined work ends. The Free Software Foundation states in its public FAQ that when a main program dynamically links plug-ins and they call each other's functions and share data structures, it considers them a single combined program. Where communication is limited to invoking the plug-in and waiting for it to return, the FAQ calls it a borderline case.

What matters is that this is a steward's interpretation rather than settled case law. The legal treatment of dynamic linking varies by jurisdiction, and squarely reasoned decisions are hard to find. So the safe posture in practice is narrow: the real hazard is quietly deciding for yourself that dynamic loading makes it separate and keeping no record of the reasoning. Make the call, but write down why. In a later negotiation that document is what demonstrates good faith.

Why It Happens - Tracking Failure Beats Evasion

From the outside, a compliance failure reads as deliberate evasion. In reality failures caused by the absence of an inventory are far more common. Take the structure apart and the reasons are visible.

  • Supply chains are long. A chip vendor board support package goes into a contract manufacturer image, and your app rides on top of that. License information thins out at every handoff
  • Some things exist only at build time. Statically linked libraries, distribution packages inside a container base image, and dependencies fetched by an install script never appear no matter how hard you search the source tree
  • Different people look at different things. Legal sets license policy, developers add dependencies, manufacturing ships. All three look at different tools at different moments
  • The duty is a period, not a moment. The condition is not whether you complied on ship day but whether you can still hand over source three years or a support lifetime later. It fails silently when staff change
  • Notice and provision get merged in people's heads. Including the license text is one duty and handing over corresponding source is another. Doing the first and dropping the second is common

One more. Acquisitions and outsourcing sever license history. Sometimes nobody, including the party that handed it over, knows what is inside an inherited codebase, and it ships in the next product anyway. Malice does not explain this class of failure, which is why the remedy is not ethics training but the mechanical routine of inventory and reconciliation.

How to Verify - Three Layers of Inventory, Scan, Reconcile

Knowing what each layer guarantees and what it cannot ages better than memorizing tool names.

Layer 1. Inventory - What Went In

A component inventory, usually called an SBOM. It is only useful if it is machine readable, and two standard formats are in wide use. The fields it carries tend to group like this.

  • Identity - component name, version, supplier, and an identifier that distinguishes it from anything else
  • Relationships - what depends on what. License analysis turns on these edges
  • Provenance - who produced the document and when. Without a timestamp it cannot be trusted
  • Terms - the license identifier attached to each component, using the short names from the standard list

It is worth knowing that this is becoming a regulatory requirement. The European Union Cyber Resilience Act requires, in its annex, a software bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies, with the relevant obligations applying in full from December 11, 2027. The inventory you build for license compliance and the one security regulation demands are the same data used for two purposes.

Layer 2. Scan - What Terms Are Attached

Find license texts and code fingerprints across the source tree and the binaries. The limits are equally clear. Files without a license header, copies whose notices an intermediate supplier rewrote, and code fragments absorbed statically all slip past. A clean scan does not mean nothing is there. It means this method did not find it.

Layer 3. Reconcile - Does What You Shipped Match What You Handed Over

The layer most often skipped, and the one that catches four of the six patterns above. The principle is simple. Treat the artifact as the source of truth, not the declaration.

  • Extract components back out of the shipped image and match them against the inventory. Do not take the manifest on trust
  • Build the published source in a clean environment following your own documentation. It only counts with no internal cache in play
  • Where possible, use a reproducible build and check the hashes. If they differ, being able to explain the difference is enough
  • Exercise the provision route from an outside connection with no account. Working only from the corporate network is a genuinely common failure
  • Confirm the path by which notice files reach the end user. Settings menu or printed insert, it has to be visible in fact

The Rights Holder Side - From Notice to Legal Process

What people on the receiving end most want to know is what happens next. At the level of general principle it looks like this.

Copyleft obligations rest on a copyright license. The common understanding is that use outside the conditions the license attaches is use beyond the scope of permission, which lets a rights holder raise the issue on copyright grounds. Separately, some approaches frame the license as a contract and treat the matter as breach of contract, and some go further and test whether a product purchaser can demand source as a third-party beneficiary of that contract. There is an active matter on that last point in the United States, and it is not at the stage where anyone can call the doctrine settled.

The process normally moves in steps. Organizations that publish community enforcement principles state in their public document that the goal is achieving compliance rather than punishment, that legal action is a last resort, and that keeping early discussions confidential improves the odds of agreement. The premise is that most violations are mistakes rather than intent.

The license text carries its own recovery mechanism. GPL version 3 and AGPL version 3 provide that rights are reinstated provisionally once you cease all violation, and permanently if the copyright holder fails to notify you within 60 days of that cessation. They also provide permanent reinstatement where the notice is the first you have received from that holder and you cure within 30 days of receiving it. By contrast, GPL version 2 contains no automatic reinstatement. Given how many older embedded components are still under version 2, that difference is not a small one in practice.

The posture this implies is unambiguous. Silence after a notice is the worst available move. The text itself opens a cure window, and the enforcing parties publicly place negotiation first.

What the Distributing Side Should Have Ready

Building these after being contacted costs far more than having them. The five below have to stay alive for as long as the product is on the market.

ArtifactContentsHow long it must hold
Notice fileLicense texts, copyright notices, statement of modification, delivered together with the productThe full product lifetime. Regenerate on every firmware update
Source archiveCorresponding source, build scripts and toolchain pinning frozen as one bundle per shipped buildTagged per model and firmware version, marked as do-not-delete
Provision routeWhich option you rely on among alongside, written offer, or same place, recorded in writingFor a written offer, at least three years plus the parts and support period
Intake channelAn address that receives source requests and a documented response procedure, with fallback routingAn organizational account rather than a person. Include it in handover checklists
Network noticeIf AGPL components are in play, the source location for the running version, placed where users can see itWired into the deployment pipeline so it updates with the version

Add one procedure and most of this takes care of itself. Put inventory refresh, scan and reconcile into the release gate, and never drop the reconcile step. Tools run the first two for you, but the third needs a person to do it once from the recipient's position. Thirty minutes spent on a fresh laptop with no account, clicking the link and building what comes down, buys back a month of negotiation half a year later.

The Other Side - Compliance Genuinely Costs Money

Treating this purely as a moral question fails to describe the work. Compliance costs money and people, and the burden falls hardest in inverse proportion to size.

  • Small manufacturers have nobody assigned. With no dedicated license reviewer, a developer picks it up on the side, and that developer is bound to the ship date
  • If upstream will not give you a list, downstream cannot build one. When a chip vendor support package arrives in binary form, the means to inspect what is inside are themselves limited
  • A written offer means three years of infrastructure. Archive storage, preserved build environments and staff to answer requests all come with it, and it outlives the product's end of sale
  • Toolchains are sometimes closed. If the compiler for a particular chip carries redistribution restrictions, providing a fully reproducible build becomes hard by itself
  • Price-competitive markets have no slack. The structural difficulty of spending unit cost on something the buyer never perceives is real

None of which shrinks the obligation. What changes is where the remedy sits. A heavy burden does not argue for leniency. It argues for choosing at design time. The cost of a decision made while picking components is small, and the cost of extracting one after shipping is large. If a component's terms are genuinely burdensome, looking for an alternative implementation or negotiating a separately licensed commercial edition is open from the start.

For larger organizations the story inverts. Inventories and archives already have to exist because of security regulation, and license compliance is one more layer laid on top of them. Plenty of organizations still run the two as separate programs, and merging them takes the cost close to half.

Conclusion: Three Sentences

First, copyleft asks not for publication to the world but for provision to whoever received the binary, and that provision has to be complete down to the build scripts. Second, the AGPL reaches the network case where no copy ever moves, which makes moving functionality onto a server a licensing decision as much as an architectural one. Third, most failures come from a missing inventory rather than bad intent, so the remedy lives in the mechanical routine of inventory, scan and reconcile.

If you take one thing from this, take reconcile. Pick a recently shipped product, download its source from an outside connection with no account, and build it following your own documentation. Wherever that stalls is your compliance gap, and if it does not stall the paperwork mostly follows. A license is judged not by the intention to honor it but by what the recipient actually ends up holding.
