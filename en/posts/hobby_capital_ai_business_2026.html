Why Hobbies Become Business Opportunities in the AI Era - Domain Knowledge Is the New Moat

As LLMs flatten general knowledge work, defensibility moves from data to deep hobbies and domain experience

Jul 8, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: Why "Hobbies" Are Suddenly a Business Story

A post on GeekNews (Korea's Hacker News-style community) caught my attention recently. Titled "Why Hobbies Become Business Opportunities in the AI Era," it argues that as LLMs commoditize knowledge work, the axis of competitive advantage shifts toward domain knowledge and what the author calls hobby capital. I largely agree with the thesis, but I wanted to work out exactly why it holds, and where it overreaches. This is not a summary of that post - it is my own research and reconstruction of the argument, written as of July 2026.

Three questions matter here. What does it actually mean for LLMs to make knowledge work cheap? Why is personal data - long assumed to be a moat - losing that status? And under what conditions does a hobby actually convert into a business? I will take them one at a time.

1. What "LLMs Commoditize Knowledge Work" Actually Means

1.1 Declarative Knowledge vs. Tacit Knowledge

Knowledge splits roughly into two categories. Declarative knowledge is anything you can state cleanly in language - "I prefer coffee with more acidity," or "here's what this library's API does." Tacit knowledge is the opposite: the feel for adjusting your pour based on how coffee grounds bloom, built only through repetition, never fully reducible to words. LLMs are extraordinarily good at the former. Search, summarization, first drafts, and first-pass analysis - the work of gathering and organizing information - now take seconds instead of hours.

1.2 The Data Behind Domain Knowledge's Power

This isn't just theory. In a report Anthropic published in June 2026, the company analyzed roughly 400,000 Claude Code sessions from about 235,000 users between October 2025 and April 2026. The single strongest predictor of session success wasn't coding background - it was domain knowledge. Software engineers hit a 34% verified success rate versus 29% for non-software occupations, a gap of just five points, with the top ten occupations clustered within a roughly seven-point band. In other words, how well you understand the problem separated outcomes more than how well you can code. Coding itself - a codifiable, language-transferable skill - has already been commoditized; what's left as the deciding variable is domain understanding.

1.3 "Commoditization" Applies to Part of Knowledge Work, Not All of It

It's easy to misread this as "LLMs make all expertise worthless." That's not the claim. What's actually happened is that the cost of acquiring and organizing language-expressible, already-documentable knowledge has collapsed toward zero. As a result, simply knowing information is worth less, while knowing what to do with that information is worth relatively more.

2. Why Personal Data Is No Longer a Moat

2.1 Personal Context Travels Easily

For years, Silicon Valley's working assumption was that owning customer data creates lock-in. But data about an individual - preferences, habits, conversation history - turns out to be remarkably portable. Chat export features, context-interchange protocols like MCP, or simply a user typing a few sentences describing who they are, are often enough to reproduce nearly the same personalization on a different service. Switch providers, and "you" come with you.

2.2 Lock-In Strategies Are Weakening

Competition among AI providers is accelerating this. As vendors race to support conversation portability, file-based memory, and standardized context protocols, the old premise - your data lives here, so you can't leave - is eroding. Services that once prided themselves on rich personalization data are the most exposed to this shift.

2.3 That Doesn't Mean Data Is Worthless

This isn't an argument that data has no value. It's that individual-level context data is portable. By contrast, data that's bound to a community, where the value is the network of other users, or that only reveals patterns once observations accumulate across many people - thousands of recurring mistakes in a given sport, for instance - remains hard to move. The moat is decided not by whether data exists, but by whether it's portable.

3. The Real Defensibility Comes From Domain Knowledge and Hobby Capital

3.1 Tacit Knowledge Doesn't Migrate

Management research is converging on the same conclusion. In a March 2026 piece, California Management Review argued that tacit knowledge is the next competitive moat: real organizational differentiation comes not from data or models themselves, but from the tacit knowledge embodied in people's judgment, and the winners will be whoever structures that knowledge into their systems most effectively. The often-cited example is legal-AI startup Harvey, which partnered with elite law firms specifically to extract the kind of legal reasoning that exists nowhere in any training dataset, because it only exists in senior partners' heads.

3.2 Defining a Hobby: The Process Is the Point, and It Has to Be You

The original author defines a hobby as an activity where the process itself, not the outcome, is the goal, and where value only exists if you are the one doing it. A robot finishing your hike for you accomplishes nothing. That definition matters because no matter how capable LLMs get, they cannot do the process of embodying a skill on your behalf. Information can be copied. Experience cannot.

3.3 Where Domain Knowledge and Hobby Capital Overlap

Domain knowledge and hobbies are, structurally, the same kind of asset. Both accumulate only through repeated practice and failure, resist full translation into language, and can't be easily copied by outsiders. The only real difference is how they're acquired - one through a career, the other through voluntary immersion. The more LLMs erode the value of declarative knowledge, the more the value of both rises in relative terms.

4. Three Mechanisms That Turn a Hobby Into a Business

4.1 Community Moats

Build a hub where people who share a hobby gather, and the community itself becomes an asset competitors can't replicate. Running and cycling activity-tracking platforms are a frequently cited example: users stay not because the features are unbeatable, but because the people they know are already there.

4.2 Recurring, Habitual Revenue

Deeply understanding an individual user's recurring patterns or mistakes, and turning that into continuously updated feedback, converts naturally into subscription revenue. This model works especially well for hobbies that require repeated practice - chess, golf - because the product isn't a one-off piece of information, it's a service that keeps watching and understanding you better, which lowers churn.

4.3 A Domain Workflow: Diagnose, Prescribe, Track

The third mechanism goes beyond simple measurement. Instead of just showing a number, it diagnoses the root cause, suggests a specific action, and tracks whether that action worked - a loop applied to a physical hobby. Designing that loop requires someone who deeply understands the domain, and that knowledge itself becomes the barrier to entry.

4.4 Trust and Physical Experience as the Last Line of Defense

All three mechanisms share a common foundation: trust and lived experience. Online information is now available to anyone in seconds, but the trust that this person has actually done this for years and can give advice suited to my situation still only accumulates through time and relationship. That trust is the final bridge that turns hobby capital into a business.

5. The Era of the Solo Creator Productizing a Hobby With AI Tools

5.1 Production and Distribution Barriers Have Collapsed

Generative AI tools have sharply cut the cost of work that once required a whole team - building a website, editing content, drafting initial marketing copy. Surveys report that 91% of creators now use generative AI regularly. You no longer need to know how to code or design to ship something credible.

5.2 The Bottleneck Shifted From "Ability to Build" to "What You Know"

Paradoxically, this makes domain knowledge more valuable, not less. In the past, a good idea often died simply because you couldn't find a developer or designer. With that bottleneck gone, the remaining differentiators are what you choose to build and how deeply you understand the hobby. Building capability has been commoditized; knowing something has become relatively more visible.

5.3 What This Looks Like in Practice

The patterns that actually show up are fairly consistent: paid communities built around the common mistakes beginners make in a specific hobby, subscription coaching services that personalize feedback for hobbies requiring repeated practice, and small classes that combine offline meetups with online content. The common thread is depth - shallow information delivery is already free, courtesy of LLMs, so what survives is content and relationships one level deeper than that.

6. Guard Against the Hype - Not Every Hobby Becomes a Business

6.1 The Reality Is Sobering

Some balance is needed here. Creator-economy research suggests that only about 4% of creators earn more than $100,000 a year. Most hobby-based activity remains a hobby, and that's how it should be. "A hobby can become a business" should not be read as "my hobby will inevitably become one."

6.2 The Conditions That Make Conversion Realistic

Hobbies that actually convert tend to satisfy several conditions at once: a clearly defined niche where you can identify people with a specific recurring problem, a problem that recurs often enough to sustain ongoing engagement, and years of your own trial and error that built tacit knowledge others can't easily copy. Miss any one of these, and you may still have a great hobby - just not a viable business.

6.3 Loving Something and Being Able to Teach It Are Different Skills

Another common trap is assuming "I love this hobby, so I'll naturally be good at creating content about it." Enjoying an activity and guiding or teaching others through it are different skills. That gap is a major reason so few long-time hobbyists successfully convert their passion into a business.

7. Opportunities and Conditions Through a Korean Lens

7.1 The Backdrop: Rising Leisure Spending

Payment data cited in the original hobby-capital piece shows leisure and experience spending rising from roughly 9.5% to 13% of total consumer spend. Korea has seen a similar drift since the 52-hour workweek and work-life-balance culture took hold - leisure and hobby spending has been climbing steadily. The more AI absorbs repetitive work and hands time back, the more that time is likely to flow into hobby consumption.

7.2 Korea-Specific Platforms and the Language Barrier

Naver Cafe, KakaoTalk open chats, and domestic platforms like Kmong and Class101 are already established infrastructure for hobby communities and knowledge transactions in Korea. The language barrier slows global platforms' penetration of the Korean market - and for anyone who can explain things in Korean, tailored to Korean context, it doubles as a moat. LLMs already handle translated English-language content well; what's harder to replace is advice from someone who understands Korea's specific regions, equipment availability, and regulatory context.

7.3 A Practical Starting Order

My recommended sequence: start by writing down what's hard to explain in words about your own hobby - the tacit part. Then find a community where people already share that specific problem, and test the reaction to small-scale advice or content. If there's a response, structure it gradually into a subscription, class, or community. Chasing a business registration and a finished product from day one is riskier than running small, falsifiable experiments first.

8. Conclusion - Three Things You Can Apply Today

The claim that LLMs commoditize knowledge work isn't hype - it's backed by data. But what disappears as a result is the value of knowing declarative information, not the value of domain experience and tacit knowledge. The old assumption that personal data is a moat is eroding, and what fills that gap is community-bound data and, above all, hobby and domain knowledge built through lived experience. That said, not every hobby converts - conversion is realistic only when niche specificity, recurrence, and accumulated tacit knowledge are all present.

8.1 Document Your Own Tacit Knowledge

It doesn't have to be polished. Writing a single page on what you clearly know but can't easily explain in words about your hobby is often enough to reveal which parts of your knowledge are a real asset.

8.2 Validate Small, Inside a Niche Community

Skip the grand business plan. Test the reaction with a few pieces of free advice or content inside a community that already exists. If there's no response, the cost of changing direction is far lower.

8.3 Let AI Tools Handle Production - Trust Is Still Yours to Build

Hand website and content production off to AI tools, but remember that trust and relationships can't be outsourced. For wider context, see my analysis on redesigning expertise in the AI age and my piece on the age of the solo founder.
