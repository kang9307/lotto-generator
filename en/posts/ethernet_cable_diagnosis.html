This article covers only what could be confirmed in the Microsoft Ethernet troubleshooting document, the Intel support articles (000038520, 000058908 and 000005593) and a summary of the TIA-568 cable grades, and its scope is diagnosing wired LAN connection failures and a link stuck at 100 Mbps.

Starting Point - Cable, PC or Router Port

Troubleshooting a wired connection starts with sorting out whether the cause lies in the cable, the adapter in the PC, the port on the router, or beyond that in the modem and the carrier. The Microsoft Ethernet troubleshooting document divides this into three zones: the router side, the PC side and another PC. The point of this layout is to change one zone at a time. The document presents a wired connection as plugging one end of a cable into the router at home and the other end into the Ethernet port of the PC to get a fast internet connection, and it says that a PC without an Ethernet port can consider a USB to Ethernet adapter.

What the document asks to look at first on the router side is the cable and the port. Confirm the Ethernet cable is securely plugged into the Ethernet port on both the router and the PC, and on the router confirm it is in a regular Ethernet port and not the Internet port that connects the modem. If one cable does not work and another is nearby, swap it in, and if that works, the document says the first cable might be the problem. There is also advice to try Wi-Fi if the router supports it, which the document describes as a way to both get an internet connection and narrow down the source of the problem.

The last zone is another PC. If another Windows PC and a USB to Ethernet adapter are available at home, connect to the same network with that PC. If it connects, the document says the cause is likely with the first PC, and if neither PC can connect over Ethernet, there might be a problem with the router, the internet service provider or the USB to Ethernet adapter.

  • Check both ends of the cable: confirm it is firmly seated in the Ethernet ports of the router and the PC (Microsoft).
  • Check the port type: confirm it is not in the Internet port used to connect the modem on the router (Microsoft).
  • Try another cable: if another Ethernet cable works, the first cable might be the problem (Microsoft).
  • Compare using Wi-Fi or another PC: connect to the same router over Wi-Fi or from another PC to narrow down where the problem is (Microsoft).

Narrowing a problem down from speed test results is covered in the internet speed test guide. This article concentrates on cable grades and the checking order.

Step 0 - Checking the Connection Status and Restarting the Modem and Router

The first thing to look at on the PC is the Ethernet connection status shown at the top of the Network & internet screen in Settings. The Microsoft document says that on Windows 11 it should show Connected under the name, and on Windows 10 it should show You're connected to the Internet under the network connection. If an error such as Action needed appears, it says to select Ethernet and check the Ethernet connection settings.

The same document also lists restarting the modem and the wireless router as a router-side check. Unplug the power cables of the router and the modem, remove the battery if the modem has a backup battery and its lights stay on, and wait at least 30 seconds or so. Then plug in the modem first and wait for its lights to stop blinking, plug in the router, and after a few minutes, when both devices are fully on, try the Ethernet connection again from the PC. This order and waiting time are what the Microsoft document says, and other vendors' documents can give a different power-on order and waiting time. For a device that combines a cable modem and a router, only that one device is restarted with the same procedure.

  • Normal display on Windows 11: Connected appears under the Ethernet name on the Network & internet screen.
  • Normal display on Windows 10: You're connected to the Internet appears under the network connection.
  • Error display: if wording such as Action needed appears, select Ethernet and look at its settings.
  • Restart order: according to the document, connect the modem first and connect the router when its lights stop blinking.

Reading Port LEDs - The Meaning of a Color Differs by Model

The lights on an Ethernet port show whether a link has been established and at what speed, but how colors map to meanings differs by manufacturer and model. The table below carries over what the Intel support article gives for the Ethernet Server Adapter I210-T1. It describes that one adapter and is no basis for applying it directly to other adapters or to router and switch ports.

LEDStateMeaning in the I210-T1 document
Link/ActivityOffNo Link
Link/ActivityOnLink
Link/ActivityBlinkingActivity
SpeedNot illuminated10Mb/s
SpeedGreen100Mb/s
SpeedAmber1 Gb/s

In this table green is 100Mb/s and amber is 1Gb/s. A general rule such as green meaning gigabit is not supported by this document, and on this adapter it is in fact the reverse. So the speed should not be asserted from the LED color alone, and the mapping between color and speed has to be looked up in the manual of the device in question. How a fully dark LED is read also depends on the model, and in the table above it is the Link/Activity LED being off that means no link.

  • Link/Activity LED: off means no link, on means link, and blinking means activity (I210-T1).
  • Speed LED: not illuminated means 10Mb/s, green means 100Mb/s and amber means 1Gb/s (I210-T1).
  • Differences by model: the same color can mean a different speed on another model, so consult the manual.
  • Do not conclude from color alone: confirm the mapping between color and speed in the manual of the device in question.

Symptom 1 - When a Gigabit Link Stays at 100 Mbps

For the symptom where a gigabit line has a link that stays at 100 Mbps, the Intel support article (000058908) explains that cable problems are the most common cause. In the document's wording, a failed Cat5e or Cat6 cable will usually still work at 100 Mbps. That is why cable problems are easy to miss, since the link does not drop completely. The document also gives examples such as a cable run over by a chair or stepped on, where physical damage can degrade performance.

The responses the document gives fall into three lines. The first is replacing the cable with Cat6 or better. Check that each cable is labeled Cat6 or Cat7 and that the connector is seated firmly enough to give an audible click. The second is the adapter setting. In the Advanced tab in Device Manager, Speed & Duplex is left on Auto Negotiation, and the document says this is the correct setting for gigabit speeds. The third is the equipment. Plug into a different port on the access point, hub, switch or router, and power cycle the network equipment.

Finally there is a test of connecting the computer directly to the modem. The document says to connect directly with a proven Cat6 cable, and it explains the purpose as sorting out whether intermediate equipment or the cabling is involved in the problem. Like the other-cable test in the Microsoft document seen earlier, it changes only one element at a time.

  • Replace the cable: a bad cable can still run at 100 Mbps, so try a Cat6 or better cable.
  • Check the cable marking: see whether each cable is printed with Cat6 or Cat7.
  • Connector seating: push it in firmly enough to hear a click.
  • Auto Negotiation: see whether Speed & Duplex in the Advanced tab of Device Manager is on Auto Negotiation.
  • Other ports and direct connection: use another port on the router or switch, and test by connecting directly to the modem with a proven cable.

Cable Grades - The Differences Among Cat5e, Cat6 and Cat6a

Cable grades are distinguished by the bandwidth they support and by how far they can carry 10 gigabit Ethernet (10GBASE-T). The table below shows values confirmed in a TIA-568 summary. The text of the standard itself is a paid document and could not be opened directly, so it should be noted that the table is at summary level.

GradeMaximum bandwidth1000BASE-T10GBASE-T
Cat5e100 MHzSupported to 100 mNo official support
Cat6250 MHzSupported to 100 mUp to 55 m in typical conditions
Cat6a500 MHzSupported to 100 mFull 100 m

The length limit applies to the whole channel. The 100 m is summarized as the 90 m permanent link inside the building walls plus a total of 10 m of patch cords at both ends. So using a long patch cord on a desk reduces the margin left for the permanent link. Judging from the values in the table alone, all three grades support 1000BASE-T up to 100 m, so the place where the difference between grades clearly shows up in the specification is 10GBASE-T. What the table means is that even Cat5e is enough on paper for 1 Gbps at home, and the fact that the Intel document still recommends Cat6 or better as the replacement is advice based on the cases of failed cables.

  • Cat5e: up to 100 MHz, supports 1000BASE-T to 100 m, but does not officially support 10GBASE-T.
  • Cat6: up to 250 MHz, and 10GBASE-T is supported up to 55 m in typical conditions.
  • Cat6a: up to 500 MHz, and 10GBASE-T is supported over the full 100 m.
  • Channel length: the limit is 100 m, made up of a 90 m permanent link and 10 m of patch cords.

Symptom 2 - When the Connection Fails Entirely: The Windows Document Sequence

If the connection still does not come up after changing the cable, look at the software on the PC. The Microsoft document says to run the Network and Internet troubleshooter in the Get Help app on Windows 11, and the Network troubleshooter under Network and Internet, Status in Settings on Windows 10. The following steps go from the one with the smallest impact to the largest.

The first is the commands. In an administrator Command Prompt, run netsh winsock reset, netsh int ip reset, ipconfig /release, ipconfig /renew and ipconfig /flushdns in that order, then see whether the problem is solved. The document explains that these commands manually reset the TCP/IP stack, release and renew the IP address, and flush the DNS client cache. The second is uninstalling the Ethernet adapter driver. Pick the Ethernet adapter under Network adapters in Device Manager, choose Uninstall device, tick Attempt to remove the driver for this device, and restart, after which Windows installs the latest driver automatically. The document says to consider this when the connection stopped working after a recent update, and for a PC that cannot get online, it urges downloading the manufacturer's driver on another PC and keeping it on a USB drive. For this, the PC manufacturer and the model name or number must be known. If Windows does not install a driver automatically after the removal, install the backup driver saved beforehand.

The last is network reset. The document makes this the last resort when none of the earlier steps help. It removes the installed network adapters and their settings and reinstalls them with default values after a restart, and it is run under Network & internet, Advanced network settings, Network reset on Windows 11, and under Status, Network reset on Windows 10. The document notes the side effects: VPN client software or Hyper-V virtual switches may need to be set up again, and known networks may be switched to the public profile. The document also says this method can help with connection problems after upgrading from Windows 10 to Windows 11, and with the case where the internet works but shared network drives cannot be reached.

  • Troubleshooter: run it from the Get Help app on Windows 11 and from the Status screen in Settings on Windows 10.
  • Command order: run winsock reset, int ip reset, release, renew and flushdns one after another.
  • Uninstall the driver: tick the driver removal item under Uninstall device and restart, with a backup driver downloaded in advance.
  • Network reset: used as the last resort, and adapters and settings return to defaults.
  • Side effects of the reset: VPN and virtual switches may need to be set up again and the network may switch to the public profile.
  • Test with Wi-Fi: if the router supports Wi-Fi, connect to it to tell whether the problem is only with Ethernet.

Adapter Settings and Equipment - Auto Negotiation and Other Ports

If the speed and duplex mode negotiated by the adapter do not match, the link becomes unstable. The Intel document (000005593) includes a LINK_DUPLEX_MISMATCH message that reports a duplex mismatch, and it says to change the speed and duplex settings appropriately on the Link Speed tab. This is a description of an event log message, and this document alone does not allow generalizing about which setting fails when.

The Intel document (000058908) says that for gigabit, leaving Speed & Duplex on Auto Negotiation is correct, and it points out that the idea that a dedicated 1.0 Gbps item should appear is a misconception. On the equipment side, change ports and power cycle the equipment, trying a different port on a hub, switch or router, in the document's own words. Before replacing equipment, the element at fault should be sorted out one at a time, and this is the method both companies' documents use in common.

  • Link Speed tab: if a duplex mismatch message appears, check the speed and duplex settings (Intel).
  • Keep Auto Negotiation: on gigabit, Auto Negotiation is the correct setting (Intel).
  • Where the setting is: the Speed & Duplex item is on the adapter's Advanced tab in Device Manager (Intel).
  • Other ports and a restart of the equipment: use another port on the router or switch and power cycle the equipment (Intel).

The Checking Order in Summary

Combining the documents above gives an order in which only one thing is changed at a time. Start with what is cheap and easy to undo, and put steps that erase settings last. The order below is this article's arrangement of the documents' recommendations, and no single document gives this order as it stands.

  • 1. Check both ends of the cable and the router port type (that it is not the Internet port).
  • 2. Swap in another Ethernet cable and test. If that works, the first cable might be the problem.
  • 3. Look at the connection status under Network & internet in Settings, and move to another port on the router.
  • 4. Restart the modem and router, or test by connecting directly to the modem.
  • 5. Run the five Windows commands in order and, if needed, uninstall the driver and restart.
  • 6. Use network reset last, and set up software such as VPN again afterward.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • A general mapping between LED colors and link speed could not be confirmed. What was confirmed in the Intel document is the values for one model, the I210-T1.
  • The menu path for viewing the current link speed in Windows does not appear in the Microsoft Ethernet troubleshooting document, so it could not be confirmed.
  • How many wires of the cable 1000BASE-T and 100BASE-TX use, and what happens to the speed if one wire breaks, could not be confirmed from an official source that opened.
  • The minimum bend radius of a cable, whether Cat6 has an internal cross separator, and the degree of alien crosstalk shielding could not be confirmed from an official source that opened.
  • Whether negotiation fails when the speed is forced to 1.0 Gbps could not be confirmed. The Intel document goes only as far as saying that Auto Negotiation is the correct setting.
  • There is no source with readable text for the wired internet check guidance of carriers such as KT, so it was left out of this article's basis.
  • The text of the TIA-568 and IEEE 802.3 standards could not be opened directly, and the per-grade values were confirmed at summary level.
