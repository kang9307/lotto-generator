What the GPT-5.6, Grok 4.5, Claude, and Muse Spark Build-Off Actually Shows

Rethinking how to pick a coding agent, based on an experiment where 12 models built the same 4 apps -- as of July 2026

Jul 13, 2026 Tech

Introduction: Why "build the same thing and compare" is back

In the first week of July 2026 alone, GPT-5.6, Grok 4.5, and Muse Spark 1.1 all shipped within three days of each other. Every time a new model drops, the vendor's own benchmark table arrives measured under its own conditions, which makes cross-vendor comparison genuinely hard. That is the backdrop for a format developer communities keep returning to: the build-off. Give several models the exact same prompt for the exact same app, and put the outputs side by side for a visual comparison. That is exactly the shape of an experiment AI dev-tooling company TryAI recently published. Twelve models each built the same 4 apps, 5 times each, and the authors published every result while explicitly stating that this is an observation, not a scientific judgment.

This piece is not a straight recap of that experiment's numbers. It is an analysis of why this topic caught fire on hada.io, and -- more importantly -- how useful this comparison format actually is for developers making real decisions. The short version: a build-off feels more tangible than a benchmark table, but it has real holes as a ranking system. Below is where to trust it and where to discount it.

1. What exactly did this build-off do

Getting the experimental design right matters, because it shapes everything that follows.

1.1 Four tasks

The tasks were four frontend visual deliverables: a Doom-style first-person 3D maze raycaster (WASD movement, wall shading, collision detection), an animated, scramble-and-solve 3D Rubik's cube, a calculator with correct operator precedence, and Conway's Game of Life with a clickable grid and animated generations. All four share one trait: they are finished outputs you can judge with your eyes, not a few lines of code. That is both a strength and a limitation, discussed below.

1.2 The 12-model lineup

Participants split into two groups. The frontier (closed, top-tier) group included all three GPT-5.6 tiers (Sol, Terra, Luna), Grok 4.5, GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Claude Fable 5, and Muse Spark 1.1. The open-weight group included GLM-5.2, Qwen 3.7 Plus, DeepSeek V4 Pro, and Kimi K2.6. Notably, GPT-5.6 (July 9), Grok 4.5 (July 8), and Muse Spark 1.1 (July 9) all launched within days of each other right before the experiment, which is part of why it drew attention -- it was one of the first comparisons to put all three brand-new models in the same room.

1.3 The limitations the methodology admits up front

What stands out is that the authors did not hide the methodology's limits. Each task-model pair was repeated 5 times, and judgments were made by a single evaluator, subjectively. Every output was published so readers could verify results themselves. The authors state plainly that this "is not an objective judgment." Quoting the numbers without that framing is a misreading of the experiment's own intent.

2.1 The gap widens as tasks get more complex

On tasks that combine 3D physics, collision, and animation -- the raycaster and the Rubik's cube -- success rates varied sharply by model. In the authors' own words, "the gap is substantial and not close" for complex work. What is genuinely interesting is that Claude Opus 4.8 went 5/5 on the calculator but never produced a perfect Rubik's cube solve. The fact that the same model's ranking flips depending on the task is arguably the single most useful takeaway from this experiment.

2.2 On simple tasks, open-weight models hold their own

Conversely, on tasks likely well-represented in training data -- the calculator and Game of Life -- open-weight models didn't fall behind. The authors summarize this as: competitive on well-known tasks, but still struggling on novel or complex ones. Qwen 3.7 Plus finishing Game of Life in $0.04 and 11 seconds across 5 runs is an extreme example, but the underlying direction lines up with a pattern seen repeatedly across other coding benchmarks too.

2.3 The speed/cost axis: when quality is close, price decides

What makes this experiment particularly useful is that it published cost and time alongside success rate. Even among models that hit 5/5, GPT-5.6 Luna was the cheapest and fastest at around 1.0 second and $0.001, while GPT-5.6 Sol was far slower and pricier for a similar result. The authors' description of Grok 4.5 as "an alternative execution model you can pick without hesitation if cost matters" comes directly from this axis. When quality differences are small, the real contest shifts to who can deliver the same result cheaper and faster.

3. What the "build-off" format is good for -- and where it breaks down

There are real reasons this format is trending, and equally real limits.

3.1 Strength: closer to real usage

Standard benchmarks, precisely because their scoring criteria are explicit, are notoriously easy to optimize for (overfit). A task like "build an app that actually works on screen," by contrast, is judged visually, making it harder to game. The fact that every output is public so readers can inspect it directly is also a transparency strength. hada.io commenters cited exactly this -- being judged by an app that actually runs -- as a positive.

3.2 Limits: one-shot, prompt-dependent, subjective

There are three structural limits at once. First, prompt dependency. It is a well-known LLM trait that small prompt wording changes can shift output quality significantly, yet this experiment used a single prompt per task. A slightly different prompt could plausibly reorder the results. Second, a single evaluator's subjectivity. Judgments like "better detail" or "purer style" are aesthetic calls that vary from person to person. Third, this was a one-shot completion task. Real development is rarely one-and-done -- it is an iterative back-and-forth of edits, which is exactly the critique some hada.io commenters raised. Iterative refinement ability is something this experiment never measured.

3.3 Where to stop generalizing: the "in this experiment" framing

So "Claude Fable 5 solved the Rubik's cube well in this experiment" and "Claude Fable 5 is generally strong at 3D graphics" are very different claims. The first is close to fact; the second is overreach built on a single experiment. Four frontend visual tasks are a narrow slice of coding work -- legacy refactors, large-scale backend design, and building out test coverage are entirely untouched by this experiment. That is why this piece keeps saying "in this experiment" instead of declaring any model generally superior.

4. What actually matters when choosing a coding agent

So how should this kind of comparison inform an actual model choice, rather than just being entertaining to read?

4.1 Fit for your workflow, over benchmark or build-off scores

Whether it's a benchmark or a build-off, it is ultimately a snapshot on someone else's tasks. Performance on your own codebase, primary language and framework, and team conventions is a separate question. Given that this experiment was confined to frontend visual apps, it offers only a rough reference -- not direct evidence -- for developers whose work is mostly backend, data pipelines, or infrastructure code.

4.2 The three-way trade-off this experiment reconfirms

Still, there is a structural fact this experiment reaffirms: quality, speed, and cost generally trade off against each other. Even within the GPT-5.6 family, Sol (higher quality, higher cost, slower) and Luna (lightweight, cheaper, faster) split cleanly, while Grok 4.5 aimed for a point that lowered cost without sacrificing much quality. In practice, it makes more sense to pick "the point that matches this task's risk and frequency" than "the single best model." Routing by task -- cheap and fast models for throwaway prototypes, higher-quality models for code headed to production -- is the practical conclusion closest to what this experiment actually demonstrates.

4.3 Build your own golden set

Following this experiment's own method is something you can do cheaply: build 10-20 of your own tasks whose outputs you can judge visually, and run 2-3 candidate models against them with identical prompts. If you log not just pass/fail but instruction-following accuracy, code readability, and cost/latency, you end up with a decision basis far closer to your actual workflow than any published leaderboard.

5. Where the newer models stand -- Grok 4.5 and Muse Spark, as far as verified

The two models making their debut in this experiment come from different backgrounds. Here is what is verifiable independent of the build-off itself.

5.1 Grok 4.5 -- xAI's first coding- and agent-focused model

xAI released Grok 4.5 on July 8, 2026, describing it as the company's first model designed from the ground up for coding and agentic work. xAI says it was trained in part on real Cursor developer session data, and published specs include roughly 80 tokens per second and pricing of $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens. In this experiment, Grok 4.5 hit 5/5 on both the raycaster and the calculator while being praised for value relative to price -- a result that lines up directionally with its published price/speed specs.

5.2 Muse Spark 1.1 -- Meta's entry into the coding agent market

Muse Spark 1.1 is a multimodal reasoning model Meta released on July 9, 2026, said to support tool use, multi-agent orchestration, and a 1-million-token context window, priced at $1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output tokens. The fact that Meta entered the coding agent market -- long dominated by OpenAI and Anthropic -- with a dedicated model is itself a notable industry development.

5.3 Where the two landed in this build-off

In this build-off, the authors described Grok 4.5 as "an alternative execution model you can pick without hesitation if cost matters," and Muse Spark as "more positive than expected, but not the top priority yet." Both are deliberately hedged phrases, and that matters. Both models were only days old at the time of the experiment. Separate numbers exist for other coding benchmarks (DeepSWE, Terminal Bench, SWE-Bench Pro, and so on), but long-term, real-world validation has not accumulated yet. There is no reason to dismiss either model just because it's new, nor to hype it up for the same reason.

6. What this means from a Korean developer's vantage point

This kind of comparison carries two implications worth noting from outside the US market. First, prices are almost always published in USD, so when exchange rates move significantly, the real cost gap in local currency can end up larger than it first appears. Teams calling these APIs at high frequency are better off re-checking pricing periodically rather than locking in a vendor based on a single snapshot. Second, the observation here that open-weight models (GLM-5.2, Qwen, DeepSeek, Kimi, and others) held up reasonably well on well-known tasks is a data point worth weighing for companies or startups that are reluctant to send proprietary code to an external API and are considering self-hosted coding agents. That said, the same experiment shows the gap widening on novel or complex tasks, so a staged rollout -- starting with lower-stakes areas like code review or prototyping -- is the more realistic path. It is also worth flagging that this experiment only covered English-language frontend tasks, so it offers no direct signal about a model's handling of Korean or other non-English languages, which still needs to be checked separately.

7. How to read comparisons like this critically

More build-offs like this will keep showing up every time a new model ships. A short checklist helps. First, check the evaluator count and number of repetitions. A single evaluator with few repeats should be read as an observation, not a verdict. Second, check the scope of the tasks. Don't apply results from frontend visual tasks to backend or infrastructure work. Third, check for conflicts of interest -- comparisons sponsored or produced by a specific model vendor deserve a heavier discount. Fourth, check the timing. AI model rankings can flip within weeks, so don't cite an old comparison's conclusion as if it were current. Content whose authors state their own limitations up front, as this one did, tends to be more trustworthy, not less -- favoring comparisons that don't hide their limits is itself a decent heuristic.

Conclusion: treat it as a list of questions, not a leaderboard

What this build-off leaves behind is not an answer to "who's number one," but three questions worth carrying forward: the same model's ranking shifts by task, the gap between frontier and open-weight models widens as tasks get harder, and when quality is close, cost and speed decide the winner. It's too early to render a final verdict on models as fresh as Grok 4.5 and Muse Spark 1.1 based on one experiment run days after their launch. The most reliable way to pick a model that fits your own workflow is still to run a handful of tasks you actually repeat against a shortlist of candidates and log the results, cost, and speed side by side. A build-off is a genuinely interesting and useful starting point -- it just isn't the conclusion by itself.
