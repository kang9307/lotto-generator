Before the number 7,704 means anything, you have to know who those 7,704 people were

A line like this scrolls past you. A survey of 7,704 people found that remote workers had the highest well-being and onsite workers the lowest. The sample is large, the paper is peer reviewed, a university press release is attached. It is tempting to jump straight to the conclusion that whoever is calling people back to the office has it wrong.

Three checks, taken in order, make the same result read quite differently: what was measured, who was measured, and how large the difference is. This article walks the actual paper in that order and separates what the data said from what the headline added. The short version is that this is a useful study that cannot carry the sentence remote work raises well-being.

What the study actually measured - well-being as four questions

Start by pinning down the word. In everyday speech, well-being bundles life satisfaction, mental health and physical condition into one blur. A paper cannot do that. The well-being scale here is four items on a five-point Likert scale, and they read as follows.

  • My organization cares about my health and well-being
  • I feel energized
  • My manager supports my decisions about my health and well-being
  • I am able to find balance in my life

Internal consistency was reported at a Cronbach alpha of 0.84, which is fine for a scale of this kind. The issue is not reliability but what the four items are made of. The first and third are plainly ratings of the employer and the manager. The fourth is closer to a working condition than to a mental state. Exactly one item, energy, asks about the person on their own.

So the well-being in this study is much nearer to how much I feel my employer accommodates me. Adopt that reading and the result becomes less surprising. Someone whose remote arrangement was approved is more likely to say the organization cares about their circumstances than someone who has to be physically present on a fixed schedule. Remote status is close to being a condition that pushes these particular answers upward.

None of this is a flaw. These are reasonable items for an engagement survey. The trouble starts when the scale word and the everyday word are treated as synonyms, and the headline ends up making a far broader claim than the paper. That is why reading the items comes before quoting the number.

Sample and design - who the 7,704 were, and what went uncontrolled

The sample size is impressive, but size and representativeness are different axes. Look first at who these people are.

  • It is a single organization: one large healthcare employer in the southwestern United States
  • Sixty-eight percent are women, average age 47, average tenure 12.8 years, and the racial composition is 30 percent White, 27 percent Asian, 22 percent Black, 18 percent Hispanic or Latino, 3 percent other
  • By arrangement: 1,869 remote, 2,099 hybrid, 3,736 onsite
  • Arrangement was not self-reported. It came from the human resources system, where remote means zero onsite days, hybrid typically two to three onsite days a week, and onsite means zero remote days

Average tenure of 12.8 years and average age of 47 sit a long way from the demographics of most technology firms. This is a workforce of long-serving mid-career people inside a hospital system. Transplanting the well-being gap found here onto a young startup or a factory floor is a move with no evidence behind it.

The design is time-lagged. An engagement survey run in 2023 was linked to turnover records collected a year later. What that buys is real: well-being is measured before the outcome it is meant to predict. What it does not buy is equally clear. Work arrangement is still an observed value at one moment, not something the researchers assigned.

And the paper says half of this article for me in its own limitations section. In a healthcare setting, arrangement is entangled with job function: remote roles cluster in administrative and analytic work, while onsite roles involve clinical or operational responsibilities. The authors state directly that unmeasured job characteristics such as emotional or physical demands may drive part of the well-being difference.

Sorting the controls: age, tenure and gender were examined, and gender showed no significant moderating effect. But salary, managerial status and job-specific demands were not controlled. For anyone who wants the isolated effect of working remotely, those three are precisely the items of interest.

Where correlation and causation part ways - how selection enters

This is the center of the whole question. Remote workers report higher well-being and remote work raises well-being are different sentences, and getting to the second one means ruling out at least three rival explanations.

One: selection

In any world where arrangement is chosen, who ends up remote bleeds into the result. Some people needed it because of childcare or eldercare. Some moved because their commute was unusually punishing. Some already had good standing with the organization and therefore leverage. If those people already differed in well-being before going remote, the observed gap was produced not by remote work but by the conditions that made remote work obtainable.

You can object that arrangement here was assigned by the employer, not chosen. But that assignment follows job function. Individual-level selection has simply been converted into job-level selection. It changed shape rather than disappearing.

Two: differences in job mix

Even inside one organization, the jobs that can be done remotely and the jobs that cannot are different jobs. In a hospital, the remote-capable seats handle documents and data; the others handle people bodily. Night shifts, infection risk, emergencies and physical strain are not evenly distributed between the two. What looks like a comparison of workplaces is partly a comparison of occupations.

Three: pay and standing

Here is where the uncontrolled salary and managerial variables bite. If income or seniority distributions differ between the groups, some share of the well-being gap is a gap in money and discretion. This design cannot exclude that.

There is direct evidence that selection is not a theoretical worry but a large, measurable force. A Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report analyzed call centers at a Fortune 500 firm. Before the pandemic, already-remote workers answered 12 percent fewer calls per hour than onsite workers. When the offices closed and formerly onsite staff went remote, their productivity fell 4 percent relative to the already-remote group. In other words, only a third of the original gap came from remote work itself; the remaining eight percentage points came from who had selected into remote work.

Selection was twice the size of the treatment effect. That single result is the reason the first question to ask of any observational study of work arrangement is whether selection has been removed.

Direction settled, the next question is magnitude. Collecting the reported figures into one table:

The table shows several things the headline does not.

First, all three groups score high. Even the lowest, onsite at 3.89, sits well above the scale midpoint of 3 and lands in agreement territory. Lowest is a relative rank, not a verdict on anyone's state. Copying the ranking alone manufactures a conclusion nobody found: that onsite workers are unhappy.

Second, the remote-hybrid gap is 0.10 points. Its reported p value is 0.0003, which is statistically significant. Yet 0.10 on a five-point scale is effectively the same place. With 7,704 respondents, differences small enough to be practically irrelevant become significant. Significance answers whether a difference exists, not whether it matters.

Third, computing explained variance makes the size concrete. The paper reports the analysis of variance as F(2, 7648) = 117.9. Converting that into eta squared gives 2 x 117.9 / (2 x 117.9 + 7648), or about 0.03. Work arrangement accounts for roughly three percent of the variance in well-being scores. The other ninety-seven percent is shared out among managers, job content, individual differences and the weather of a given week.

How to read three percent. Small is not the same as meaningless. A policy lever that shifts three percent across thousands of employees is worth an organization's attention. But it also means that for an individual trying to improve their own well-being, location is one variable among many rather than the dominant one. The same number carries different weight at the organizational and personal levels.

The fourth point is the one most often misquoted. The turnover difference was not statistically significant. Seven point eight, 8.3 and 8.8 percent line up in a plausible direction but did not clear the threshold. This study therefore did not show directly that remote workers quit less.

What was significant is a different relationship: people with higher well-being scores were less likely to have left a year later. So the paper's claim runs along a two-step path, from arrangement to well-being and from well-being to retention. That structure is called mediation, and despite the name, mediation analysis does not prove causation. It assumes a causal ordering and checks whether the data contradict it. The first step rests on observational data, so the chain is only as strong as that first link.

How the not isolated finding was measured

The most widely shared line from this study is that remote workers were not isolated - that they used teamwork and support language slightly more than others. Here is how that was measured.

  • The engagement survey contained one open-ended item: what three to five words describe our organization's culture
  • Responses were processed with the LIWC-22 text tool against a researcher-created dictionary of 88 words such as inclusion, camaraderie, belonging, diversity and together
  • The result was coded dichotomously: one if any such word appeared, zero if none did
  • Mention rates were 0.61 remote, 0.58 hybrid, 0.55 onsite, and a chi-squared test found the association significant

Two things snag here. First, words describing organizational culture are not the same thing as personal loneliness. Someone who calls the culture inclusive and someone who has a colleague to ask when they get stuck may or may not be the same person.

Second, flattening to present-or-absent erases degree. A respondent with one qualifying word out of three and a respondent with five out of five both become a one. The paper's limitations section concedes the point: keyword presence cannot capture the depth or quality of workplace relationships, and misses emotional closeness, trust and interaction frequency.

Stated precisely, the finding is that remote workers were about six percentage points more likely to use a connection-related word when describing company culture. That is some distance from remote workers are not lonely. Still, the direction matters: this dataset does not support the assumption that remote automatically means isolated.

Evidence pointing the other way, and how to grade it

Collecting only results that agree with you is cheerleading, not evidence. Studies reporting the costs of remote work belong on the same table.

  • Collaboration networks silo. A study of 61,182 US Microsoft employees analyzed emails, calendars, instant messages and calls across the first half of 2020. After the firm-wide shift to remote work, collaboration networks became more static and more siloed, and the share of collaboration time spent across groups fell by roughly 25 percent of the pre-pandemic level. Communication within teams rose while the bridges between them thinned
  • Promotion and quality. The same call center research found that remote work degraded call quality, particularly for less experienced workers, and lowered promotion rates
  • The value of sitting nearby. A study tracking software engineers at a Fortune 500 firm from 2019 to 2024 estimated that sitting near teammates increases feedback on code by 18.3 percent. With offices open, co-located teams received 23.9 percent more comments, 1.92 more per program, than teams split across buildings. And these gains concentrated in less-tenured and younger engineers at roughly twice the average

None of this contradicts the well-being study. They measured different things. One looked at how people feel; the others looked at output and career trajectory. Remote work can lift the felt side while trimming the learning side, and both can happen inside the same person at once.

When comparing evidence, the thing to inspect is design rather than conclusion. On one topic, different designs license different sentences.

When remote helps and when it costs

Put the evidence together and the question stops being remote or office and becomes under which conditions does each win. Three axes recur across the studies above.

AxisFavors remoteWorks against remote
Job characteristicsOutput lands in documents and code, and uninterrupted focus produces the resultTacit judgment has to be learned by watching, or the result is on-site responsiveness
Career stageStandards are already internalized and the work can be self-directedEarly stage, where feedback density sets the rate of growth
Home environmentWork and life can be physically separated, and the commute was longNo dedicated workspace, and caregiving overlaps with work in the same room

Of the three, career stage is the most consistently underrated. The engineering study found gains twice the average for less-tenured staff, and the call center study found the quality drop concentrated among inexperienced workers. Both point the same way. For a newcomer the office is not a room; it is bandwidth for observational learning.

The home environment resists averages because the variance is enormous. Two people on the same remote policy receive different treatments if one has a door to close and the other works at a kitchen table. Talking about arrangements only in terms of average effects throws that variance away entirely.

Why hybrid keeps coming up, and how strong that evidence is

Hybrid is not discussed constantly because it is a tidy compromise. It is discussed because that is where the randomized evidence happens to sit.

The clearest case is a 2024 trial published in Nature. At a Chinese travel technology company, 1,612 employees were randomly assigned for six months. One group worked a hybrid schedule including two days from home; the other came in five days a week. The results:

  • Quit rates fell by about a third. The drop was largest among non-managers, women and employees with long commutes
  • Job satisfaction improved
  • Performance ratings over the following two years were unaffected, confirmed with equivalence testing

This result has a different status from the well-being study. Because assignment was random, selection bias was removed at the design stage. People who wanted remote work could not pile into the remote group. Causal language is legitimate here.

What the trial verified is nonetheless narrow. It tested two days from home, not full remote; one company; six months; and performance measured by that firm's own ratings. Evidence of comparable grade for fully remote work is still uncommon. A good part of why hybrid gets recommended so often is not that it is better but that it is the one place where the evidence is firm.

Translated into working language: expecting a two-day-from-home policy to improve retention is an expectation backed by an experiment. Expecting a fully remote policy to improve well-being is an inference from observational data. Stating both with the same confidence is the error.

The Korean context - commuting, housing, uptake

This study comes from one American hospital system. At least three conditions change when it is carried to South Korea, and all three are documented in official statistics.

Commuting - makes the remote benefit larger

One mechanism the authors offer for the well-being gap is reduced commuting stress. On this axis Korea is set up for a bigger effect. According to an analysis of commuter movement for 2024 by the national statistics agency, which linked census information with pseudonymized location data from the three mobile carriers, the average round-trip commute in June 2024 was 73.9 minutes over 17.3 kilometers, and the capital region was longest at 82.0 minutes.

Eighty-two minutes a day is 6.8 hours a week across five days. A change that removes that much travel cannot have the same effect size as the same policy in a place where commutes are short. It is more natural to read much of the remote benefit as coming from the avoided commute rather than from remoteness itself, and if so, Korea sits on the high-benefit side.

Housing - makes the remote benefit smaller

A condition pushes the other way too. The quality of working from home depends heavily on whether a workspace fits in the home. The national indicator system, drawing on the housing survey run by the transport ministry, puts floor area per person at 36.0 square meters as of 2024, up from 26.2 in 2006 and 31.7 in 2012. The same indicator page compares that with 67.4 square meters in the United States and 44.5 in the United Kingdom for 2023.

Less floor area means less room to separate work space from living space. Recall that the fourth item on the well-being scale asks about finding balance in life, and this condition pushes that exact answer in the opposite direction. Part of what is gained on the commute is returned on the housing side.

Uptake - the sample gets narrower

Third is how many people actually work from home. In the supplementary survey on employment type within the economically active population survey, 15.0 percent of wage workers used some flexible work arrangement as of August 2024 - 20.1 percent among regular employees and 6.9 percent among non-regular employees. Within that group, and allowing multiple answers, staggered hours accounted for 35.0 percent, flexible-hours systems 29.5 percent, selective working hours 25.4 percent, and working from home or remotely 15.9 percent.

Read that carefully. The 15.9 percent is a share among flexible-work users, not among all wage workers, and multiple answers were allowed. Multiplying the two gives something in the low single digits per hundred wage workers, but the multiple-response structure makes that conversion imprecise. Either way the conclusion holds: working from home is a minority condition in Korea.

Which brings back the earlier part of this article. The narrower the remote population, the more selected the people inside it are: those with bargaining power, document-centered roles, or a reason for the organization to accommodate them. The selection bias that was already hard to remove in the American sample is likely to operate more strongly in Korean data. If a domestic survey shows high satisfaction among people working from home, the safer reading is that it reflects the conditions of people who managed to obtain it before it reflects any effect of remoteness.

Variables an individual can actually control

Few people set their own work arrangement. Yet pulling this study's scale apart makes the controllable items easier to see, precisely because three of the four well-being items concern the organization, the manager and boundary setting.

  • The relationship with your manager. One of the four items asks about managerial support directly. That answer varies enormously between people in identical locations. The fact that this variance dwarfs the arrangement variance is the other face of the three percent figure
  • Physical boundary design. Even in a small home, separating the place you work from the place you rest is possible. The balance item is asking less about hours than about whether a separation exists at all
  • Sourcing your own feedback and visibility. If the comments that used to arrive from the next desk stop coming, do not leave the gap empty. Regular review requests and the habit of briefly sharing where you are stuck do not appear automatically when you go remote. The finding on lower promotion rates is best read not as avoid remote work but as treat making your output visible as a separate task. The weight of this item is highest early in a career
  • Deciding where the reclaimed commute goes. If the hours saved on travel are simply absorbed into working hours, the commuting benefit disappears. Decide in advance what fills them

Nothing on this list requires changing your work arrangement, and that is the point. Location explained three percent of the variance in well-being; the remaining ninety-seven percent contains the things an individual can touch.

What I could not confirm

Items this article could not settle:

  • How the analytic sample was narrowed. The survey went to 23,316 employees and 20,856 responded, yet the analyzed sample is 7,704. What criteria cut it to roughly a third is not something I could confirm. Excluding records without an arrangement classification or without an open-ended response is the obvious guess, but it remains a guess
  • Two figures absent from the paper. Standard deviations for the well-being score were not given, only confidence intervals, which is why effect size appears here as eta squared rather than Cohen's d. The full 88-word connection dictionary is also unpublished apart from a few examples, so the effect of its composition cannot be assessed
  • Salary and seniority distributions across groups. The paper states that these were uncontrolled but does not report how far apart they actually are
  • Korean flexible-work detail for August 2025. The breakdown quoted here is from the August 2024 survey. I could not open the type-level table from the later release, so the older reference period is stated as such

Conclusion - six questions to ask a headline like this

This is a well-executed observational study: large sample, lagged outcome, limitations documented in detail by its own authors. The damage occurs not in the paper but in the stretch between the paper and the headline. That is where conditions get dropped, a scale word turns into an everyday word, and a null result quietly disappears.

Next time you meet a sentence like this one, six checks in order will do.

  1. What was measured? Read the actual items. Well-being, engagement and satisfaction are empty words until you see the questions
  2. Who was measured? Country, industry, occupation, age, tenure. Sample size is not representativeness
  3. Assigned or observed? If researchers assigned the condition, causal language is available. If people ended up in it, selection is mixed in
  4. How large is the difference? Look at the mean gap and the explained variance, not the p value. Large samples make small differences significant
  5. What was not significant? The unreported side often carries the more important information
  6. What points the other way? Find studies on the same topic that measured something else. Apparent contradictions usually come from different measurements

Run this study through those six and the surviving sentence is: at one large healthcare employer, people in roles assigned to remote work rated their employer as more accommodating, and those who rated it higher were less likely to have left a year later. That is enough to support the claim that letting people work remotely when they want to is not a bad decision. It is not enough to support the claim that remote work creates well-being. Knowing the size of that gap while you quote the study, rather than not knowing it, is what reading headlines like this one actually consists of.
