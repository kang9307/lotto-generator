Ghost Font - The Font Humans Can Read but AI Cannot, Explained and Limited

A motion-based anti-AI experiment that hides messages in moving dots - verifying the source to explain how it works and where it falls short

Jul 13, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: In an Age Where AI Reads Everything, an Idea for Letters Only Humans Can Read

Over the past few years the web has been pulled in two opposite directions at once. On one side, search engines and screen readers have pushed publishers to make content more readable. On the other, the crawlers that feed large language models have hoovered up that same readable content without consent, fueling a growing backlash from creators. In July 2026, Ghost Font surfaced on GeekNews (news.hada.io) as an experiment that runs straight into this tension. It comes from Eric of Mixfont, a one-person project based in San Francisco, and the pitch is simple: a font a human can read but an AI struggles to.

The problem is that the picture most people imagine from that pitch - a static image with distorted letter shapes and pixel noise - is not actually what sits on the original page. Let me lay out what the source material actually shows first, then place the idea within its real limits.

1. What Ghost Font Actually Is - Checking the Source Directly

1.1 Not a Static Font - Motion Encoding

Ghost Font is not the distorted-glyph font most people picture. The actual implementation is a video with dots scattered across the frame that shift position slightly over time, and only through that motion does a letter shape become visible to a human eye. Pause the video or take a screenshot, and the dots collapse into what reads as background noise. The message is not encoded in the pixel layout of a single frame - it is encoded along the time axis, in motion spread across many frames. This is where it decisively parts ways with ZXX, the 2013 anti-OCR font that overlaid dots, X marks, and false letters onto static images. ZXX was a spatial, static distortion; Ghost Font is a temporal one.

On top of that, each video carries a decoy message. A plausible-looking fake message sits alongside the real one, so even when an AI manages to extract something, it can be misled into treating the decoy as genuine.

1.2 Real Tests - Where It Worked and Where It Broke

The creator states these videos were actually run against recent models in the GPT and Claude families. The headline result is one model that spent 19 minutes analyzing a video and hallucinated a message that did not exist. But that success is unlikely to hold for long. Looking at the hada discussion and related community reaction, there are reports of newer-generation models correctly decoding the message, alongside pointers that averaging frames, analyzing optical flow, or exploiting video-compression artifacts can bypass the scheme entirely. The creator themselves acknowledges that an agent with a local code execution environment can still analyze the motion. In other words, this project is not a finished shield - it is closer to a proof of concept that pokes at one soft spot in today's AI vision models.

2. Why This Idea Emerged - The Lineage of AI-Scraping Resistance

2.1 From ZXX to Ghost Font, a 13-Year Gap

Ghost Font openly cites 2013 designer Sang Mun's ZXX as its inspiration. ZXX was a set of four typefaces built by a former NSA contractor turned designer, meant to disrupt OCR out of concern about mass surveillance. What is telling is that even its creator admitted the project carried more symbolic weight - raising awareness about surveillance - than actual defensive effect, and today's LLMs read ZXX text with a single prompt. The visual camouflage that was cutting-edge 13 years ago is effectively neutralized now. Ghost Font's choice of motion over static distortion reads as a direct response to that precedent: a static disguise dies the moment AI vision catches up to it.

2.2 2026: A Closing Web and a Wave of Technical Pushback

The backdrop to this idea is the rapid "closing of the web" underway in 2026. CDN operators like Cloudflare now block AI crawlers by default or charge them through pay-per-crawl arrangements, and millions of sites use robots.txt to explicitly refuse AI training collection. The EU AI Act has begun requiring general-purpose AI providers to disclose training-data sources and honor machine-readable copyright opt-outs. But robots.txt remains a gentleman's agreement, not a technical barrier that forces compliance, and there have already been repeated cases where policy signals alone failed to guarantee it. Ghost Font is one of several experiments trying to fill that gap - the space where policy cannot force compliance - with technology instead.

3. Taking the Mechanism Apart - Human Perceptual Tolerance vs. Frame-by-Frame Machine Analysis

3.1 Why Humans Aren't Fooled and Machines Are

Human vision does not perceive a stream of isolated stills - it perceives flow. When a handful of dots shift position slightly at every moment, the brain automatically integrates that into a single trajectory and shape rather than a list of discrete points. Most AI video-understanding models, by contrast, tend to sample video frame by frame and treat it as a sequence of still-image analyses. Since any individual frame carries nothing but noise, the message stays invisible without a separate step that stitches the frames together and reconstructs the trajectory across time. Ghost Font targets exactly this processing gap - exploiting the fact that temporal integration, a default for human perception, is not yet a default in most current AI pipelines.

3.2 Where It Resembles and Diverges From Adversarial Perturbation

This approach sits in the same lineage as adversarial perturbation, a technique long studied in machine learning. Adversarial perturbation adds a small, nearly imperceptible distortion to an image to make a classifier misfire - a double-edged tool used both to design CAPTCHAs that humans can read and machines cannot, and, in the reverse direction, to automatically break CAPTCHAs. Ghost Font can be read as an extension of that lineage in a broad sense, but the mechanism differs. Classic adversarial perturbation optimizes noise in a spatial dimension - pixel layout - while Ghost Font scatters information across a temporal dimension. The goal is identical: exploit the gap between human perceptual tolerance and a machine's recognition weaknesses. But since the axis of disruption differs, lumping this project in with simple noise fonts is not quite accurate.

4. Effectiveness and Limits - The Illusion of a Perfect Defense

4.1 A Battle That May Already Be Lost - The Gap Keeps Closing

Multimodal vision models are advancing quickly not just on still images but on video understanding as well. Layer the precedent of ZXX's effective defeat after 13 years alongside community reports that some newer models already cracked Ghost Font messages shortly after release, and it seems safer to assume a short shelf life for any claim that "this model can't read it right now." Even the creator admits that truly hiding a message requires encryption in the end. Visual camouflage does not hide a message so much as briefly raise the difficulty of reading it - and AI keeps clearing that bar more and more cheaply.

4.2 Who Pays the Price - Accessibility, SEO, and Copy-Paste

A more fundamental problem is that the cost of using this as a real content-protection tool is simply too high. Legibility is low enough that even the creator admits it is not easy for humans to read either, and message length is capped at a few dozen characters, so it is not something you could apply to actual body content. Text cannot be selected or copied, screen-reader users relying on assistive technology are shut out entirely, and search engines cannot index meaning hidden in the motion of dots - which, from an SEO standpoint, is functionally the same as erasing your own content. Because of these traits, Ghost Font is less a general-purpose shield for website content than a communication channel for short messages between humans, or a benchmark for measuring AI vision capability.

5. Where Ghost Font Sits in the Copyright and Data-Protection Conversation

5.1 Technical Resistance and Legal-Policy Resistance Are Different Layers

Responses to AI scraping split roughly into two layers. One is technical resistance - robots.txt, access control, fee-charging pay-per-crawl schemes, and visual camouflage like Ghost Font. The other is legal-policy resistance - the EU AI Act's opt-out obligations, text-and-data-mining exceptions under copyright law, and licensing negotiations or litigation. Technical resistance can be deployed immediately but cannot escape the fate of an arms race; legal-policy resistance takes longer to establish but is comparatively stable once in place. Ghost Font clearly belongs to the former, and given that it leans toward raising awareness rather than delivering practical protection, its own framing as ZXX's spiritual successor is an accurate one.

5.2 The Arms-Race Pattern Glaze and Nightshade Already Showed

The image domain already demonstrated this pattern first, through Glaze and Nightshade from a University of Chicago research team. Glaze subtly shifts a painting's style signature so an AI learns the wrong style, while Nightshade goes further and poisons the image-text association itself - training a model, say, to learn a dog as a cat. The development team self-reported protection rates in the 90-percent range, but independent research was far less generous: a tool called LightShed detected Nightshade-protected images with very high accuracy and effectively stripped the protection away. The reasonable read on both tools as of 2026 is not "an unbreakable shield" but "a defensive line that gains meaning as adoption spreads" - a deterrent whose cost to attack rises with the number of people who adopt it. Ghost Font deserves the same framing. Fooling one particular model at one particular point in time is not evidence that the defense is permanent.

6. Comparing Other Defenses - Text-as-Image, Cloaking, and Where Ghost Font Sits Between Them

Several attempts to protect content already exist. Rendering text entirely as an image is an old technique, but today's vision models often read text inside images more accurately than a human would, so its defensive value has largely evaporated - while accessibility and SEO are still sacrificed. Cloaking - serving different content once a visitor is identified as a bot - has long been a banned black-hat technique in the SEO industry, punished with ranking penalties when detected. Lined up together, the positioning becomes clear. Text-as-image shows the same screen to humans and machines but turns text into pixels to make extraction harder - a static approach. Cloaking is outright deceptive, showing humans and machines different content entirely. Ghost Font shows the same video to both but exploits a machine's weakness in temporal processing - a dynamic approach. All three share the same limitation: their effectiveness erodes as AI vision keeps improving.

7. The Creator's Trade-Off - Is It Actually Worth Using

The short answer is that applying a Ghost Font-style technique to a real website or blog's body content is hard to recommend. The costs - reduced accessibility, no copy-paste, guaranteed SEO damage - are certain and immediate, while the AI-resistance benefit you get in exchange is temporary and keeps shrinking. For a creator who genuinely wants to protect content, a more realistic combination is a layered defense: explicit opt-out signals through robots.txt and meta tags, bot detection and pay-per-crawl services like Cloudflare's, and copyright registration paired with licensing negotiation. Ghost Font has a real place, just a different one - a short message meant for humans only, a fun Easter egg, and above all, a benchmark for measuring how far AI vision has actually come. The project's own plan to open-source its code reads as a signal that it identifies more as a research and measurement tool than as a defensive one.

Conclusion

Once you check the source material for Ghost Font, the picture most people assume from the name alone - static, distorted letters - turns out to be wrong. What it actually is turns out to be far stranger and more experimental: a message hidden in the motion of dots on video, with a decoy thrown in to confuse AI further. It is an interesting proof of concept and a spiritual successor to ZXX, but by its own creator's admission it is nowhere near a perfect defense, it is hard even for humans to read, and search engines and accessibility tools cannot read it at all. In the fight against AI scraping, a technical trick like this can create meaningful friction, but it cannot be the solution on its own. As of July 2026, the center of gravity in this fight still sits with policy instruments - robots.txt, licensing, legislation - more than with visual camouflage, and experiments like Ghost Font are more valuable for raising awareness in that gap than for closing it.
