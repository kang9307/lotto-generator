Agent-Built 3D Worlds - What the Two Hours and Thirty-Three Dollars Do Not Tell You

The gap between wall-clock time and agent time, the boundary of procedural generation, and what verification inside the pipeline actually did

2026-09-05 Tech & IT

There is an open repository holding a real city block rebuilt as a 3D world you can walk through in a browser, and the work was not done by people at a keyboard but by a swarm of agents. Three numbers travel with the announcement. About two hours, about eight million tokens, about thirty-three dollars.

Those three numbers are the subject here. How convincing the demo looks is answered by watching the video, and nothing in that answer transfers to anyone else's work. What does transfer is what those numbers are a measure of, what they include and what they leave out. Establishing that requires opening the repository rather than repeating the headline.

The trigger - three numbers that travel together

In early September 2026 an open repository made the rounds on a developer community. A swarm of agents had researched a real place, modelled it, turned it into a 3D world that runs in a browser, and verified the result. Alongside that description sat three figures: eight million tokens, two hours, thirty-three dollars.

Combinations like that spread easily. They are short, specific and surprising. What remains after they spread, however, is close to zero information. Two hours of what? Is the thirty-three dollars per deliverable or for the whole project? Do the eight million tokens include attempts that failed and were thrown away? With those three questions unanswered, the numbers cannot be used to estimate anything.

The same measurement gap has already produced a case pointing the other way. An autonomous agent run that ended in a bill far larger than anyone expected showed exactly where budget measurement needs to be cut, and this repository circulates as the number at the opposite extreme. Either way the lesson holds: a single figure lifted out of its conditions stops being useful.

The first thing that stands out is that the repository is not yet three days old. Those 426 stars accumulated within those three days, which makes them an indicator of reach rather than an indicator of maturity. Twenty forks, two open issues and no watchers describes a project that has not yet attracted outside contribution.

The size is worth a second look too. It runs to roughly 165MB, yet the 3D modules that constitute one world total 5.1MB, and the other world ships zero bytes of binary asset. Almost all of the weight is walkthrough video and preview animation. In other words, the repository is heavy because of the footage of the world, not because of the world.

Those numbers are not written in the repository

This is the crux. The top-level README, both per-world READMEs, a 27,022-byte brief file, two final QA reports and even the README of a comparison build produced with a different vendor's model all share one property: none of them state a token count, a dollar amount or an elapsed time.

It is not that the project is thin on documentation. The Kyoto world alone ships an architecture document of roughly 199KB, a street document of roughly 158KB, a method document of roughly 123KB and a geography document of roughly 43KB. A project that records how many elevation queries were issued and how many steps a stone stairway has did not record what it cost.

So where do the three numbers come from? They circulate through the announcement post and the secondary coverage that picked it up. It is entirely plausible that the original source is the author. But they are not values confirmed by the repository's own documents, and that distinction is not a small one. A number written into a repository can be opened and disputed by anyone; a number circulating outside the documents has nothing to be checked against.

  • Confirmed — what was built, which data it was grounded in, how it was verified, and where it fell short
  • Not confirmed — how much it cost, how long it took, and how many times it had to be re-run
  • Therefore, the qualifier to attach when quoting — a figure from the announcement, absent from the repository documents

The boundary the commit history puts on two hours

The commit history, however, is public, and on its own it settles what the two hours cannot possibly be two hours of. Laying out the dates of all twelve commits gives this.

  • 2 September — initial commit, QA automation scripts (capture, compare, walkthrough, performance), the walkthrough rendered at 1920×1080, the brief file added and its links repaired. That is one world, San Francisco
  • 3 September — the Higashiyama world in Kyoto added, and the top-level README rewritten twice
  • 5 September — a comparison build made with a different model, plus the side-by-side footage

Three separate production events across three days. The stated two hours therefore cannot refer to the repository as a whole under any reading. Its ceiling is one world, and even then the documents do not say which one. Whether documentation, video capture and two README rewrites fall inside or outside that window is equally unstated.

This kind of check costs one page load, and it is precisely the step that gets skipped when a headline figure is passed along. Asking first whether public metadata already bounds the claim is where the effort pays.

Wall-clock time and agent time are not the same number

Dividing the three figures into one another surfaces something else. The arithmetic below is nothing more than two circulating numbers divided by each other, and is not itself a verified value.

Circulating       8M tokens · ~2 hours · ~$33

  33 ÷ 8            = about $4.1 per million tokens
                      ← a blend of input, output and cache hits

  8,000,000 ÷ 7,200 = about 1,111 tokens per second
                      ← a rate a single sequential stream cannot reach

  Parallel research agents named in the brief    8
  Independent reviewer agents in the QA report   9
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
  2 hours of wall-clock  ≠  2 hours of agent work

Roughly eleven hundred tokens per second is not something one sequential generation stream produces. Which means the two hours is elapsed wall-clock time, and several agents ran concurrently inside it. The brief itself assigns eight parallel agents to the reconnaissance stage, and nine independent reviewers attach at the QA stage. Summed across agents, the total work time is far larger than the wall-clock figure.

The practical split is clean. Wall-clock time answers when the result lands on my desk. Summed agent time answers what I will be billed. The structural reason multi-agent setups burn tokens at a multiple is laid out in the piece using a newsroom as the analogy, and the overhead that attaches as agent count grows is covered in the piece on sizing subagent counts.

What changes when you carry thirty-three dollars into your own work

Five things have to be established before a cost figure can be reused. For this repository, not one of the five is fixed by the documents.

Five places the meaning splits

  • The unit. Per deliverable or per project? The commit history suggests one world, but nothing says so explicitly
  • Retries. Is this the cost of the one run that passed, or of every attempt including the discarded ones? The QA report records 147 comparison sheets across 14 runs, so the cycle certainly turned more than once
  • Scope. Research, code generation, verification, documentation, video rendering — where does the accounting stop?
  • Rate composition. The $4.1 per million tokens is a blended quotient mixing input, output and cache hits. Without the ratio it cannot be converted against any other job's price sheet
  • Human time. Someone wrote that 27KB brief. That authoring time, and any mid-run intervention, is not inside the thirty-three dollars

What the figure is still good for

None of which makes the number worthless. It is usable as an order-of-magnitude anchor. Being in the tens of dollars means that, set against what the same output would cost in human labour, the API spend is close to a rounding error. That moves the decision away from price and onto a different question entirely: did the output clear the bar? And this repository shipped an answer to that question as well, which the later sections get to.

Splitting token consumption apart by session to see where it actually went is the approach described in the piece on decomposing tokens from execution logs. This repository ships no such log.

The pipeline - reconnaissance, assets, runtime, verification

The production sequence the repository states for itself has four stages, and it claims every stage lives in the repository and can be re-run.

  • Reconnaissance — parallel research agents pull map geometry, elevation, transit, street specifications and a storefront census. The condition attached is that each fact carries its source and a confidence level
  • Asset generation — scripts emit the kit that world needs: façade modules, street furniture, vehicles, vegetation, fixtures
  • Runtime — a plain Three.js application reads JSON specifications and assembles terrain, streets, façades, props, crowds and traffic
  • Verification — browser automation drives the real application, captures fixed viewpoints and diffs them against photographs, while separate reviewer agents file reports

The San Francisco brief assigns eight parallel research and reconstruction agents to that first stage, with dedicated coverage for each side of the plaza, for the storefront census and for the hero stores. The brief also constrains the 3D authoring tool to offline asset generation only, keeping it out of the runtime entirely.

What stands out most is that the brief fixed the pass mark as a number before any work began. No critical category may remain below eight out of ten, with geographic accuracy and storefront verification targeting nine. Because those numbers predate the work, the project ends up with a structure in which a shortfall can be called a shortfall.

Where generated as code ends and asset production begins

The claim the repository presses hardest is that it uses no game engine, no proprietary 3D tiles and no downloaded meshes. Every building, storefront, sign, tree and traffic light is said to be generated by code that lives in the repository.

The two worlds hold to that principle to different degrees, though, and the difference maps the boundary of procedural generation precisely.

The left column is the parts-stamped-in-advance approach. Window units, cornices, storefronts, marquees, streetlights, signals, benches, hydrants, vehicles, palms, retail fixtures, character statues and even pedestrian body parts come out as 206 modules packed into 5.1MB — roughly 25KB apiece. Nobody modelled them by hand, but the output persists as files.

The right column is the approach that leaves no file at all. The texture of every sign, noren curtain, lantern, roof tile, lattice screen and paving stone is painted onto a canvas at run time. Which is how the whole thing packages into a single HTML page.

What procedural generation makes cheap, and what it does not

Contrasting the two exposes the character of the technique. Repetition with a rule becomes nearly free; individuality without a rule stays expensive.

  • Cheap 1 — repeating structure. Four hundred and fifty-three buildings are covered by 75 authored façade specifications. That is roughly one specification per six buildings, and the 11,950 openings are what those rules unfold into
  • Cheap 2 — revision. In the repository's own framing, asking for deeper shopfront recesses is a diff rather than a re-roll. Only the requested part changes, and the change survives into every subsequent render
  • Cheap 3 — checkability. A wrong number becomes a failing test rather than a matter of taste, because the value sits in code where something can be compared against it
  • Not cheap 1 — singular subjects. This is exactly where the unresolved items in the QA report sit. One department store's cladding and dome remain approximations, and another building is missing its proper height and roof detailing
  • Not cheap 2 — repeating texture. The report states outright that procedural textures repeat visibly at one to three metres. That is the classic price of making things by rule
  • Not cheap 3 — particular facts. A storefront roster cannot be generated from a rule. It has to be researched and filled in, which is why research occupies the first slot in the pipeline

The 3D fundamentals are not re-explained here. Standing database contents up as a browser 3D scene is covered in the piece on another project that built a city with the same library.

Verification living inside the pipeline

This is the part of the repository actually worth carrying away. Verification is not a post-hoc inspection; it is a stage of the pipeline.

[Recon]     maps · elevation · street specs · storefront census
                → every fact carries a source and a confidence level
   ↓
[Assets]    scripts stamp out the modules
   ↓
[Runtime]   JSON specs → scene assembly
   ↓
[Verify]    browser automation drives the real application
            34 viewpoints pinned by lat · lon · heading · FOV
            diffed against photographs from the same spots
            9 independent reviewer agents each file a report
   ↓
[Fix]       the defect list becomes the next cycle's input
            ※ builders never grade their own work

Pulling the pieces apart: the viewpoints are 34 positions pinned by latitude, longitude, heading and field of view, and 28 of them have freely licensed photographs secured as the comparison target. The comparisons accumulated to 147 sheets across 14 runs. The walkthrough check is recorded as 18 of 18 passing.

The reviewers split by role: architect, geographer, technical artist, interaction. And the repository states one rule explicitly. Builders never grade their own work. Standing up separate reviewer agents is not itself novel; shipping their findings unredacted is much less common.

Fixing the pass mark as a number before starting and then judging completion against it belongs to the same family as building the evaluation first and attaching development afterwards. How the same instinct settled into code review is covered in the piece on agents reviewing code.

This table is the most important thing in the piece, for one reason: the shortfall was left standing as a shortfall. Instead of nudging scores up or dropping categories, the report shipped them and named the fix for each.

The remaining defects are specific too. Of 88 named tenants from the census, 21 are not yet placed. Pedestrians cannot enter stores, no queue forms outside the game store, and buses do not stop at shelters. One department store's window geometry is doubled, and using OpenStreetMap footprints directly leaves the sidewalks narrower than they are in reality.

The difference between having that list and not having it is large. Without it, judgement rests on the video, and videos are shot from the angles that came out well. With it, what the world can and cannot be used for resolves immediately. It is usable for checking pedestrian circulation; it is not yet usable for retail site assessment.

The grounding is cultural-property records and restoration surveys, plus roughly 200 independent point queries against a one-metre airborne-LiDAR bare-earth elevation model. Street widths were taken by casting perpendicular rays to building polygons every eight metres.

What deserves attention is not the corrected values themselves but why the correction happened at all. Standing geometry up as code forces a single number to be chosen, and when sources disagree the disagreement cannot be waved past. The obligation to build is what forced the verification. Writing 46m in a sentence costs nothing; erecting a pagoda at 46m next to its neighbours makes the error visible.

That said, the repository's figures were not independently confirmed here. What was confirmed is that the repository states those values and names the class of data it grounded them in.

Defects that leave no log and show only on screen

One line in the Kyoto documentation summarises the character of the project: nothing in this project is judged by reading its source.

The list of bugs that actually surfaced makes the claim believable. The defects caught during development produced no errors and no log lines, yet were immediately obvious in the rendered output.

  • Geometry errors in the roof ridge caps
  • Façades attached with a whole-element rotation applied
  • Precision loss in the random number sequence driving texture generation
  • Interpolation artefacts introduced by the terrain solver
  • Discontinuities where the height field failed to join

Every one of these is the kind of fault that passes type checking and unit tests. Values stay inside their valid ranges and nothing throws. That is why verification was built on screenshot comparison, and why browser automation ended up as a pipeline stage rather than an optional extra.

The same attitude shows in performance measurement. The documentation records that wall-clock frame time in a headless browser drifts twenty to thirty per cent between runs, and adopts instead the property that draw call counts reproduce exactly. Separate the values that wobble from the values that do not, and base regression judgement on the ones that do not. Where to catch anomalies in agent output at all is the same family of problem as tracking the mistakes production agents make.

How to read a model comparison hosted in the same repository

The repository also carries a comparison build in which the same brief was handed to another company's model. It arrived in the 5 September commit, with both results filmed along the same camera route and placed side by side in a 59-second video. The stated conditions are these.

  • The same brief — research the real location, model it, render in plain Three.js, verify in the browser
  • The comparison scene was finished before the other result was viewed
  • Identical camera route, timing and shot order. 1920×1080 side by side, with no cropping, grading or re-rendering
  • No downloaded models or external asset services permitted on either side
  • The comparison side authored four meshes in a 3D tool; everything else was generated at start-up
  • Tested in Chrome, requiring WebGL2 rather than WebGPU

Documenting conditions to that level is unusual and deserves credit as such. One qualifier has to travel with it, though. The party that designed and published the comparison is also the party that built one of the two entries. However detailed the conditions, this is a self-published comparison and not an independent benchmark.

So this piece does not report which side came out better. The footage is public and readers can watch it. What is established here is two things: that the conditions were stated, and that no third party exists to check them. One axis that genuinely did diverge in this comparison was not model capability but the choice of asset strategy — one side authored four meshes in a tool, the other went fully to code. That the same brief splits along that fork is itself a restatement of the point in section eight.

How prompts should be migrated when a model generation changes is a separate topic, handled in the piece on adherence and breaking changes. This one looks at the pipeline rather than the specification.

Where to cut the measurement for cost and for quality

Pulling it together: when a long generation job goes to agents, there are two places to take a measurement. Cut cost at the unit and the retry; cut quality at a pass mark set before the work starts.

On cost

  • Define it per deliverable. A whole-project total does not feed the estimate for the next job
  • Count retries in. Counting only the passing run understates what actually gets billed. This repository alone logged fourteen comparison cycles
  • Record wall-clock and summed time separately. The first is delivery, the second is the invoice. Parallel agents pull them far apart
  • Do not reverse a blended rate into a price sheet. Total divided by total tokens hides the input, output and cache-hit mix
  • Do not omit the human hours. Writing a 27KB brief is not free

On quality

  • Write the pass mark down as a number before starting. Per-category floors are what let you call a shortfall a shortfall at the end
  • Separate the judge from the producer. When builders grade their own work, the scores only ever go up
  • Pin the comparison target. Viewpoints fixed by coordinates and field of view, photographs from the same spot — the reference has to be re-measurable
  • Do not judge regressions on a wobbling metric. Base the call on the values that reproduce, not the ones that drift twenty to thirty per cent
  • Ship the defect list with the deliverable. That list is what determines the uses the output is fit for

What this repository actually demonstrates is not a striking 3D scene but a pipeline in which those ten lines are implemented. The scene is the output, and more than half of that output's scores sit below target. The reason there is still something to take away is that it is built so that it can say so itself. Breaking large work into stages with gates between them is organised on the same principle in the piece treating code migration as partitioning and stop conditions.

What could not be confirmed

  • The source and scope of the eight million tokens, two hours and thirty-three dollars could not be pinned down. The top-level README, both world READMEs, the brief file, both final QA reports and the comparison build README contain none of these values. Whether the figure is per deliverable, whether retries count, whether video rendering and documentation are inside it — all unknown. The piece treats them only as numbers circulating outside the repository
  • The storefront denominators do not reconcile. The world README records 129 storefronts identified and a 122-entry census, while the QA report records 21 of 88 named tenants unplaced. What each of the three numbers counts could not be aligned from the documents alone
  • The official documentation for the 3D library and the modelling tool could not be opened. One renders its body through JavaScript so no text was retrievable, and the other returned 403. Consequently every technical statement above rests on what the repository wrote in its own documents, not on vendor documentation
  • The worlds were not run directly. Frame rate, real loading time and how noticeable the procedural texture repetition is are transcribed from the QA report rather than observed
  • The correctness of the figures the Kyoto world revised was not separately verified. The primary cultural-property records were not consulted, so what is confirmed stops at the repository stating those values and naming its data sources
  • The outcome of the two-model comparison was deliberately left out. It is a self-published comparison by one of the two parties, with no third-party reproduction available to check it
  • Reproduction cost could not be confirmed either. The repository holds no record of whether re-running the same brief lands at a similar figure and a similar duration
