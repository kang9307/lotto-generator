AI Smells 2026 - How to Detect AI-Generated Writing and Why It Matters

Can You Spot AI Writing? The 2026 Guide to Detecting AI Smells in Text

May 31, 2026 Tech

Introduction: The "I Think a Machine Wrote This" Feeling

There is a strange moment that happens while reading these days. The grammar is fine, the facts check out, and yet two or three paragraphs in, a quiet suspicion creeps up from somewhere: did a machine write this? You cannot point to a single broken sentence. And still, the feeling is right more often than not. I got curious about where that feeling comes from. What exactly are people reading when they decide something "smells like AI"?

On May 30, 2026, a topic titled "Identifying AI smells in writing" showed up on the Korean tech community news.hada.io. It was a small post with six points, but the comments were the interesting part. People started listing their own personal catalog of AI tells. One pointed at bullet-point abuse. Another pointed at the frequency of the word "therefore." This article takes inspiration from that thread and gathers, in one place, the catalog of AI tells I have built up over a year of working with AI on text, why those tells appear in the first place, and the practical ways to make AI-assisted writing sound human again.

Let me state the core up front. An AI smell is never one forbidden word. It is the smell that rises when several subtle patterns stack on top of each other. That is why "if it uses this word, it's AI" almost always fails as a test. You have to read the accumulation instead. And there is one meta-point that governs this entire piece: this article itself has to read without an AI smell. So I am going to deliberately mix in my own experience, specific examples, and the occasional rough, unbalanced sentence.

1. What "AI Smells" Means - Borrowed From Code Smells

The phrase "AI smells" comes straight from the software-engineering idea of a code smell. Code smell was popularized in the 1990s by Kent Beck and Martin Fowler, and its definition is oddly precise. A code smell is not a bug. The code compiles, the tests pass. But it gives off a signal that something may be structurally wrong: a function that runs too long, duplicated logic, a meaningless name.

The metaphor fits AI writing almost perfectly, and here is why. An AI smell is also not a "bug." The sentence AI produces is not wrong. There are no typos, almost no broken grammar. It is often cleaner than what a human would write. And yet it gives off a signal that something is structurally off. The word "smell" is the exact right word, because a smell is not proof. You cannot convict on it. But once you catch it, you cannot un-smell it.

So everything I call a "tell" in this article is not conclusive evidence. Each one is a signal that raises the probability. One signal proves nothing. But when five or six signals overlap, the smell gets strong. It works exactly like code review: nobody rejects code over a single smell, but when the smells pile up, you decide to refactor.

2. The 10 Common Tells of AI Writing

What follows is a list of ten signals I assembled by reading hundreds of pieces and sorting them into human and AI. Let me stress again: each one, taken alone, is a habit humans share too. The point is whether they swarm into a single piece all at once.

Tell 1) Overuse of "Moreover" and "In Conclusion"

AI writing loves the formulaic phrases that open and close paragraphs. "Moreover," "in conclusion," "in essence," "in other words." Humans use them too, but the frequency is different. When "in conclusion" shows up three times in one piece, that alone is a strong signal. A real person reaches a conclusion once and stops.

Tell 2) Bullet-Point Abuse

The habit of chopping content into bullets even when nothing needs chopping. AI was trained so heavily to "organize information" that it keeps converting prose that should just flow into a list. If you see three bullets and each item is shorter than a full sentence, that is not organization. That is prose that got smashed into fragments.

Tell 3) The Balanced Both-Sidesism

This is the signal I trust most. AI writing almost always ends with "on one hand A, on the other hand B." It treats every position with perfect fairness and lands on "balance is important." Safe, and exactly equivalent to saying nothing. A real person has a position. They take a side and risk being wrong.

Tell 4) The Vague Hedge of "Can Help"

"This approach can help improve efficiency." Does it improve efficiency or not? AI dodges commitment to lower its chance of being wrong, so it escapes into "can." When this appears three or four times in a single paragraph, the whole piece feels slippery, like there is nothing to grip.

Tell 5) The Absence of Emotion and Concrete Experience

This is the decisive one. AI cannot write "at 2 a.m. last night this bug nearly made me throw my keyboard across the room." It is great at generalities, but it has no specific memory of that day, no real number, no failure story. If a piece has no human smell, nine times out of ten it is because someone's actual lived day is missing from it.

Tell 6) Uniform Sentence Length

Humans write a long sentence and then suddenly cut it short. Like this. The breathing is irregular. AI converges on a similar sentence length. Every sentence runs about two lines and the rhythm is flat. Stylometry calls this "low burstiness," and the lower it goes, the stronger the AI signal.

Tell 7) Excessive Transition Words

"Additionally," "furthermore," "moreover," "meanwhile." A transition word glued to the front of nearly every sentence. Humans connect by context without needing one. When transitions are packed this densely, the writing does not read smooth, it reads mechanical.

Tell 8) The "In Today's Fast-Paced World" Cliche

"In today's fast-paced digital landscape." The signature AI opener. A sentence that carries zero information but strikes a pose of importance. A real person opens with a concrete event or scene. A piece that opens on a generic cliche is almost always AI, or a human imitating AI.

Tell 9) The Formulaic Wrap-Up After a List

After laying out a list, it closes with something like "through these various methods, we can achieve better results." A ceremonial sentence that summarizes the list one more time while adding nothing. A human usually just stops at the last item.

Tell 10) Excessive Formality

Formality that does not fit the context. It is a note to a friend, but it reads like an academic paper. AI defaults to a formal register, so it stays stiff even where the moment calls for lightness. When the tone is out of sync with the situation, that itself is a tell.

Summary: one or two of these ten are common in human writing too. But when six or more crowd into a single piece, the AI smell becomes nearly certain. Read the accumulation, not the single signal.

3. Why AI Tells Appear - Training and RLHF

Memorize the list of tells and you hit a wall fast. To catch new tells on your own, you have to understand why they appear. The causes break into two.

First, a language model writes toward the average. It predicts the most probable next word from its training distribution. Answering toward the center of that distribution minimizes the chance of being wrong, so the model leans toward safe generalities over specific claims. Tells 3 (both-sidesism), 4 (vague hedge), and 8 (cliche) all flow from this. Average writing uses average phrasing, and average phrasing is the bland wording that offends no one.

Second, RLHF reinforces formula. In the human-feedback alignment process, a neatly organized answer scores higher on average. A well-bulleted answer, a smoothly transitioned answer, an answer that closes with a summary all get rewarded. So Tells 2 (bullets), 7 (transitions), and 9 (formulaic wrap-up) are the shape of "good writing" the model was explicitly trained toward. Ironically, much of the AI smell is the result of the model trying hard to write well.

And the third cause lives not in the model but in the data. The model does not have that 2 a.m. bug. Even if someone's concrete experience sits in the training data, the model cannot state it as its own. So Tell 5 (absence of experience) is a gap the model cannot fill in principle. That gap is the deepest line dividing human from machine.

4. AI Detection Tools vs Human Intuition - Comparing Accuracy

So why not trust an automated detector instead of human intuition? The short version: in 2026, AI detection tools are dangerous to use as standalone evidence.

Detectors mostly measure burstiness and perplexity (how predictable the text is), on the assumption that AI text is highly predictable with a flat rhythm. There is a large hole in that assumption. People trained to write in a formal, consistent style get flagged, and non-native English speakers writing careful, regular English get flagged at an especially high rate. International students have repeatedly had their genuine essays misclassified as AI and faced disciplinary action over it.

The other direction leaks too. Lightly editing AI text, or just adding a prompt like "increase the burstiness," sails past the detector. The tools produce false positives and false negatives at the same time. Run one piece through several detectors and the verdicts routinely contradict each other.

That said, human intuition is not perfect either. Humans are good at semantic signals like Tell 5 (absence of experience), but well-polished AI text fools them. My conclusion is this. Read the statistical signal from the tool and the semantic intuition of a human together, and treat neither as a sole basis for judgment. Using a detection score as the only evidence in a high-stakes decision like an academic penalty or a hire is reckless.

5. 7 Ways to Make AI Writing Sound Human

Now let me flip direction and lay out how to take AI-assisted text and make it smell human. The core principle is one line. Do not swap the words. Add the information. Replacing words with synonyms does nothing. You remove the smell only by filling in the specificity that only a human could know.

  1. Add concrete experience. The time of day, a real number, the attempt that failed. Turn "efficiency improves" into "a build that took 12 minutes dropped to 4," and Tell 5 dies.
  2. Take a position. Do not end on both-sidesism, pick a side. Instead of "both have merit," write "I choose A, and here is why."
  3. Shake up sentence length. Put a very short sentence right after a long one. Like this. The rhythm comes back to life.
  4. Cut the transitions. Halve the "moreover" and "furthermore." If context already connects the sentences, the transition is dead weight.
  5. Delete the formulaic wrap-up. Remove the summary sentence at the end of lists and the "in today's fast-paced" opener entirely.
  6. Convert to spoken register. Where the tone is stiff, loosen it toward how you would actually say it out loud to a colleague.
  7. Read it aloud. Any sentence that does not sound like speech when you say it is the sentence that smells like AI. Fix only those spots.
Tip: of the seven, number 1 (concrete experience) and number 2 (position) account for more than half the total effect. No amount of rephrasing removes the smell if the specificity of a real day and your own stance are missing.

6. Why AI Smells Matter - SEO, Trust, AdSense, Academic Honesty

The reason to care about AI smells is not simply fear of getting caught. Four concrete stakes are on the table.

First, SEO and search visibility. Google does not punish the fact of AI authorship, but it clearly demotes valueless mass-produced content. A strong AI smell usually means the piece is full of generalities with no unique information, which is a disadvantage in quality signals. Second, trust. The moment a reader feels "oh, a machine wrote this," the authority of the piece collapses. Even if the information is correct, credibility takes the hit. Third, AdSense. AdSense requires original, valuable content, so thin AI-spun pieces struggle with ad approval and retention. Fourth, academic honesty. As AI-use rules tighten across coursework and papers, smell-heavy writing becomes a target of suspicion.

7. Google and AdSense AI Content Policy - What Actually Is the Problem

Let me clear up a common misconception here. "Google punishes AI writing" is not accurate. Google's official position is explicit: it looks at quality and purpose, not the method of production.

What Google's spam policy targets is "content mass-produced primarily to manipulate search rankings." The two key words are manipulate and mass. Whether a human or AI wrote it, text churned out only to chase rankings, with no value, is the target. Conversely, AI-assisted text with real value to readers is fine. In the E-E-A-T framework Google emphasizes (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust), the leading Experience is precisely the gap Tell 5 cannot fill.

AdSense is similar. The policy language is not "no AI" but "original and valuable content." So the Google and AdSense bar reduces to the same single sentence. The question is never "did you use AI," it is "is this valuable to readers." Removing the AI smell turns out to be the same job as filling in that value.

8. The Right Way to Write With AI - Use, Don't Reject

If you have read this far and concluded "so the answer is don't use AI," it is the opposite. I worked on this very piece with AI. The point is not rejection. It is division of labor.

Here is the method I recommend. Do not hand AI the draft. Hand it the verification and the structuring. The concrete experience, the position, the core claims, a human writes those first. Then you ask AI: is the structure of this clear, is there a counterargument I missed, is this paragraph bloated. Hand over generation and the AI smell walks in. Hand over verification and the human's writing gets stronger.

Concretely, this is my loop. 1) The human writes the skeleton and the experience by hand. 2) AI points out awkward sentences and missing logic. 3) The human takes the feedback and rewrites. 4) At the end, read aloud and strip out the transitions and cliches AI is likely to have slipped in. This order keeps AI's productivity while protecting the human voice.

9. The Special Tells of AI Writing in Non-English Languages

Everything above was language-agnostic. Non-English languages carry extra tells, and Korean is a clear case I work in directly. Translationese is the first: phrases that read like a literal carry-over of English prepositions and clause structure, because a model whose training data skews English leaks English syntax when answering in another language. The second is inconsistent register, where formal and casual tones blend in a single paragraph in a way no native speaker would write. The third is pronoun overuse: Korean drops subjects constantly, but AI text glues "we" and "you" to every sentence the way English does.

So editing non-English AI text needs an extra layer that English does not: unwinding translationese into natural speech, deleting unnecessary subjects, and unifying the register to a single consistent tone. If you write in a non-English language, treat language itself as one more variable in managing the AI smell.

10. The Risk - The False Positives of Over-Detection

Finally, the danger on the other side has to be stated plainly. Obsess over hunting AI tells and you start convicting innocent people.

As covered above, detection tools tend to misflag non-native speakers, students trained in formal writing, and people with a consistent style for any number of reasons, including autistic writers. Layer human suspicion on top of that and someone's writing can be unfairly devalued on nothing more than an "feels like AI" impression. The crude formula "uniform sentences equals AI" hurts the diligent, careful writer first.

Caution: an AI tell is a starting point for suspicion, not a verdict. In decisions where a person's future is on the line, such as discipline, evaluation, or hiring, never use a tell or a detection score as the sole basis. Always give the person a chance to explain, and weigh the context and the writing process together.

11. Conclusion and Three Things You Can Do Today

In one sentence: an AI smell is not a single banned word but the smell of generalization, both-sidesism, and absence of experience overlapping, and the way to remove it is not rephrasing but filling in the specificity only a human could know. Detection tools are a supporting signal at best, never the sole basis for a judgment that decides someone's future.

Three things you can apply today.

  1. Count "in conclusion," "moreover," and "furthermore" in your most recent piece. If any appears three or more times, cut it in half. Tells 1 and 7 fade immediately.
  2. Insert one sentence somewhere with a concrete number or failure from that day. A single sentence collapses Tell 5 (absence of experience).
  3. Delete the formulaic wrap-up in your final paragraph. If it closes with "thus we can achieve," cut that sentence and end on your own position instead.

An AI smell appears not because AI is incompetent but because AI was built to write toward the average. And the average does not contain your particular day. The people who write the more readable pieces in the second half of 2026 will not be the ones who rejected AI. They will be the ones who laid their own specificity on top of AI's productivity. The smell is not a constant you have to endure. It is an empty space you can fill and erase.
