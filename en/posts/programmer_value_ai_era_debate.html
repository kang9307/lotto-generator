What the Claim That Code Is the Easy Part Leaves Out

Requirements, legacy, incidents, review - how to find where the difficulty actually moved instead of assuming it vanished

Aug 10, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: when someone in the planning meeting says coding is the easy part now

The moment somebody in a planning meeting says that coding is no longer the hard part, the engineers in the room usually go quiet. Not because they have no answer, but because objecting gets you filed under people who resist change. And that silence goes straight into next quarter headcount plans and delivery estimates.

In August 2026 a post aimed squarely at that sentence set off an argument across developer communities. The rebuttal is blunt. If coding were genuinely trivial, developer compensation would not look the way it does, burnout would not be endemic, and multi-year specialised training would make no sense. And if deciding what to build were genuinely harder than building it, the market would not price the roles that do that work below engineering. The author calls both extremes, code is easy and code is art, a form of cope.

This article is not a summary of that post. It takes the question the rebuttal points at: what does the claim that coding is the easy part quietly drop from the ledger? The short answer is that difficulty did not disappear. It moved. And the place it moved to is mostly not writing code, but reading it and being accountable for it.

1. This argument is a fight over definitions - where does coding end

The debate never resolves because both sides use one word for different things. Define coding as the last step of typing an already settled design into an editor and it really is easy, and AI takes a large share of it. Define coding as the whole act of turning a problem into something computable and it contains domain understanding, data modelling, failure-mode design and deployment strategy. That version is hard now and will stay hard.

1.1 Four definitions and where the difficulty sits in each

Scope of the wordWhat it coversWhere the hard part sitsHow much AI absorbs
TypingEntering settled logic with correct syntaxNothing. It is repetitionMostly absorbed
CodingFunction and class level implementation, unit testsBoundary conditions, error handling designDrafts absorbed, verification stays human
ProgrammingData structure choice, module boundaries, state managementConsequences show up six months laterSuggestions yes, judgement no
Software engineeringArchitecture, operations, migration, team coordinationThe cost of irreversible decisionsAssistive at best

People who say coding got easy almost always mean the first two rows. Engineers who bristle at it are thinking about the bottom two. Both are correct within their own row, which is exactly why the conversation goes nowhere. The first move that makes this argument productive is asking which row the other person means.

1.2 How the definition decides the conclusion in advance

In a headcount planning session this confusion is expensive. Book a productivity gain based on row one, leave the row-four work untouched, and you end up with a shorter schedule and the same amount of work. The symptoms are recognisable.

  • Feature timelines shrink while QA and release preparation stretch out
  • Pull request counts rise while time to merge gets longer
  • New feature velocity improves while time to restore service does not
  • Junior commit volume climbs while senior review hours spike

When all four appear at once, the organisation has not removed difficulty. It has pushed it downstream.

2. Dropped ① requirements only surface once you write code

The claim that deciding what to build is the real difficulty carries a hidden premise: that the decision comes first and implementation follows. Practice usually runs the other way.

2.1 No specification was ever complete

A requirements document always carries two kinds of hole. One is what the author did not know. The other is what the author judged not worth writing down. The second is more dangerous. A line reading same as the existing behaviour routinely turns out to contain three exception branches. That gap is not found in meetings. It is found during implementation.

  • If two users edit the same resource at once, who wins? Rarely specified
  • If an external integration does not respond within three seconds, what does the screen show?
  • Is a partial success a success or a failure, and what does the user see?
  • When a newly mandatory field is empty in historical rows, what is the migration default?
  • For an unauthorised user, do we hide existence entirely or return access denied?

These questions are not missing because the product manager was lazy. They are the kind of question that does not become visible until the problem is expressed as code. Implementation is therefore not downstream of requirements. It is also the probe that discovers them.

2.2 Cheaper implementation means more exploration, not less work

When AI makes first drafts cheap, a sensible team does not bank the saved hours as rest. It builds more options. Comparing three prototypes improves decision quality, but comparing and discarding is itself new work. That mechanism explains why productivity gains rarely convert into free time, which is covered separately in why AI productivity gains do not shorten your working hours.

The direction is what matters here. When implementation gets cheaper, the number of decisions goes up. And decisions are made by people.

3. Dropped ② legacy - most code is edited, not authored

Demonstrations of how easy coding has become almost always start in an empty folder. The file you actually open at work is not empty. It was written by someone who left seven years ago, there are no comments, there are three tests, and one of them has always failed and nobody knows why.

3.1 The greenfield assumption

The difficulty of a legacy change is not in writing the code. It is in establishing how far you are allowed to touch. The real sequence looks like this.

  1. Find every caller of the function, including reflection, config files, batch scripts and other teams repositories
  2. Decide which contracts break if you change it: API response shape, database constraints, external integration formats
  3. Split the change into reversible pieces: feature flags, dual writes, staged cutover
  4. Decide what you will look at after deploy to declare success: metrics and thresholds
  5. Write the rollback procedure before you need it

Of those five, AI reliably helps with part of step one. The rest are questions you can only answer if you know the organisation.

3.2 What a large migration programme actually showed

The Google report on applying LLMs to code migration at scale (arXiv:2504.09691) illustrates the point well. Over twelve months the programme ran 39 migrations, producing 595 changes and 93,574 edits. Three people staffed it.

You can read those numbers two ways. One is the amplification: three people did all of that. The other is what those three people were doing. They chose the migration targets, bounded the safe scope of each transformation, verified generated changes, and classified failures to improve the pipeline. In other words, the model produced the edits and the humans kept the judgement. Results like this also assume a large monorepo, strong static analysis and dense test coverage, and most organisations have none of the three.

4. Dropped ③ incidents - at 3am you need reading, not generating

Incidents are the litmus test for this argument. If code were not the hard part, incident response should be easy too. It is the opposite.

4.1 The difficulty is causal tracing, not authoring

When the pager goes off, the skill you need is not writing code quickly. It is narrowing down what is happening within thirty minutes. The path usually looks like this.

  • Establish blast radius: everyone or a subset, a region, a specific client version
  • Cross-check the change log: recent deploys, config changes, external dependency status pages
  • Correlate signals: error rate, tail latency, queue backlog, connection pool saturation
  • Form one hypothesis and decide how you would falsify it before chasing it
  • Restore first, diagnose second. Reversing that order lengthens the outage

If failures appear only from one range such as 203.0.113.0/24, the problem is more likely routing or policy than application logic. That call does not come from reading code. It comes from knowing the shape of the system.

4.2 Where AI helps and where it does not

AI is genuinely useful during incidents. It interprets unfamiliar stack traces, summarises log patterns and remembers command syntax for you. Three things still stay with a person.

  • Contextual judgement - whether this metric was always like this is known only by someone who has watched the system
  • Risk acceptance - deciding that rolling back now may break data consistency comes bundled with accountability
  • Communication - what to tell customers and executives, and when, is not a technical question

5. Dropped ④ code review - AI increases the volume you must read

Cheaper generation means more generated output. Everyone agrees so far. But generated code has to be read by somebody, and the cost of reading did not fall.

Review is not syntax checking. It is a judgement that this change is safe in our system, which requires the reviewer to know things outside the changed files. Generated code is usually syntactically clean and conventional, which creates a new hazard: it looks good enough to wave through.

  • It fails to find the existing utility and writes a near-duplicate function, so duplication accumulates quietly
  • Exception handling is present in form but swallows failures
  • Tests mirror the implementation and therefore catch no regressions
  • It picks an implementation with different performance characteristics, invisible at 100 rows and obvious at a million
  • Security boundaries drift toward the client side

So the difference between organisations where AI adoption works and where it does not is not the tool. It is review throughput. A team where review was already the bottleneck makes that bottleneck worse the moment it adds generation. Tool choice ultimately attaches here too, and the relationship between tooling and trust is covered in developer goodwill as an asset. Meanwhile the shrinking of public question-and-answer communities during the same period is laid out in the decline of Stack Overflow.

6. Where perception and measurement diverge

The evidence cited most often in this debate is personal perception. I am three times faster is hard to argue with and equally hard to verify. Fortunately a few controlled measurements exist.

SourceDesignHeadline resultRead with care
METR randomised controlled trial (Jul 2025) 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks in their own repositories, AI allowed or disallowed randomly per task Completion time 19% longer with AI allowed. Participants estimated 20% faster afterwards Small sample, limited to developers with about five years on those repositories, early-2025 tooling
DORA 2025 report Survey of roughly 5,000 technology professionals worldwide plus qualitative interviews AI adoption around 90%. AI acts as an amplifier of existing organisational strengths and weaknesses Self-reported survey data. Correlation, not causation
Google migration report (arXiv:2504.09691) 39 migrations over twelve months, three staff 595 changes, 93,574 edits. Generation by model, verification and judgement by people Assumes a single large repository and strong static analysis, an unusual baseline

6.1 Nineteen percent slower, twenty percent faster in the mind

The METR randomised controlled trial is the most uncomfortable item in this debate. Participants forecast a 24% reduction beforehand, still reported a 20% reduction afterwards, and the measured result was a 19% increase in completion time. Economics and machine learning experts had forecast reductions of 38 to 39%.

Reading this as AI is useless overshoots. Participants worked in repositories they had known for years, and in that condition human tacit knowledge is already optimised, leaving little room for a tool to insert itself. What the study actually establishes is narrower and sharper. Perceived productivity is not a trustworthy measurement. And that gap becomes estimation error the moment an organisation plans around it. METR itself announced in February 2026 that it was changing the experiment design, so the figure is best treated as one observation under one set of conditions rather than a settled conclusion.

6.2 What the word amplifier is really saying

The DORA 2025 report frames AI as an amplifier. Teams that were working well work better, and teams with problems grow those problems faster. An organisation with unclear requirements and ageing architecture ships low-quality work more quickly. Seen that way, the conclusion that coding got easy so headcount can shrink has the order backwards. You inspect what is about to be amplified before you attach the amplifier.

7. Things that genuinely did get easier

The other direction deserves an honest entry too. Some work really did get easier, and denying that is the same species of avoidance.

Type of workWhat changedWhat remains human
Boilerplate and config filesNearly goneChecking the defaults fit our environment
First drafts in an unfamiliar language or frameworkEntry cost down sharplyVerifying the idioms are actually that ecosystem standard
One-off scripts and data transformationThe clearest winDefining behaviour when input assumptions break
Test case scaffoldingVolume is easy to growChoosing boundary values that mean something
Documentation and comment draftsDrafting time downFiltering out statements that are not true
Compliance-driven bulk editsRepetitive editing greatly shortenedSpotting exceptions and giving final approval

A pattern shows up in that table. The lower the context dependency and the narrower the correct answer, the bigger the gain. The higher the context dependency and the more valid answers exist, the more a person remains. Coding is the easy part is a verdict on the whole delivered after looking at only the first half.

8. Why this debate lands differently in Korean engineering teams

The same argument reaches a different conclusion under a different organisational structure, and Korean practice has several conditions that bend it.

8.1 What to build is locked in the contract first

Where contracted system integration work is a large share of the business, deciding what to build often sits outside the engineering organisation entirely. Scope is fixed in documents first, and changing it means running a formal change process. In that structure, advice to move toward requirements definition because that is the genuinely hard work is either unexecutable or requires changing profession.

At the same time this structure manufactures a different difficulty. The full responsibility for translating one sentence in a document into a system that actually runs lands on implementation. If the document arrives ambiguous, the cost of that ambiguity arrives with it.

8.2 When code is a more accurate specification than the design document

Few organisations have current design documents at handover time. When document and code disagree, people believe the code. As a result the ability to read code becomes the only real way in. That is why coding is the easy part grates particularly on practitioners here. Most of their day is not spent authoring code but decoding it.

8.3 One team building and operating

Team sizes often mean the same engineers handle deployment and overnight response. In that setting an individual skill portfolio bends as follows.

  • Making things reversible carries more real value than raw implementation speed
  • People who invested in observability, meaning logs, metrics and traces, get woken up less than people who invested only in coverage
  • Choices that do not add operational burden earn more team trust than adopting new technology
  • Someone for whom documenting is a habit is hard to replace even when teams change

That list is also, item by item, what AI tooling struggles to take over. All of it is judgement with accountability attached.

9. A different answer for each seniority level

The answer to so what do I do depends on where you are. The original author also prescribes differently for seniors and juniors, and the direction is worth agreeing with.

9.1 Juniors - foundations you can verify with

  • Data structures and algorithms, memory and pointers, how networks and databases behave
  • The goal is the minimum knowledge to recognise generated code as wrong
  • Get fluent with a debugger. Print statement habits stall on genuinely hard problems
  • Practise splitting your changes into pieces someone else can review
  • Tool usage ages fast. Verification ability lasts

9.2 Mid-level - learn the shape of the system

  • You should be able to draw the dependency map of the service you own
  • Join incident retrospectives and leave the prevention items in code, not in a document
  • Get comfortable with reversible deployment: feature flags, staged cutover
  • Take on reviewer duty. Reading ability improves fastest in review

9.3 Seniors and leads - leave the reasoning behind

  • Record decisions and why they were made. Context is the scarcest asset in the AI era
  • Learn the language of users and the business. That is the original author prescription too
  • Manage review throughput as a tracked team bottleneck
  • Inspect what is about to be amplified before adopting the tool

Solo developers end up holding all three roles at once, and the workflow under that condition is covered separately in the reality of agentic coding for Korean solo developers.

10. What could not be verified

  • Specific DORA 2025 figures such as deployment throughput and change failure rate were not available in the accessible summaries, so only adoption and the amplifier framing are cited here
  • No authoritative primary survey of AI coding tool adoption or code review maturity in Korean engineering organisations was found. Section 8 is structural analysis, not statistics
  • Compensation gap statistics between engineering and product or business analysis roles in Korea were not confirmed and are not cited. The original compensation argument is made about overseas markets
  • The exact share of the arXiv:2504.09691 edits generated by the model is not stated here. Only change counts, edit counts and staffing are cited
  • The revised design and results of the follow-up METR experiment are not yet available

Conclusion: difficulty does not vanish, it relocates

The sentence code was never the hard part stings for more than reasons of pride. For it to be true, requirements would have to be complete in advance, the codebase would have to be clean, incidents would have to not happen, and review would have to be free. None of those describe working conditions.

The opposite position, that code is art, is not the answer either. Attaching craftsmanship to hand-typing boilerplate is a defence, not an argument. What actually happened is a repricing. The unit cost of producing fell, and the unit cost of deciding what to produce, verifying what was produced and owning it when it breaks went up.

So the useful question for an individual is not whether coding got easier. It is what share of my day consists of easily replaced work. Most people are surprised when they actually count. Pure typing time is shorter than expected, and reading, checking and coordinating take far longer. The longer half is the half that is appreciating.

There is one useful question for an organisation as well. If implementation speed rises 20%, can review, QA and operations absorb that 20%? If they cannot, speed accumulates as inventory, and inventory settles up as incidents. As of August 2026, that is where the practical conclusion of this debate sits.
