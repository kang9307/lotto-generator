The intake queue breaks first

Anyone running a vulnerability reporting channel has felt the queue stretch over the past year. Submissions climbed, the number of things actually worth fixing did not climb with them, and the hours spent sorting doubled. This is not a management failure. It is a structural shift: the cost of finding a candidate vulnerability collapsed, while the cost of confirming one did not move at all.

The clearest case is curl. Its paid bug bounty, launched in April 2019, closed on January 31, 2026 after nearly seven years, 87 confirmed vulnerabilities, and more than $100,000 paid out. Maintainer Daniel Stenberg pointed to the valid-report ratio falling from north of 15 percent to below 5 percent. The program did not run out of money. It ran out of the patience required to open twenty reports to salvage one.

This piece is not about the people using AI to find bugs. It is about the other side of the transaction — the design problem faced by whoever receives the reports. The story of AI-assisted researchers landing large payouts is covered separately in the write-up on finding Google vulnerabilities with AI for a six-figure bounty. What follows is about the other nineteen reports that arrive alongside the good one.

The bottleneck moved from discovery to verification

A bug bounty is fundamentally a cost-shifting arrangement. Discovery is outsourced to external researchers; the organization spends its own people on verification and remediation. The arrangement holds as long as discovery is more expensive than verification. Finding things was hard, so you paid for finds, and a small internal team could confirm them.

That premise inverted. Reading an entire codebase for suspicious constructs, enumerating exposed assets, matching against known patterns — all of that is now minutes of work. Meanwhile, opening one report, standing up an environment, attempting reproduction and scoring the impact still consumes tens of minutes to several hours of a human being's time. When supply multiplies a hundredfold and throughput stays flat, the queue bursts. It is arithmetic.

The real problem is that plausibility got cheap

Low-quality reports have always existed. They used to be obvious. A pasted scanner dump, incoherent sentences, no impact statement at all — a triager could close them in three seconds.

The invalid reports arriving now look nothing like that. They carry a weakness classification, a paragraph of impact analysis, complete sentences, sometimes even quoted code. Except the quoted function is not in the repository and the described behavior never occurs. Establishing that a report is fiction now requires reading it to the end and trying it yourself. Triage cost did not fall. It rose.

At DEF CON 34, triage leads from the major platforms sat down to sort through this, and the taxonomy that came out of that session is practically useful.

  • Hallucinated findings — a nonexistent vulnerability described in a convincing format. The better the formatting, the longer it takes to disprove.
  • Volume-padded reports — a trivial observation buried under dozens of paragraphs of background and generality, so the actual claim is hard to locate.
  • Automated mass submissions — tool output sprayed across many programs by someone with little domain expertise. The volume itself is the problem, independent of any single report's quality.

What the three share is that none of them requires bad intent. Some are opportunistic, but a large share come from people who genuinely believe they found something. That is why any approach framed as filtering out bad actors misses.

How programs actually die

Bug bounty programs do not die because the reward budget runs dry. They die because the triage staff burns out. The distinction matters because the remedies diverge completely. A budget problem is solved by lowering the reward ceiling. A staffing problem is not — cutting rewards does not slow inbound volume, and the skilled researchers leave first.

Smaller projects fall first. A large platform can hire more triagers; an open source project with two or three maintainers has no absorption capacity at all. curl closed early partly because curl is unusually popular, but also because it illustrates a general rule: the organization without slack reaches the limit first.

Why reproducibility becomes the only test

Discussions of remedies almost always open with a proposal to filter out AI-written reports. That direction does not work, for two reasons, and both are decisive.

First, the detection is not possible. No reliable method identifies the authoring tool from prose. Text classifiers have already failed in other domains, and they failed in an ugly way: the false positives do not scatter randomly but concentrate on particular groups — non-native writers, people with formal style, people who use templates. Security reports are formal and templated by nature. Bolt on a classifier and the conscientious reporters get flagged first.

Second, the detection is not needed. What sustains a program is valid vulnerabilities, not authorial craft. A report drafted with AI that reproduces and has confirmed impact is a good report. A report hand-written over three nights that does not reproduce is a cost to absorb. The tool is not a variable worth measuring.

Three questions remain: does it reproduce, what is the impact, was it in scope. Policy should be written from those three if it is meant to be enforced. A ban on AI use relies on self-declaration, so it penalizes only the honest. A rule that reports without reproduction steps are closed can be applied from the report itself.

Attaching the reward to the proof

Google's 2026 rule changes to its open source vulnerability reward program apply exactly that logic to payout design. The published updates removed cash rewards for product vulnerabilities and other security issues at the lower tiers and moved toward requiring stronger evidence such as an OSS-Fuzz reproducer or a merged patch for certain tiers. Reporting on the Android and Chrome programs described a parallel adjustment: concentrating rewards on vulnerability classes that automated tooling has more trouble reaching.

This is not stinginess. It is moving the attachment point of the reward. Payment used to attach to «I found it». It now attaches to «I confirmed it» and «I fixed it». Part of the verification burden shifts to the reporter, and reports that carry it get paid properly.

HackerOne's move to require identity verification for paid bug bounty submissions from August 2026 sits on the same axis but does something different. It is not a quality bar; it is a price increase on accounts. Spinning up unlimited anonymous identities to spray volume gets more expensive. The trade-off is real, though: the same barrier applies to skilled researchers, and it excludes people in regions or circumstances where submitting identity documents is not viable. Effect and side effect belong in the same calculation.

An intake rubric: what counts and what does not

Deciding in advance which report attributes are evidence and which are noise means triagers stop re-deriving that judgment for every submission.

The column worth staring at is «not used». Attributes that served for years as proxies for report quality have been demoted. Clean prose, orderly structure, a plausible classification identifier — none of these is evidence of care anymore; they are the default. Keep trusting the proxies and the best-disguised invalid report reaches the front of the queue.

The reporter side: surface got cheap, depth did not

Viewed from the submitting side the same shift inverts. Where the reward structure tilts is, in practice, the question of where to spend research hours.

What got cheap and what did not

  • Cheap now — matching against known weakness patterns, enumerating public assets and endpoints, assisted reading of large codebases, drafting the report, working through documentation in unfamiliar languages.
  • Not cheaper — flaws that only appear once you understand what the service actually does. Business logic errors, multi-step privilege escalation, state machines that break only in a particular order, timing-dependent races.
  • More expensive — reporter credibility. With invalid submissions common, a report from an unfamiliar account starts from a position of suspicion.

The conclusion is straightforward: the return on surface enumeration is falling and will keep falling. When thousands of people sweep the same public surface, duplicate rates rise and unit value drops. Areas requiring domain understanding do not see the same crowding. A tool can read the code for you; it cannot tell you what this organization's settlement rules are or which sequence of operations is never supposed to happen.

If time has to be allocated, the rule is this: an hour spent building a reproducer is worth more than an hour spent polishing prose. The first hour subtracts from the verifier's clock. The second is no longer counted by anyone.

There is a genuine cost to newcomers here. Reputation weighting and identity verification favor people who already have a name. That is a side effect rather than an intent, and program designers should keep a separate queue or a lower-tier entry path for first-time reporters. Without one, the reporter population simply ages out over five years.

Operational design: intake, duplicates, automation, reputation

There are roughly six levers worth pulling, and each one should be read together with what it costs. Pull any single lever hard enough and something else gives way.

Duplicate handling is dispute management

When enumeration is cheap, many people sweep the same surface simultaneously. Duplicate rulings necessarily rise, and duplicates are the ruling reporters resent most. Trust lost here costs you the good reporters first. Three things demonstrably help.

  • Use first-report time as the rule, and show that timestamp to the reporter. Invisible reasoning breeds more distrust than an unfavorable ruling does.
  • Pay something for a duplicate that carries a better reproducer. It genuinely reduced verification time, which is a contribution worth compensating.
  • Say so when the defect was already known internally. If that surfaces later, the whole program's credibility takes the hit.

Do not give automated triage the power to close

Running inbound reports through a language model for first-pass sorting is a natural move, and it genuinely helps with ordering. What has to enter the calculation is that the two error directions are not symmetric.

Letting a fake through costs one more human read. Discarding a real one leaves the vulnerability in place. Because the two errors carry entirely different prices, automated triage should be confined to adjusting order, with final closure left to a person. That principle holds no matter how accurate the automation becomes. Cost asymmetry is not cancelled by accuracy.

A response SLA you cannot meet does more damage than none

Many programs promise a first response within some number of business days. At double the inbound volume that promise mostly breaks, and a broken promise is worse than no promise. Rewrite the response window to something achievable and then hold it. If the window grew, say why in the policy. An unexplained extension reads as abandonment.

Removing rewards is not removing the channel

Closing a paid program can be entirely reasonable. Even then, the unpaid disclosure channel should stay open. Volume drops sharply once money leaves, but someone who finds a genuinely serious defect still needs somewhere to send it. Close that too and there is no telling where it goes instead. Making this distinction explicit is the most important part of any program shutdown notice.

Safe harbor: the one protection a program can actually offer

Safe harbor is the clause in which a program states it will not pursue legal action over good-faith research conducted inside the stated scope and conditions. Standard language lives in the disclose.io open source policy templates and in CISA's vulnerability disclosure policy template; U.S. federal agencies are required to publish a VDP under CISA's binding operational directive BOD 20-01.

Automation raises the stakes on this clause for a simple reason: going out of scope by accident is far easier than it was when everything was done by hand. An automated sweep follows a redirect onto another host, a subdomain list quietly includes an affiliate's asset, request volume runs higher than intended. If policy does not address that situation in advance, a good-faith researcher and the organization collide after the fact.

The items that actually belong in the clause:

  • Both an in-scope list and an out-of-scope list — listing only inclusions leaves the boundary vague. Exclusions are what reduce real disputes.
  • Permitted and prohibited testing types — denial of service attempts, social engineering, physical access, and access to other people's accounts or data are normally prohibited.
  • Rules for encountering data — confirm the minimum necessary, then stop, report, and delete. Do not download or retain.
  • A numeric rate limit for automated tooling — «avoid excessive traffic» is not a standard anyone can follow.
  • Notice that safe harbor does not reach third-party assets — cloud providers, payment processors, and contractor infrastructure are not the organization's to authorize.
  • A procedure for going out of scope — a clause saying that stopping and reporting immediately carries no penalty is what keeps mistakes from being hidden.
  • The disclosure timing process — when, what, and agreed with whom, including what happens if agreement fails.

Do not misread what safe harbor covers. It is a promise by one organization. It does not stop a criminal proceeding, it does not bar a third party's claim, and it does not exempt anyone from another country's law. The U.S. Department of Justice policy announced in May 2022 defines good-faith security research and directs prosecutors to decline those cases, but it is internal charging guidance, not immunity. Civil liability and state law remain untouched by it.

The U.S. context: what good-faith research does and does not shield

In the United States the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act remains the statute that governs unauthorized access, and the 2022 DOJ policy sits on top of it as charging guidance. The policy's own definition is the useful part: accessing a computer solely for good-faith testing, investigation, or correction of a security flaw, carried out in a manner designed to avoid harm to individuals or the public, with the resulting information used primarily to promote the security of the class of devices or services involved.

Read that definition closely and the operative words are solely, designed to avoid harm, and primarily to promote security. Research that also serves as leverage in a commercial negotiation, that copies more data than confirmation required, or that continues after impact became apparent falls outside it. And the policy binds federal prosecutors, not private plaintiffs and not state prosecutors.

Scope documents are authorization documents

This is the practical consequence and it generalizes beyond the United States. The published scope document is what stands in for authorization. Testing inside scope, using the methods the policy permits, gives a researcher something concrete to point at. Outside scope, intent to report changes nothing about the exposure.

Both sides get working guidance from that.

  • Researchers — test only the assets and methods a published bug bounty or disclosure program names. A service without a program is not a target. «It looked insecure» is not authorization.
  • Operators — pinning scope and prohibited actions in writing protects researchers and the organization alike. Vague scope endangers good-faith reporters and leaves the organization with no standard to apply afterward.
  • Both — list affiliate and subsidiary assets separately. Shared branding is not an indication of shared authorization.

Where national programs fit

Alongside company-run programs, national coordination bodies operate their own channels — CISA's coordinated disclosure process in the United States, run through the VINCE platform hosted by Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute, and equivalents elsewhere. These exist to coordinate multi-vendor cases and unresponsive vendors.

One misconception worth clearing up: the existence of a national reporting channel is not permission to probe someone's production service. Coordination bodies handle reports about defects; they do not grant access rights to the systems those defects live in. The program and the authorization are entirely different layers.

How the jurisdictions differ

Handle a product sold in several countries and a single vulnerability lands under several rule sets. This map should be consulted before a disclosure deadline is set.

What the table shows is that the frameworks push in different directions. The U.S. arrangement operates as pressure that accelerates disclosure when a vendor stalls. The Chinese provisions operate in reverse, blocking publication before remediation. Japan's scheme declines to compel anyone but keeps a state-maintained intake and coordination channel open. Korea focuses less on the publication process and more on the legality of the access itself. Applying one global disclosure policy uniformly will conflict with at least one of them.

What changes about coordinated disclosure deadlines

The concept of a deadline has not changed. Ninety days and forty-five days are still the working numbers. What changed is when the clock starts and what gets published when it expires.

Double the inbound volume and vendor first response slows. The deadline clock, however, starts at submission. The result is a situation where half the window elapses before the vendor has opened the report at all. That is neither the vendor's fault nor the reporter's; it is a queue problem, and existing deadline conventions were not designed with it in mind.

The trial Google Project Zero began in July 2025 approaches this from another angle. It keeps the existing policy — 90 days to fix, plus 30 days for patch adoption once fixed — while publishing, roughly a week after a report is filed, only which vendor and product received a report and when the deadline falls. No technical detail, no reproduction path. The target is the gap where an upstream fix fails to reach downstream products.

CISA's side sets the complementary rule: where a vendor is unresponsive or will not commit to a reasonable remediation timeframe, disclosure is possible 45 days after first contact regardless of whether a patch exists. The convention of treating 45 to 90 days as the reasonable band comes from here.

Three practical changes for the receiving side.

  • Negotiate the deadline in the first reply. Starting on day 60 out of a backlog is not negotiation, it is notification. A first response should carry an expected timeline and a channel for adjustment, not just an acknowledgement.
  • Stage the disclosure. There is no reason for existence-and-deadline, mitigations, and technical detail to publish on the same day. Staging creates room to handle jurisdictional constraints and user protection at once.
  • Do not default to simultaneous worldwide publication. Publication constraints differ by jurisdiction, so order the release against where the product actually ships.

One rule for reporters handling deadlines: a deadline is a predictability device, not a punishment. Stating it up front and holding to it draws more cooperation than extending and contracting it as the situation seems to warrant.

What could not be confirmed

  • The per-platform submission growth rates, backlog multiples, and resolution-rate drops shared at the DEF CON 34 panel have not been published as datasets by the platforms. Because they rest on a session write-up, the taxonomy and direction were used here but the figures were not carried over as numbers.
  • Reporting on HackerOne's identity verification mandate splits between August 1 and August 14, 2026 for the effective date. The original notice could not be checked, so the text says only «from August 2026».
  • The current tier-by-tier reward table for the KISA reward program could not be confirmed on the public page, so no Korean payout figures were carried into the text beyond the framework itself.
  • Revised reward ceilings for Google's Android and Chrome programs appeared in press coverage but could not be cross-checked against the official rules pages, so the amounts were left out.
  • No credible statistic exists for the share of reports written with AI. The value is undeterminable by construction, which is itself the reason this piece uses reproducibility rather than tool detection as the criterion.

Conclusion: designing the intake queue is now security work

The bug bounty began as a simple arrangement: pay whoever finds it. The simplicity worked because finding was expensive. With that condition gone, the center of gravity has moved from reward design to the design of intake and verification.

Three points. First, who wrote a report cannot be determined and does not need to be — reproducibility is what remains. Second, every countermeasure carries a price, so decide what is being reduced and what is being lost at the same time: identity verification builds a barrier, automated triage introduces false negatives, reward cuts lose reporters. Third, the scope document is the authorization document. Testing a system that no published program has authorized in writing carries legal exposure regardless of intent, and no organization's safe harbor clause reaches beyond its own assets.

If you run a program, the work right now is not adjusting the payout table but rereading the intake form, the scope document, and the safe harbor clause. If you submit, it is moving the hours from prose to reproducers. One standard survives for both sides — only what can be confirmed has value.
