Introduction: why 「we can get it later」 does not hold

Having a right to demand material and exercising that right in time are two different things. The right survives delay. The material does not. Logs are deleted on a schedule, storage is overwritten by newer records, and systems keep changing underneath. Nothing guarantees that the same demand months later produces the same material.

And even where material survives, a second problem waits. Data received late cannot describe what happened to it in the meantime. Opening a file tells you nothing about whether it is what the system wrote, whether anyone opened it on the way, or what fell outside the scope that was pulled. That information is not inside the file. It is created only during acquisition.

Hence a single proposition.

Digital evidence has to be acquired early in the matter, in one pass, and in a state where integrity is assured. Miss any one of the three and it is not recoverable afterwards.

Behind that proposition sits a wider principle: what cannot be verified is not evidence. Two companion articles work the same principle from different sides. Why You Cannot Trust the Client - Trust Boundaries and Web Security Controls is the technical axis: where identification controls have broken down, an account name sitting in a log does not establish who acted. Personal Data Access Requests - How Far You Can Ask for IPs, Identifiers and Logs is the rights axis: where the entitlement to logs comes from, and why 「there are no records」 and 「everything was normal」 cannot both stand. This article is the acquisition axis. It does not repeat what the other two cover, so follow the links when you need the controls or the request procedure.

Across the three axes the same principle wears different faces. On the technical axis it reads 「a log from a system without controls cannot establish who acted」. On the rights axis it reads 「without records you cannot claim things were normal」. Here on the acquisition axis it reads 「if the acquisition itself is not recorded, nothing shows that the material matches the original」.

The article has six parts. Part one is what the standards require, part two is why early, part three is why in one pass, part four is why with integrity assured, part five is how the route of acquisition changes things, and part six is what can still be done with what remains. Section 3 and sections 7 to 9 are the core.

1. The phases of handling digital evidence - identification, collection, acquisition, preservation

Handling digital evidence is not one act but a sequence of named phases. That matters because the naming itself breaks the flat claim 「we received the data」 into several separately answerable questions: which phase, performed when, by whom, recorded how.

1.1 The four phases NIST names

NIST SP 800-86 defines digital forensics as 「the application of science to the identification, collection, examination, and analysis of data while preserving the integrity of the information and maintaining a strict chain of custody for the data」. Two constraints are built into the definition itself: preserving integrity, and maintaining chain of custody. Drop those two and the remaining activity is not forensics, it is opening files.

  • Collection. Identifying, labeling, recording and acquiring data from the possible sources of relevant data. SP 800-86 attaches a condition: this must follow procedures that preserve the integrity of the data
  • The same document says collection is typically performed in a timely manner, because of the likelihood of losing dynamic data such as current network connections, and of losing data from battery-powered devices
  • Examination. Forensically processing large volumes of collected data with a combination of automated and manual methods, assessing and extracting data of particular interest. Integrity preservation is a condition here too
  • Analysis. Analysing the results of examination using legally justifiable methods and techniques, to derive useful information addressing the questions that prompted the collection and examination
  • Reporting. The final phase, which may include describing the actions used, explaining how tools and procedures were selected, determining what further actions are needed, and providing recommendations for improvement
  • The practical value is that the phases have names in this order. 「We looked at the data」 decomposes into which of collection, examination, analysis and reporting took place, and how each was documented

1.2 Why the international standard fences off initial handling

ISO/IEC 27037:2012 is titled 「Guidelines for identification, collection, acquisition and preservation of digital evidence」. The title enumerates four activities, and the scope of the standard is limited to exactly those four, that is, to the initial handling process. Analysis, interpretation and presentation in court fall to other documents and to each jurisdiction's procedural law.

  • Fencing initial handling off as its own standard is itself a statement. Whatever is lost in the first phase is not recovered by doing the later phases well, and that judgement is what the boundary encodes
  • The four activities are genuinely different jobs. Identification is finding what is where, collection is taking the media or device, acquisition is producing a copy of its content, and preservation is keeping that state intact
  • With that split available, 「we secured the data」 can be interrogated four ways: what was located and how, what was taken, how the copy was made, and how it has been held since
  • The devices in scope reach well past ordinary computers to mobile phones, memory cards, navigation systems, digital cameras and networked devices. The list is indicative rather than exhaustive
  • This standard is sold rather than published openly, and this article cites it only within its public title and scope statement. What could not be checked against the full text sits in the reservations section

2. What the standards require - minimal alteration, full documentation, verifiability

More fundamental than the phases are the principles that apply across all of them. Different documents phrase them differently, but they collapse into three: change the subject as little as possible, record everything you did, and let someone else reach the same result.

2.1 The five properties evidence has to have

In its section on legal considerations RFC 3227 lists five properties computer evidence must satisfy: Admissible, Authentic, Complete, Reliable, Believable. These are not five independent boxes; they hold each other up.

  • Admissible. Does it meet the form that procedural law requires. This is the one property fixed by each jurisdiction's law rather than by technique
  • Authentic. Does it come from the source it claims. Without a record made at acquisition, this is the first property to wobble
  • Complete. Is it a slice cut from one side of the story. Section 14 on selection bias aims squarely at this property
  • Reliable. Do the collection and handling methods leave room to doubt the result. Only recorded tools and procedures can answer
  • Believable. Is the account intelligible. Raw material acquires this property only when how you got to it travels with it

2.2 Minimal alteration and reproducibility

In its section on the collection procedure, RFC 3227 states the transparency requirement directly.

The methods used to collect evidence should be transparent and reproducible. You should be prepared to reproduce precisely the methods you used, and have those methods tested by independent experts. (RFC 3227 §3.1)

  • Reproducibility is a principle for a plain reason. A procedure that cannot be reproduced cannot be checked, and material produced by an uncheckable procedure gives no ground for saying what it is
  • RFC 3227 also names what to avoid. Do not shut down until evidence collection is complete. Much evidence may be lost, and startup or shutdown scripts may have been altered to destroy evidence
  • Do not trust the programs on the system. The RFC says to run evidence gathering programs from appropriately protected media
  • Do not run programs that modify the access time of all files on the system. If the act of examining changes the subject, every observation afterwards is no longer an observation of the original state
  • The same document says to keep detailed notes including dates and times, generate automatic transcripts where feasible, and document the difference between the system clock and UTC. Material with inconsistent time bases cannot support a timeline reading at all
  • There is a privacy requirement alongside. Make sure that information collected along with the evidence is not available to anyone who would not normally have access to it. Acquisition is not unlimited harvesting

3. How to check an answer that says 「this is the standard」

One sentence turns up more than any other wherever technical judgements are exchanged: 「this is the industry standard」. It has two properties at once. It persuades strongly, and it is not verifiable as stated. Yet standards by definition exist as documents, and existing as a document means a number can be given. This section builds a checking method on that single property.

3.1 Common practice is not a basis

「Everyone does it that way」 is a factual claim with no means of checking it inside the sentence. Who is everyone, how many, and as of when are all missing.

  • A practice may be a standard in waiting, or a residue left over after the standard moved. From outside, the two look identical
  • Claims about practice have no falsifiable shape. One counter-example is absorbed as an exception, and so are a hundred
  • Claims about standards do have that shape. Give the number and the clause, and anyone can open the document; if it says no such thing, the claim is simply wrong
  • So the move on receiving 「it is common practice」 is not rebuttal but translation: ask which document the practice rests on
  • If the answer is that there is no such document, that too is useful information. From that point the statement should be handled as a choice rather than as a technical necessity

3.2 An expert opinion is not, by itself, a basis either

This is not a claim that expert opinion is worthless. It is a claim that what the opinion rests on is the basis, and the person's credentials are not. Credentials raise the probability that an opinion has a basis; they do not substitute for one.

  • Experts in the same field routinely disagree. If credentials were the basis, that fact would have no explanation
  • Every opinion carries a scope of review. Without knowing what was and was not examined, you cannot tell how far the opinion reaches
  • 「It was reviewed」 and 「the review found this」 are different statements. The first asserts a process, the second asserts content. What usually needs checking is the second
  • Opinions rest on assumptions. Where an assumption does not match the facts, the conclusion may be logically sound and still not apply to the matter at hand
  • So the thing to ask for is not qualifications but the review criteria, the review scope and the reference documents. With those three, the opinion becomes a checkable object

3.3 Model output is not a source

Text generated by a language model is increasingly offered as a basis. Using the tool is not the problem. The problem is treating the output as a basis without checking the source. Language models do produce standard numbers and clause references that do not exist, in flawless format, and such output is visually indistinguishable from the real thing.

  • The reliability of model output is unrelated to how fluent the sentence is. If anything, fluency raises the chance it passes without a check
  • Invented numbers are also the easiest class of error to catch: open the document at that number and you are done. The failure mode is nobody opening it
  • Models are especially weak on amendment history. Repealed provisions and superseded wording come out looking exactly like current text
  • Even when a quotation reads convincingly, whether that sentence is actually in the source is a separate question, and standards documents are searchable phrase by phrase
  • In short, a language model is useful as a tool for finding where to look and unusable as a tool for settling what is true. Keep that line and the tool keeps its value

3.4 The three-step check, with sentences you can reuse

Verification takes three steps, none of which needs specialist knowledge.

  1. Ask for the number. Which document, which clause. A standard has a number; if there is no number, it is not a standard
  2. Open the source. With the number in hand, read that part of the document yourself. For public documents this costs nothing; for paid standards, the public title, scope and summary are the boundary of what can be checked
  3. Check reproducibility. Ask whether another person following the procedure the document describes would reach the same result. That property is exactly what RFC 3227 §3.1 requires

4. Logs disappear with time - rotation, retention, overwriting

Underneath the idea that acquisition can wait sits an assumption: that the material stays put. That assumption is contradicted explicitly by the standards.

4.1 The natural attrition of rotation and retention

NIST SP 800-86 splits data by volatility and then refuses to let non-volatile data off the hook. Non-volatile data may also be somewhat dynamic in nature, and the example the document gives is log files that are overwritten as new events occur. Sitting on disk is not a guarantee of survival.

  • Log rotation. Most servers roll a log file over at a size or age threshold and keep only a set number of older files. Anything past that count is designed to be deleted
  • Fixed-size buffers. Records held in a ring buffer overwrite the oldest entries once capacity is reached. Here loss tracks traffic volume rather than elapsed time, so records from the busiest periods vanish fastest
  • Retention policy. In its section on disposal the OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet states that log data, temporary debug logs and backups, copies and extractions must not be destroyed before the duration of the required data retention period, and must not be kept beyond this time. Absence after the period is policy being followed, not a defect
  • Statutory retention. Where law sets a minimum retention period for access records, as Korea does through Article 29 of its Personal Information Protection Act and the accompanying notification on safety measures, a minimum is precisely what it is: nothing guarantees the survival of anything older
  • Collection pipelines. Where logs ship to a central server, transport failures, disk exhaustion and configuration changes leave whole intervals empty. Such gaps are often noticed only after the source host has already rotated its local copy away
  • Infrastructure churn. When an instance is destroyed or a container is redeployed, the local records inside go with it. This loss arrives without notice and follows the operations calendar, entirely independently of the matter

4.2 This is design, not deletion

All six share one feature: none of them requires anyone's intent. They are what a correctly operated system does under its normal procedures. That is what makes them decisive for timing.

  • The fact that material is absent, on its own, points at nothing, because natural attrition and other causes produce the same end state
  • Late acquisition therefore costs two things at once: the material, and the basis for distinguishing why the material is gone
  • Acquire early and the reverse holds. Even if the source later disappears, the copy taken at acquisition remains, and subsequent attrition drops out of the dispute
  • The approach of a retention deadline is a computable quantity. Read the policy and the window for acquisition falls out as arithmetic
  • Which yields one operational consequence: a preservation demand does not need to wait on the outcome of any other process. Retention clocks run at their own pace regardless

5. The order of volatility - what has to be fixed first

That acquisition has an order was standardised long ago. RFC 3227 dates from 2002 and is designated BCP 55. Its central instruction is one line: 「Proceed from the volatile to the less volatile」.

  • The practical conclusion is single. Start by asking for what disappears last, and the rest is already gone. A demand that begins at archival media begins with the six rows above it out of play
  • RFC 3227 sets out the collection steps as: establish relevance, work out the order of volatility, remove external avenues of change, collect the evidence, record clock drift, document the steps and note the people involved
  • Removing external avenues of change has a trap. The same document warns that simply disconnecting or filtering from the network may trigger deadman switches that detect being off the net and wipe evidence
  • The order of volatility is not an order of importance. The most important material may well sit in the longest-lived row. The only axis is speed of loss

6. The losses that delay makes unrecoverable

Delay does not only cost material. Losing material also removes the questions only that material could answer, and those questions are not answered by substitutes.

Whether a given access continued an existing session or began a new authenticated one, for instance, can be answered directly only while the session identifier survives. Much of the relevant session state lives in memory, high in the RFC 3227 ordering. Once that is gone, no amount of careful reading of the remaining records answers the question directly, and the work moves to the indirect inference in section 15. Indirect inference is powerful when it holds, but it needs more assumptions than a direct record does.

6.1 Preservation demands come with a clock attached

One jurisdiction has written that time constraint into procedural law. Article 197(3) of Japan's Code of Criminal Procedure allows prosecutors and judicial police, where necessary for a seizure or a seizure with a record order, to specify which of the communication history records held by a telecommunications carrier in the course of business are needed and to request in writing that they not be erased, for a period not exceeding 30 days. Article 197(4) permits extension where particularly necessary, while capping the total: the period for which non-erasure is requested may not exceed 60 days in aggregate.

  • What the provision reveals is not the cap but the premise. The legislature treated communication history as something that disappears if left alone. A preservation mechanism is unnecessary for material that persists
  • A 60-day aggregate ceiling also means preservation is itself finite. It buys time; it does not remove time from the problem
  • The same provision requires the request to be made in writing and the target to be specified. What was requested, and when, becomes a record in its own right
  • Paragraph 5 allows a request that the existence of the demand not be disclosed without good reason, reflecting a judgement that preservation partly depends on the subject not knowing about it
  • Other jurisdictions frame preservation differently, but the underlying physical fact is the same everywhere: the source keeps changing while procedures run. Physics does not check the jurisdiction

7. Ask in instalments and scope control passes to the producer

Part three starts here, across three sections on why in one pass is a condition. All three share a premise: the conclusion holds even assuming the producer acts in perfect good faith. Nothing here presumes forgery or concealment.

Ask for material across several rounds and the decision about what to pull, and on what key, moves to whoever holds the data. The requesting side cannot point at what is missing, because it does not know what it does not know. This is information asymmetry, not attitude.

  • Korea's statute puts scope-setting inside the procedure. Article 106(3) of the Criminal Procedure Act provides that where the object is a computer disk or similar information storage medium, the stored information shall be printed out or copied within a defined scope and produced in that form. Defining the scope becomes a procedural act
  • The proviso to the same paragraph allows seizing the medium itself only where defining a scope and printing or copying is impossible, or would make the purpose of the seizure markedly difficult to achieve. The statute's shape makes clear which is the rule and which the exception
  • Splitting into rounds moves that decision outside the procedure and out of the record. What was pulled survives; what was not pulled does not
  • Careful wording does not fully close the gap. A scope description written by someone who does not know the internal data model can diverge from that model, and the divergence is filled in by the other side's reading
  • Which is why a request should describe the nature of the information and the retrieval key rather than name a file. The drafting method sits in section 4 of the rights-axis article
  • Acquiring in one pass dissolves the problem for a simple reason: narrowing is pushed after acquisition. Fix the whole first, decide what to look at second, and what you did not look at is still inside the acquired set

8. Drift between rounds - nothing guarantees round one and round three match

The second loss comes from the time axis. Real time passed between the first instalment and the third, and the source did not stand still. Nothing inside the material supports the claim that both came from the same state of the source.

  • Natural attrition from section 4.1 keeps running throughout. An interval that existed at round one may have rotated away by round three, and that fact is not marked anywhere in the round three output
  • The retrieval basis can differ between rounds. The same request wording, executed by a different person with a different query, yields a different result set, and which is correct is decided only outside the material
  • Operational change continues too. Deployments, migrations and schema changes make a field of the same name carry different values, which undermines the premise of laying two rounds side by side
  • Time formatting can also shift between rounds. If one is local time and the other is UTC, the same event appears at two different times. This is why RFC 3227 requires clock drift to be recorded
  • A single acquisition removes the inter-round interval entirely. One acquisition moment means one source state, and whatever changes afterwards, the acquired copy pins that moment

9. Completeness cannot be tested - there is no way to confirm 「this is all of it」

The third loss is the most fundamental. Whether what you received is everything is not answerable from inside what you received. Without the size of the whole set, no test decides whether a subset is complete, and this has nothing to do with anyone's honesty.

No amount of close reading produces the answer. Material can be tidy, consistently formatted and unbroken in its timeline, and all of that is consistency within the given subset, not a basis for completeness. RFC 3227 lists Complete as its own property precisely because it does not follow from the others.

9.2 All three survive a producer acting in good faith

The important property of this argument is that its premises are weak. The conclusion stands without assuming ill intent anywhere.

  • A producer who understands the request precisely and pulls conscientiously still leaves no record of what was not pulled. Good faith improves the selection; it does not remove the selection
  • A producer who runs an identical query every round still carries through whatever the source changed in between. Good faith does not stop time
  • A producer who states that everything was provided does not change the fact that no means of checking that statement sits inside the material. Unverifiability is not dissolved by assertion
  • Which is why all three cut against a good-faith producer too. Anyone who wants their own material to carry weight benefits from a single acquisition, and the more the acquisition is documented, the more support their own account has
  • So 「in one pass」 is not a condition that suspects the other side. It is the condition that makes both sides' statements checkable, which is also why it is easy to justify asking for

10. A hash only means something if it is computed at acquisition

Part four is integrity. Hash values appear more often than anything else in integrity discussions and are misread more often too. Starting from the definition clears most of it up.

A message digest is a hash that uniquely identifies data and has the property that changing a single bit in the data will cause a completely different message digest to be generated. That is NIST SP 800-86's definition. The same document describes integrity verification as follows: using tools to compute the message digest of the original and copied data, then comparing the digests to make sure that they are the same.

Read literally, the definition settles what question a hash answers. It answers 「are these two the same」. It does not answer 「is this the original」. A hash produces information only through comparison of two values, and with nothing to compare against it says nothing at all.

  • So a hash computed later shows only that 「what was received then and what is held now are the same」. It shows nothing about what happened before. The later the hash attaches, the longer the blind interval grows
  • SP 800-86 writes its procedure to reflect exactly that. When a bit stream image is performed, the digest of the original media should be computed and recorded before the image is performed, the digest of the copy should then be computed and compared with it, and the digest of the original should be computed again to verify that the imaging process did not alter the original, with all results documented
  • Three computations, three jobs: the first fixes the starting point, the second confirms the copy is identical, and the third confirms that the act of acquisition did not change the original. Drop any one and a question becomes unanswerable
  • SP 800-86 dates from 2006 and recommends SHA-1 by the standards of that time. After collision attacks against SHA-1 were published, practice moved its default to the SHA-256 family. The structure of the procedure is unchanged; only the algorithm choice was updated
  • As a request it is one line: record the hash value, the time of computation, what it was computed over, and the algorithm. All four together are what let anyone say what the value pinned. How to put that sentence into a request in advance is in section 9 of the rights-axis article

11. Chain of custody - whose hands it passed through

The question a hash cannot answer belongs to chain of custody: the record linking who held the material from acquisition until now, and what was done at each step. NIST SP 800-86 builds 「maintaining a strict chain of custody」 into its definition of forensics.

The same document spells out what the practice consists of, in five parts: keeping a log of every person who had physical custody of the evidence, documenting the actions they performed on the evidence and at what time, storing the evidence in a secure location when it is not being used, making a copy of the evidence and performing examination and analysis using only the copied evidence, and verifying the integrity of the original and copied evidence.

  • All five serve one purpose: turning 「nothing happened in between」 from an assertion into a record. Assertions get contradicted; records get compared
  • The requirement to examine and analyse only the copy matters most. Opening the original can itself change it, after which every subsequent observation is no longer an observation of the original state
  • RFC 3227 points the same way, advising that where feasible you should consider generating checksums and cryptographically signing the collected evidence, since this may make it easier to preserve a strong chain of evidence
  • SP 800-86 also requires a detailed log of every step taken to collect the data, including information about each tool used, and gives the reason: the documentation allows other analysts to repeat the process later if needed. Reproducibility is the goal here too
  • Even the default for uncertain cases is written down. If it is unclear whether evidence needs to be preserved, by default it generally should be preserved, says SP 800-86. Which way to err is settled in advance
  • Material without a chain of custody is not thereby forged material. It is material with no means of showing it was not. The direction is symmetric, which is why the practice is equally useful to whoever produces the material

12. Text logs can be edited without leaving a trace

The shortest demonstration of why integrity procedures exist is this: editing a text file leaves no trace of the edit inside the file. Delete a line and you have a file without that line; change one timestamp and you have a file that always read that way. This is not a defect but a property of the format.

NIST SP 800-86 converts the point into an organisational requirement: because electronic logs and other records can be altered or otherwise manipulated, organizations should be prepared, through their policies, guidelines, and procedures, to demonstrate the integrity of such records. The burden of demonstration sits with whoever holds the records.

The OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet states the same problem as an operational requirement. Under protection it requires 「Store or copy log data to read-only media as soon as possible」 and 「Build in tamper detection so you know if a record has been modified or deleted」. It also requires that all access to logs be recorded and monitored, that read privileges be restricted and periodically reviewed, and, for timestamps, that time be synchronised across all servers and devices.

12.1 How far file metadata actually gets you

「Can we not just look at the modification time」 comes up constantly. File metadata is useful, but the range of what it carries is narrow.

  • Modification, access and creation times are values managed by the filesystem, not part of the file's content. They sit in a separate layer and carry nothing about whether the content is genuine
  • Copy a file or move it to different media and the metadata behaves according to the copy method. The original times may be carried over, or replaced with the time of copying
  • Files forwarded by mail or messenger generally acquire new times in transit. What the recipient sees is not the original's time
  • RFC 3227's warning not to run programs that modify the access time of all files shows how easily this layer moves. The act of checking changes what is being checked
  • The conclusion is complement, not substitute. Metadata reinforces an acquisition-time hash and a procedural record where those exist, and does not stand in for them where they do not

13. Voluntary production and documented process - why the weight differs

Part five is the route of acquisition. The same material supports different statements depending on how it arrived. In United States federal practice the difference shows up in what has to be certified.

Federal Rule of Evidence 902(13) makes self-authenticating 「A record generated by an electronic process or system that produces an accurate result, as shown by a certification of a qualified person that complies with the certification requirements of Rule 902(11) or (12)」, and requires the proponent also to meet the notice requirements of Rule 902(11). Rule 902(14) does the same for 「Data copied from an electronic device, storage medium, or file, if authenticated by a process of digital identification, as shown by a certification of a qualified person」 complying with those same requirements. Both paragraphs took effect on 1 December 2017, and the Advisory Committee note explains that identical hash values for the original and copy reliably attest to the fact that they are exact duplicates. In other words the rule accepts a certificate in place of live testimony, but only where a digital identification process was actually run and can be described.

Other jurisdictions reach the same requirement from a different direction. 14. Selection bias is a problem even without forgery

Picking material out is a completely different act from forging it. Every item picked can be entirely genuine. The problem remains because the criterion for picking shapes the result. Pull from the same source on different criteria and you get pictures that never contradict each other and are nevertheless entirely different.

  • Where the period is cut. Where the start and end fall decides which events are inside and which outside. The items sitting on the boundary decide the most
  • Which key is queried. Results pulled by account identifier, by connection origin and by the record acted upon are three different sets. None is wrong and none is the whole
  • Which logs are in scope. Web server records, application records, database records and authentication records capture the same event at different levels of detail. Pull one type and the other three fall silent
  • Whether normal entries are included. Extract only the problematic entries and their share of the whole becomes invisible. Produce everything and that share declares itself
  • Which format is used. A summary table reads easily and drops fields that existed in the source. Which fields were dropped is not visible from inside the summary
  • The single defence against selection bias is therefore pushing selection past acquisition. Fix the whole first and decide what to look at afterwards; selection still happens, but what is on the record is the selection itself rather than only its output. How to spot whether received material was picked over is covered in section 11 of the rights-axis article

15. Session continuity - when the same session ID brackets a gap

Part six deals with what comes after acquisition has failed. Incomplete material does not mean nothing can be said. There are inferences that follow from the properties of what remains, and where such an inference holds it carries weight close to a direct record.

Session identifiers are that kind of material. Where an interval of records is empty but the session ID on either side of the gap is the same, that reads as no re-authentication in between. The basis is session regeneration. The OWASP Session Management Cheat Sheet states that the session ID must be renewed or regenerated by the web application after any privilege level change within the associated user session, and identifies the authentication process as the most common scenario where regeneration is mandatory, because the privilege level changes from the unauthenticated or anonymous state to the authenticated state.

15.1 Both branches lead to the same conclusion

  • Branch A - the implementation regenerates. A new session ID is issued at each login. Then observing the same session ID either side of the gap means no login happened inside it. The explanation that a different account took over needs the session ID to have changed, and it did not
  • Branch B - the implementation does not regenerate. The same document says regeneration is mandatory to prevent session fixation attacks, where an attacker sets the session ID on the victim's browser instead of gathering the victim's session ID. An implementation that does not regenerate fails that requirement, and that failure is the session fixation weakness
  • What follows from branch B matters. If the session ID persists across authentication, the session records of that system cannot distinguish users in the first place. The same document says that once an authenticated session is established the session ID is temporarily equivalent to the strongest authentication method used by the application, and that equivalence presupposes regeneration
  • So on either branch, the explanation that a different account took over during the gap does not stand: on branch A because no handover occurred, on branch B because those records cannot show a handover at all

15.2 The point is to take the argument form with you

This argument has the same shape as the one section 19 of the rights-axis article runs about IP addresses. There, the answer 「IP addresses are not personal data, so we cannot provide them」 is split into two branches, and neither branch lets the answer support itself. Here the same shape recurs around session identifiers.

  • The form has three moves. Split the other side's statement exhaustively into two branches, push each branch to its end separately, and check whether the two ends agree. If they agree, the conclusion holds regardless of which branch is true
  • Its strength is that it never has to deny the other side's factual claim. Take their premise as given and the conclusion still arrives, so the exchange does not collapse into a dispute about facts
  • It has one precondition: the two branches really must be exhaustive. A third branch destroys the argument on the spot, so check that before splitting
  • For how the same form works on the technical side, see section 10 of the technical-axis article, which splits what an account name in a log does and does not establish according to whether the controls were in place

16. After acquisition has failed, and what operators should have in place

Work remains where a single early acquisition did not happen, but its character changes. It moves from direct proof to consistency checking: whether several sources disagree, and where they do, that point becomes the next question.

  • Gather sources that recorded the same events from different angles. Web server records, application records, authentication records, alert history and monitoring metrics all cover the same window differently. Check whether a gap in one appears in the others
  • Look for records held by third parties. Mailboxes, payment histories and carrier records are managed by someone else and survive independently of one side's circumstances, which makes them reference points for time
  • Fix what is on your own devices first. Browser history, caches, notifications and screen recordings are the only material you control. For those you can record a hash and a timestamp right now
  • Test the timeline for consistency. Where two sources place the same event at different times, first separate a timezone formatting difference from a real one. That is exactly why RFC 3227 requires clock drift to be recorded
  • Establish the extent of the absence. Where an interval is empty, checking whether records for other subjects over the same interval are also empty separates a blanket absence from a selective one. That argument is developed in section 15 of the rights-axis article
  • Handle everything from now on properly from the start. For material received from here, you can record the time of receipt, the route, the hash value and the algorithm as you go. The past interval is closed; everything after this point need not be

16.1 What the operating side should have in place

None of this is useful only to whoever demands material. From the operator's side, having an acquisition procedure ready is the cheapest available defence. Without one, an operator ends up degrading the evidential weight of their own records.

  • Fix retention periods in writing. If policy states which logs are held for how long, that document becomes the basis for explaining an absence. It is the same direction as the OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet requiring both edges of the retention period
  • Copy to read-only media as soon as possible. That is the same document's requirement verbatim. As long as the only copy lives on a running system, less can be said about it
  • Build in tamper detection. Without a means of knowing whether a record was modified or deleted, there is no way to show that it was not. The blockage is symmetric
  • Synchronise time. Timelines only compare if clocks across servers and devices agree. Where a device cannot be synchronised, record its offset instead
  • Write the production procedure in advance. If it is settled what gets produced, in what format and with which accompanying records, responding becomes execution of a procedure rather than an improvised judgement
  • Put hashing into the procedure. Compute the hash at the moment of export and state it in the response with the time, the target and the algorithm, and the response itself becomes the reference point for every later comparison
  • Check whether the logs are in a shape that can prove anything. An identifier in a record points at an actor only if the system-side controls stand behind it. Those conditions are covered in section 10.1 of the technical-axis article

Frequently asked questions

How soon does digital evidence have to be preserved? Can it wait?

The standards answer with a property rather than a deadline. RFC 3227 tells collectors to work from the most volatile source downwards, in the single line 「Proceed from the volatile to the less volatile」. NIST SP 800-86 says collection is typically performed in a timely manner because of the likelihood of losing dynamic data such as current network connections, and adds that non-volatile data may also be somewhat dynamic in nature, giving log files that are overwritten as new events occur as its example. Whether a log still exists is therefore decided by that system's rotation cycle and retention policy, not by the calendar. The OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet states that log data, temporary debug logs and backups, copies and extractions must not be destroyed before the duration of the required data retention period and must not be kept beyond this time, which means disappearance after the period is normal operation rather than a defect. So the only honest answer to 「can it wait」 is that it depends on the system, and that uncertainty is itself the reason to acquire early.

If I have a hash value, does that prove the file is the original?

It does not. NIST SP 800-86 describes integrity verification as computing the message digest of the original and copied data and then comparing the digests to make sure that they are the same. The question a hash answers is 「are these two the same」, not 「is this the original」. A digest computed later fixes only what happens after that moment and says nothing about the interval before it. That is why the same document instructs that, when a bit stream image is performed, the message digest of the original media should be computed and recorded before the image is performed, compared against the digest of the copy afterwards, and then recomputed on the original to verify that the imaging process did not alter it, with all results documented. So what to ask for is not one value but four: the hash, the time of computation, the target, and the algorithm. Federal Rule of Evidence 902(14) rests on the same structure, requiring a process of digital identification shown by a certification of a qualified person, and the Advisory Committee note explains that identical hash values for the original and copy reliably attest that they are exact duplicates.

What actually changes if data arrives in several instalments?

Three things are lost at once. First, control over scope passes to whoever holds the data, because each round requires someone to decide what to pull and on what key, and the requesting side cannot point at what is missing when it does not know what exists. Second, state identity between rounds is not guaranteed: rotation keeps running, deployments keep landing and schemas keep changing between round one and round three, and nothing inside the data records that drift. Third, completeness cannot be tested. Without knowing the size of the whole set, no amount of careful reading tells you whether the subset in hand is all of it. What matters most is that none of the three depends on bad faith. They follow from the shape of the process rather than from anyone's intent, which is why a producer who wants their own material to carry weight is better served by a single acquisition with the scope written down.

How does data handed over voluntarily differ from data obtained through a documented process?

The difference is not only compulsion. It is who fixes the scope, whether that decision leaves a record, and whether an integrity step is attached. Federal Rule of Evidence 902(13) allows a record generated by an electronic process or system that produces an accurate result to be self-authenticating on a certification of a qualified person complying with Rule 902(11) or (12), and Rule 902(14) does the same for copied data authenticated by a process of digital identification. Both took effect on 1 December 2017, and both require the proponent to meet the notice requirements of Rule 902(11). Korea writes the scope question directly into statute: Article 106(3) of its Criminal Procedure Act provides that where the object is a computer disk or similar information storage medium, the stored information shall be printed out or copied within a defined scope, and only where that is impossible or would make the purpose markedly difficult to achieve may the medium itself be seized. Korea's Supreme Court held in case 2007Do7257 that a printout is usable only if identical to what the original held, and that identity requires assurance the original was unchanged from seizure to printout. How any particular matter is assessed rests with the court.

If the session ID before and after a gap in the logs is the same, what does that tell me?

It tells you there was no re-authentication in between. The basis is session regeneration. The OWASP Session Management Cheat Sheet states that the session ID must be renewed or regenerated after any privilege level change within the associated user session, and that the most common scenario where regeneration is mandatory is the authentication process, because the privilege level changes from the unauthenticated or anonymous state to the authenticated state. That leaves exactly two branches. If the implementation does regenerate, a session ID that continues across the gap means no login occurred inside it. If the implementation does not regenerate, it fails the requirement the same document calls mandatory for preventing session fixation, and the session records of that system cannot distinguish users in the first place. Either branch defeats the explanation that a different account took over during the gap. The caveat is that the exact conditions under which a session ID is renewed differ between implementations, so a real assessment has to confirm how that system handles authentication.

Conclusion - four propositions

Instead of a summary, four propositions. The numbers after each point to the sections that support it. The proposition in the introduction is redeemed by these four.

  • Proposition 1. 「Early」 is not a preference but a physical condition. Non-volatile data is overwritten as new events accumulate, and disappearance after the retention period is normal operation. The axis that orders collection is speed of loss rather than importance, which RFC 3227 compresses into a single line about proceeding from the volatile to the less volatile. → Section 4, Section 5, Section 6
  • Proposition 2. 「In one pass」 is not a condition that suspects the other side but the condition that makes both sides' statements checkable. Taking material in instalments costs scope control, state identity between rounds and completeness testing, and all three survive a producer acting in good faith. → Section 7, Section 8, Section 9
  • Proposition 3. A hash answers only whether two sets of data are the same, and means something only when the time of computation is recorded with it. A value computed later shows nothing about the interval before. Whose hands the material passed through is a chain of custody question rather than a hashing one, and text records do not mark their own edits. → Section 10, Section 11, Section 12
  • Proposition 4. If the acquisition itself is not recorded, nothing shows that the material matches the original. So ask an answer citing a standard for the standard number, and an answer asserting integrity for the time of computation. Even after acquisition has failed, inferences that follow from the properties of what remains still hold, and that argument form travels across jurisdictions and matters. → Section 3, Section 13, Section 15

Once more: this article is not legal advice. Everything above is a general account of standards documents, technical guidance, statutory text and judicial holdings verified as of August 2026, not an assessment of or advice on any particular matter. Which material is admitted as evidence, and which procedure is appropriate, turns on the facts, and that determination rests with the competent authority and the courts. Standards and statutes are amended, so check the text in force before acquiring or demanding material, and take a lawyer's help where it matters.
